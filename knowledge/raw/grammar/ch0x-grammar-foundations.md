# Raw extraction — Grammar foundations (parts-of-speech primer)

Source: Meltzer SAT Grammar 6th ed., front-of-book primer (scans p0041–p0046).
Status: raw note, our words only. Claims marked unverified need bank/official verification.

## Concepts taught

- `word-role-vocabulary` — The minimal set of word-role labels a student needs before
  any punctuation/agreement work: verb, noun, pronoun, preposition, conjunction, plus
  adverb and adjective as secondary. The point is functional literacy (can you find the
  verb?), not full grammar taxonomy.
- `verb-as-action-or-state` — A verb expresses an action or a state. Quick
  identification test: if you can put "to" in front of a word and it reads as an
  activity ("to decide"), it can serve as a verb.
- `conjugated-vs-infinitive-verb` — The dictionary form ("to" form) is unconjugated;
  a working sentence needs a conjugated form matched to a doer. This distinction later
  powers the fragment rules (an unconjugated form can't anchor a sentence).
- `verb-number-marking` — Third-person verbs mark number backwards from nouns: the
  -s ending marks a SINGULAR verb, no -s marks plural ("it runs" / "they run"). The
  exam leans on the it/they contrast almost exclusively.
- `irregular-be-have` — The two highest-frequency verbs have conjugations that look
  nothing like their base forms, so students can't pattern-match; their
  singular/plural present and past forms must be automatic (is/are, was/were,
  has/have, had).
- `tense-as-time-signal` — Tense places an action in time. The primer teaches tenses
  by recognizable form (has been = present perfect, had been = past perfect, would
  be = conditional, will have been = future perfect) rather than by definition —
  recognition over production.
- `noun-as-nameable-thing` — Nouns name people, places, things, and ideas.
  Identification test: it can follow "a(n)" or "the". Abstract nouns (idea-nouns)
  often wear suffixes like -tion, -ment, -tude — useful because students misread
  abstract nouns as "not real nouns."
- `pronoun-as-noun-stand-in` — Pronouns substitute for nouns already in play. Includes
  the personal-pronoun person/number grid and the fact that some pronouns
  (this/that/which/both/some/few/many) also stand in as subjects.
- `preposition-as-relation-word` — Prepositions locate things in space/time and are
  always completed by a noun. Matters later because a preposition+noun chunk can never
  contain the sentence's subject or main verb.
- `conjunction-as-connector` — Conjunctions announce a relationship between words,
  phrases, or clauses. Deliberately taught shallow here; the coordinating vs
  subordinating split is deferred to the sentence chapters.
- `conjunctive-adverb-vs-conjunction` — Transition words like "however/therefore/
  moreover" MEAN the same as conjunctions like "but/so" but are grammatically adverbs:
  they can only open a sentence or clause (after boundary punctuation), never glue two
  clauses the way a conjunction can. This meaning-vs-grammar split is the single most
  load-bearing idea in the primer for SAT purposes.
- `adverb-and-adjective-as-modifiers` — Adjectives modify nouns; adverbs modify verbs,
  adjectives, other adverbs, whole clauses. SAT relevance is mostly via conjunctive
  adverbs, not -ly adverbs.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Ruthless triage: eight word roles exist; the book flags which five matter directly
  for the exam, which two occasionally matter, and drops the rest. Vocabulary is
  taught only insofar as later chapters consume it.
- Definitions are OPERATIONAL — every category ships with a hands-on test ("to" test
  for verbs, "a/the" test for nouns) instead of an abstract definition. This suits
  weak grammar students who can't recite terminology but can run a check.
- Recognition before production: conjugation and tense are given as lookup patterns
  the student learns to recognize in answer choices, not paradigms to generate.
- The primer plants seeds it harvests later: conjugation (→ fragments), preposition
  chunks (→ subject-finding), conjunctive adverbs (→ comma splices). Sequencing is
  driven by what the punctuation chapters will need.
- Practice items are written in SAT-passage register (science/arts topics, academic
  tone) so the identification skill transfers to test conditions rather than staying
  a worksheet skill.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

- Role-by-slot, not by word: experts classify a word by what it's doing in this
  sentence (the same surface word can be noun, verb, or modifier). The taught habit is
  to ask "what job is this word doing here?" before applying any rule.
- Verb-first parsing: the expert's entry point into any sentence is locating the
  conjugated verb(s), then asking "who/what does this?" — everything else (fragments,
  agreement, boundaries) hangs off that.
- Substitution checks over terminology: swap in "to ___", "a/the ___", or a bare
  pronoun to type-check a word when unsure.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **verb-s-means-plural** — Students generalize noun morphology (-s = plural) to
  verbs, where it's inverted. Produces subject-verb agreement misses on it/they items.
- **abstract-nouns-arent-nouns** — School examples of nouns are concrete
  (dog, city), so idea-words like "formation" or "development" don't register as
  nouns; students then can't find subjects in academic prose.
- **transition-equals-conjunction** — Because "however" means "but," students assume
  it behaves like "but." Meaning-similarity masks a grammatical difference; this is
  the root cause of a whole comma-splice family later.
- **sentence-must-name-its-subject-meaningfully** — Pronoun subjects with unknown
  referents feel "incomplete," priming the fragment misjudgments of the next chapter.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim)

- Verb questions concentrate on third-person singular vs plural, especially it vs
  they, and on be/have because their irregular forms hide the number cue.
- Conjunctive adverbs appear on the exam mainly as boundary-punctuation bait, not as
  vocabulary.

## Prerequisite edges implied

- `word-role-vocabulary` → `clause-recognition` (ch3) → all boundary punctuation (ch4+)
- `conjugated-vs-infinitive-verb` → fragment identification
- `preposition-as-relation-word` → subject-isolation → subject-verb agreement
- `conjunctive-adverb-vs-conjunction` → comma-splice diagnosis and transition
  questions

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: operational tests per category; strict exam-relevance triage; primes exactly
  the distinctions later chapters consume; exercise items in authentic test register.
- Poorly: pure prose + tables, no visual or interactive scaffolding; tense treatment
  is a bare form-name table that a struggling student will skim without retention;
  never explicitly states the "find the verb first" routine it implicitly relies on —
  our graph node should make that routine explicit.
