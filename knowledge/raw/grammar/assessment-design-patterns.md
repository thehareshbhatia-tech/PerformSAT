# Raw notes — Grammar source A: assessment design in mixed practice sets + answer key

Source: Meltzer grammar guide, 6th ed. — five all-chapter mixed sets (10 items each)
plus the full answer key. Focus of this note: HOW a master author composes mixed
practice and explains answers. No items are recorded; only archetypes, frequencies,
and design principles, in our words.

## Mixed-set composition (observed distribution across ~50 items)

Each 10-item set is a deliberate miniature of a Writing module, roughly:

- **Transitions: ~35%** (3-4 per set, usually opening the set as items 1-3)
- **Boundaries/punctuation: ~30%** (sentence separation/joining, comma-with-name,
  non-essential clause with dashes, list punctuation, punctuating the transition word
  itself)
- **Verb items (agreement/form): ~10%** (1 per set)
- **Pronoun items (number/logical referent): ~12%** (1-2 per set)
- **Modifier placement (dangling): ~5%** (appears in about half the sets, always as a
  harder composite item)
- **Rhetorical synthesis ("student notes" bullet-to-goal): ~18%** (2 per set, ALWAYS
  the closing items 9-10)

Design principles worth stealing:
- Fixed skeleton, shuffled middle: transitions cluster early, notes questions close the
  set — mirroring official module ordering — while conventions items interleave in
  between so consecutive items never test the same rule.
- One-of-each coverage beats depth in mixed review: a set never has two verb items or
  two pronoun items; the redundancy budget is spent entirely on transitions and
  boundaries, matching their real exam weight.
- Composite items are the difficulty ceiling: the hardest item in a set tests two rules
  at once (modifier placement + clause separation; agreement + comma-between-subject-
  and-verb; list punctuation + parallelism). Single-rule items are the floor.

## How difficulty is signaled (never labeled)

There are no difficulty tags. Difficulty is engineered through:
1. **Distractor tightness** — easy items have one plausible answer and three
   category-mismatches; hard items have two grammatically clean choices where the
   discriminator is logical (which noun a modifier touches, which idea a transition
   actually connects).
2. **Concept disguise** — a question is dressed as a different concept than it tests:
   answer choices vary tense but the real split is singular/plural (agreement posing
   as tense); a plural decoy noun sits right before a verb whose true subject is a
   distant singular (proximity trap).
3. **Blank position** — blanks placed mid-clause force re-reading from the sentence
   start; blanks at natural pause points are gentler.
4. **Prose density** — harder items embed the tested structure in longer, more
   technical sentences; the grammar itself is not harder, the parsing is.

## Recurring distractor archetypes (cross-topic catalog)

1. **Wrong-category transition** — a transition from the wrong logical family
   (contrast where an example-introducer is needed, etc.). Diagnostic: student read
   the sentences but misjudged their relationship, or never built the
   relationship-first habit.
2. **Same-category twins** — two choices from the SAME logical family (two
   contradictors). Both must be wrong; the unique-category choice wins "by default."
   Students who pick either twin are matching tone/register, not logic.
3. **Comma splice / fused pair** — same words, four punctuation variants; one variant
   joins two independent clauses with only a comma, another with nothing. Diagnostic:
   picking the splice = cannot yet identify a complete clause on each side.
4. **Illegal-mark neighbor** — the near-correct choice uses a legal boundary in an
   illegal spot: comma directly before "that," comma between subject and verb, comma
   before a preposition, colon after a fragment. Diagnostic: student knows a boundary
   is needed but places marks by breath/rhythm.
5. **Half-right punctuation pair** — dash opened but closed with a comma, or a list
   mixing commas and semicolons. Tests mark-consistency. Diagnostic: student
   evaluates the blank locally instead of scanning for the partner mark.
6. **Number flip with tense noise** — verb choices differ in BOTH number and tense;
   tense variation is pure distraction. Diagnostic: picking a wrong-number verb with
   confident tense reasoning = solved the wrong problem.
7. **Proximity-referent pronoun** — pronoun agrees with the nearest noun instead of
   the logical referent, or choices offer its/their/they're + a possessive homophone.
   Diagnostic: local matching instead of asking "who actually does/owns this?"
8. **Clean-but-dangling** — grammatically flawless, concise choices that attach a
   descriptive opener to the wrong subject. Explicitly the most dangerous archetype:
   the author warns that clarity and correctness of the choice IN ISOLATION is the
   bait. Diagnostic: student evaluates choices without re-attaching them to the
   sentence frame.
9. **On-topic wrong-goal (notes items)** — factually true bullets, fluently combined,
   that answer a different goal than the stated one (mentions the event but not its
   speed; gives scope but not purpose; background instead of contrast). Diagnostic:
   student matched topic keywords instead of the goal phrase's demand.
10. **Partial-goal match (notes items)** — covers one half of a two-part goal
    (says "declined" but not "rapidly"; names the person but omits the required
    unfamiliar-audience background). The strongest notes distractor.

## How strong explanations are structured (the answer-key pattern)

Header format: item number + correct letter + concept label ("Transition,"
"Pronoun Agreement," "Dangling Modifier, Comma Splice"). The label is FIRST — the
reader always learns what was tested before why.

Then, consistently, the order is: **evidence → rule/category → verdict → distractor
autopsy**:
1. State the relationship or structure found in the passage (the evidence: "the two
   sentences oppose each other," "the subject is a gerund").
2. Name the requirement class it creates ("a contradictor is required," "gerunds are
   always singular").
3. Identify the only choice satisfying it — often "the only option in that category,"
   making correctness feel forced rather than preferred.
4. Dispatch remaining choices by category, with a "be careful with (X)" callout when
   one distractor is genuinely magnetic, explaining WHY it tempts and where it breaks.

Recurring procedural moves the explanations teach implicitly:
- "Back up and read from the beginning of the sentence" — never judge a blank locally.
- Plug-in-and-reread as final verification; for paired dashes/commas, delete the
  enclosed span and check the sentence still stands.
- Explicit shortcuts, flagged as such: a period inside a choice hints the item is a
  two-sentence split; on unfamiliar-audience notes items the right answer is usually
  the longest because it must carry background; goal-keyword scanning ("only one
  choice contains a contrast word — start there").
- Answer-choice forensics as diagnosis: read the CHOICES first to learn what's tested
  (mixed singular/plural verbs → agreement; identical words with shuffled marks →
  boundaries; list → scan punctuation consistency).

## Diagnostic signal carried by each distractor family (for our tutor)

- wrong-category transition → relationship-identification gap (reread the two ideas,
  classify agree/oppose/cause/example before looking at choices)
- same-category twin picked → student shops by word feel; teach category-first
  elimination
- splice/fused picked → clause-completeness gap; remediate at sentence-recognition
- illegal-mark neighbor picked → punctuation-by-pause habit; remediate with the
  structural role of each mark
- number-flip picked → subject-finding gap (prepositional-phrase blindness)
- proximity pronoun picked → referent-logic gap, not pronoun knowledge
- clean-but-dangling picked → strong grammar, missing whole-sentence integration —
  a HIGH-scorer error worth distinct messaging
- on-topic wrong-goal picked → reads bullets, ignores goal phrase; teach
  goal-phrase-first protocol
- partial-goal picked → reads goal once, matches first keyword; teach two-part goal
  decomposition

## What the key does for chapter drills vs mixed sets (deliberate asymmetry)

Chapter-drill keys are terse: bare letters, or corrected sentences with the offending
word struck out, or the verb + its true subject in parentheses; fill-in items list
"also acceptable" punctuation alternates. Mixed-set keys get full prose explanations.
The logic: inside a chapter the context already explains the why; in mixed review the
student doesn't know which rule fired, so the explanation must carry identification +
reasoning. Our product analog: drill feedback can be compact when the drill is
single-skill; mixed/adaptive sessions need type-labeled, full-reasoning feedback.

## SAT patterns claimed (verify against our bank)

- Transitions + boundaries together ≈ two-thirds of mixed conventions practice —
  matches their claimed exam weight; cross-check against official digital module
  composition before hard-coding our blueprints.
- Notes questions consistently terminal in a set; official modules do place synthesis
  at the end — consistent with our PT4-11 flow memory, but verify per-module counts.
