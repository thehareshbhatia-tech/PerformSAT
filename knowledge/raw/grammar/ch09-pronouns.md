Maps to: Standard English Conventions → Form, Structure, and Sense (pronoun-antecedent

## Concepts taught (candidate node ids)

- `pronoun-antecedent-number` — a pronoun inherits number from the noun it stands
  for; choosing singular vs plural pronoun requires locating that noun first, not
  guessing from vibe.
- `antecedent-must-be-on-the-page` — a pronoun may only point at a noun that
  literally appears in the text. A noun that is merely implied by the logic of the
  passage (e.g., the plural of a stated singular) does not count.
- `antecedent-search-direction` — the referent usually sits earlier in the same
  sentence or in the previous sentence; rarely, it follows. Skilled readers back up
  one sentence when the pronoun opens a sentence.
- `person-vs-thing-pronouns` — some pronouns are people-only, some thing-only, some
  dual-use; a mismatch (thing-pronoun for a person or vice versa) is an error class
  of its own.
- `generic-person-consistency` — the generalized-person pronouns (the formal "one,"
  the collective "we," the direct-address "you") are each internally fine but cannot
  be swapped mid-passage; the correct choice is whichever the surrounding sentences
  already use. This is a cross-sentence consistency check, not a single-sentence
  grammar check.
- `each-every-singular` — quantifiers meaning "every individual one" are singular
  and take singular pronouns, even when followed by an of-the-plural phrase.
- `noun-over-pronoun` — when the choices offer both a pronoun and a specific noun,
  and the pronoun's referent is missing or ambiguous, the specific noun wins. The
  source states this as near-absolute for the SAT.
- `bare-demonstrative-vagueness` — a demonstrative standing alone ("this/that/these/
  those" with no noun after it) tends to be too vague; demonstrative + noun is the
  preferred repair. "More specific is better" is the tiebreak.
- `self-pronoun-agreement` — intensive "-self/-selves" forms must match the noun
  they spotlight in number and person/thing type.
- `noun-number-agreement` — role/category nouns after linking constructions must
  match the subject's number (two founders become two of a role, not one), and
  interrupting phrases like an accompaniment phrase ("along with...") do NOT
  pluralize a singular subject.
- `relative-pronoun-selection` — who/whom for people, which for things, whose and
  that for either; whose is possessive of both who and which despite looking
  people-only.
- `who-vs-whom-position` — practical case rule: the subject form sits before a verb;
  the object form follows a preposition. A verb immediately after the object form is
  the error signature. Exception understood: in "quantity + of + object-form +
  verb" constructions, the preposition (not the later verb) governs the case.
- `where-when-vs-prep-which` — the place adverb is for physical locations only, the
  time adverb for times/events only; "preposition + which" is a formally correct
  substitute for either, and a trailing duplicate preposition at the clause end is
  redundant once "prep + which" is used.
- `whereby-flag` — a formal connective meaning roughly "through which," used for
  systems/processes; sounds stilted so students wrongly eliminate it.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Teaches agreement as a three-question checklist executed in order: (1) what noun
  does this pronoun logically stand for? (2) is that noun singular or plural? (3) is
  the candidate pronoun singular or plural? Mismatch = eliminate.
- Deliberately front-loads thing-pronouns before people-pronouns, arguing errors
  about things are harder to hear because thing-number is less salient than
  person-count. People questions are framed as the easy case.
- Repeatedly widens the student's frame: single-sentence reading is insufficient for
  generic-person consistency and antecedent location; the method forces re-reading
  the prior sentence.
- Position on informal singular "they": acknowledges everyday usage but advises
  treating the gender-split singular phrase ("he or she") as the tested standard
  until official materials show otherwise. Flagged for verification against current
  digital-SAT released items — this may be dated advice.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. On seeing answer choices that mix singular and plural pronouns, stop and hunt the
   antecedent before evaluating anything else; grammatical plausibility of each
   choice in isolation is a trap.
2. Verify number morphologically (is there a plural marker on the noun?) rather than
   semantically (does it "feel" plural?).
3. Strip interrupting phrases (non-essential clauses, accompaniment phrases) to
   expose the true subject before judging number.
4. When two same-gender people are named, any singular personal pronoun is
   automatically ambiguous — scan for a choice that names one of them.
5. For who/whom, look at the immediately following word (verb → subject form) and
   the immediately preceding word (preposition → object form); don't translate into
   "m = him" mnemonics mid-test.
6. Choose the boring-but-explicit option: named noun over pronoun, demonstrative +
   noun over bare demonstrative.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **implied-antecedent-ok** — students accept a plural pronoun because the plural
  concept is obviously intended, even though only a singular noun appears. Forms
  because conversational English resolves reference by meaning, not by literal text;
  the test's stricter written standard feels pedantic. Trap: a grammatically fine
  plural pronoun choice next to the correct singular one.
- **they-for-generic-person** — students pick the plural possessive for
  each/every/a-person subjects because spoken English uses singular "they"
  universally. Schooling reinforces it as inclusive. The tested standard (per this
  source) still expects singular forms here. [unverified-claim — check against
  recent official items]
- **register-blindness** — students judge the generic-person pronoun sentence-locally
  ("your eyes" sounds fine) without checking which generic pronoun the passage
  already committed to. Forms because school grammar rarely tests cross-sentence
  consistency.
- **which-for-people** — feels acceptable because "that" is used for people
  colloquially, blurring the person/thing line for all relative pronouns.
- **whom-by-formality** — students choose the object form because it "sounds
  smarter," not by structure; the test exploits this by placing it right before a
  verb.
- **where-for-abstractions** — using the place adverb for times, books, or
  situations; feels right because spatial metaphor is pervasive in speech.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim, verify vs our bank)

- Thing-agreement (it/its vs they/their) historically outnumbers person-agreement
  items.
- Answer sets deliberately include both singular and plural pronouns that are all
  grammatical out of context.
- Noun-vs-pronoun choice sets: the specific-noun option is virtually always correct
  when present alongside pronouns.
- Referent may occasionally appear in the sentence AFTER the pronoun.
- "That" referring to people has never been directly tested (controversial usage the
  test avoids).
- Where/when vs "prep + which": when both appear in different choices and both would
  be acceptable, some second factor decides — the test doesn't make you pick between
  two right answers.

## Combination + trap evidence from exercises and Cumulative Review II

- Agreement combos observed: pronoun-number + demonstrative-vagueness in one choice
  set; agreement + apostrophe simultaneously (decide number first, then punctuation
  form); "each" + possessive-pronoun number in one item.
- Cumulative review recurs: demonstrative + noun beating bare demonstrative;
  plural pronoun for a plural human group; noun-number agreement (plural subject →
  plural role noun) alongside comma decisions.
- Distractor recipe: one choice with correct number but bare/vague reference, one
  with specific reference but wrong number, one fully correct — students who check
  only one axis get caught.

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the "the referent must physically appear" rule is crisp and diagnostic; the
  choices-mixing-numbers trigger ("stop and find the noun") is an excellent tutor
  cue; who/whom reduced to two positional checks is fast and testable.
- Well: explicitly separates thing-agreement (hard, common) from person-agreement
  (easy) so practice can weight the hard case.
- Poorly: singular-"they" guidance may be stale for the current digital test; needs
  verification before our tutor asserts it.
- Poorly: ambiguity coverage is example-driven with little taxonomy of WHEN the test
  considers a pronoun ambiguous vs acceptable; our graph node should formalize
  (two same-type candidate nouns = ambiguous; unique candidate = fine).
- Poorly: relative-pronoun section is a list of micro-rules without a unifying
  mental model; we should teach it as "match the pronoun to (a) person/thing axis
  and (b) grammatical slot axis."
