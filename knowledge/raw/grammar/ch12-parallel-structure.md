# Raw extraction — Grammar source (Meltzer 6th ed), Ch. 12: Parallel Structure

Source studied: chapter on parallelism (lists, two-item conjunctions), plus the
Cumulative Review covering chapters 8-12. Maps to: Standard English Conventions →
Form, Structure, and Sense (parallel form). All notes are our own synthesis; no
source wording or examples retained.

## Concepts taught (candidate node ids)

- `parallelism-equal-form` — coordinated ideas of equal weight should share one
  grammatical shape (all nouns, all gerunds, all finite verbs, all clauses).
  Framed as both a correctness rule and a readability principle.
- `list-parallelism` — in a three-plus-item series, every item must open in the
  same form; the fastest check is comparing the FIRST word(s) of each item rather
  than parsing items whole.
- `conjunction-pair-parallelism` — two items joined by a coordinating conjunction
  must match; because one item usually sits OUTSIDE the blank in the fixed text,
  the fixed item dictates the answer's form. The whole sentence must be read —
  choice-only solving fails structurally here.
- `optional-function-word-repetition` — in matched infinitive/prepositional pairs,
  repeating the shared function word is optional; both the repeated and elided
  versions count as parallel. Prevents over-strict eliminations.
- `phrase-level-parallelism` — matching applies at phrase granularity too: a noun
  phrase pairs with a noun phrase; a choice smuggling in a finite verb becomes a
  clause and breaks the match even if it opens with a noun.
- `list-punctuation-consistency` — a series uses commas throughout or semicolons
  throughout, never a mix; parallelism items can be decided purely on separator
  consistency (bridge to the punctuation chapters).
- `first-words-scan` — the shared strategy with modification: solve by scanning
  item-opening words across choices instead of reading four choices fully.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Presents exactly two testable shapes (series of three; conjunction pair) so
  students can classify instantly, then reuses one mechanical check (compare item
  openers) for both.
- Sequences list-form before list-punctuation, then shows the same stem retested
  with punctuation permutations — teaching that one surface (a series) hosts two
  different question types, and diagnosis precedes rule application.
- Emphasizes reading beyond the blank as the chapter's core discipline: the
  matching partner is deliberately placed in fixed text.
- Reinforces prior chapters by cross-referencing the comma/semicolon series rule
  instead of restating it — the book trains cumulative recall.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. Spot coordination first: a series marker or a coordinating conjunction near the
   blank flags parallelism before anything else.
2. Find the anchor item(s) in the fixed text; classify their form by first word
   (noun? gerund? finite verb? full clause?).
3. Filter choices to those opening in the anchor form; usually one survives.
4. If multiple survive, check the next axis: does a choice add a verb and become a
   clause? does it flip voice? does it mix separators?
5. For separator questions, scan only the punctuation marks between items across
   choices; grammatical content is secondary.
6. Passive-voice rewrites and clause-ified items are the standard distractor
   builds — treat unexpected "was/were ... by" shapes as suspicious.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **meaning-over-form** — students accept any item that conveys the right idea,
  since all four choices typically paraphrase the same fact. Forms because school
  writing feedback rewards content over structure; the test isolates structure.
  Signature: choosing a clause where a noun phrase is required.
- **parallel-means-identical-words** — the inverse error: eliminating a correct
  choice because it doesn't repeat a function word the anchor used. Forms from
  overliteral application of "make them match"; the elision rule is unknown.
- **blank-local-solving** — evaluating choices without locating the anchor item in
  fixed text; students assume everything needed is underlined. Forms because many
  question types are indeed blank-local (modification, apostrophes); parallelism
  is the counterexample.
- **separator-blindness** — treating comma vs semicolon inside a series as a style
  choice, so mixed-separator options look fine. Forms because students read past
  punctuation when comprehending; they never re-scan the marks themselves.
- **longest-fanciest-wins** — under uncertainty students pick the most elaborate
  choice; parallelism answers are often the plainest, shortest form-match.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim, verify vs our bank)

- Two dominant formats: three-item series and two-item conjunction pairs.
- The matching partner of a pair usually sits outside the blank, forcing
  full-sentence reading.
- Series items may re-test the same stem for form or for separator consistency —
  distinct skills on one surface.
- Correlative constructions (the both/and family) follow the same match-what-
  follows-each-marker logic.
- Distractors are built by: converting an item to a clause (adding a subject or
  finite verb), switching to passive voice, swapping gerund/infinitive/noun forms,
  or mixing separators.

## Combination + trap evidence from exercises and Cumulative Review II

- Exercise recipe (series): four choices permuting {consistent commas + one
  non-parallel item, consistent semicolons + parallel items, mixed separators,
  parallel items but one clause-ified}. Only one choice is clean on BOTH axes —
  students must check form and punctuation together.
- Exercise recipe (pair): the anchor is an "-ing + noun" or noun-phrase pattern
  earlier in the series/sentence; distractors offer relative-clause and infinitive
  paraphrases of the same content.
- Cumulative Review II mixes (single set, chapters 8-12): a semicolon-separated
  three-verb series testing tense consistency inside parallelism (all simple past);
  coordinated role nouns testing number agreement inside a parallel pair (plural
  subject → both role nouns plural); parallelism items interleaved with tense,
  subject-verb agreement (interrupter between subject and verb), pronoun-vs-noun
  specificity, apostrophe stacks, and dangling-modifier voice items.
- The review's meta-lesson: the hardest skill is CLASSIFYING which convention a
  choice set tests (choices differing only in verb form → tense or agreement;
  differing in openers → modifier or parallelism; differing in apostrophes →
  possession grid). Our tutor should teach choice-set diagnosis as a first-class
  skill.
