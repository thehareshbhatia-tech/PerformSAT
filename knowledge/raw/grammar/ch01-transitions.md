# Raw notes — Transitions (source: Meltzer grammar guide, ch. 1)

Maps to: Expression of Ideas → Transitions. Provenance only; all wording is ours.

## Concepts taught

- `transition-function-taxonomy` — Every connective belongs to one of three relationship families: (1) **continuation** (the next statement extends the same idea), (2) **cause-effect** (one statement is the result of the other), (3) **contrast** (the statements pull in different directions). Continuation subdivides into sub-jobs: adding information, giving an example, defining/restating, emphasizing, comparing (likeness), and sequencing in time.
- `relationship-before-choices` — The core method: decide what relationship the two statements actually have BEFORE reading any answer choice, then hunt for the option whose family (and sub-job) matches. The prediction is the answer key; choices are only checked against it.
- `transition-anchors-backward` — A transition ties its host sentence to the PREVIOUS sentence regardless of where it physically sits: sentence-initial (followed by comma), mid-sentence (between two commas), or sentence-final (after a comma). All three placements carry identical meaning; placement is purely stylistic. The high-value corollary: a transition sitting between commas mid-sentence is NOT about that sentence's two halves.
- `single-comma-intra-sentence` — Exception case: a transition following exactly ONE comma may genuinely link two halves of the same sentence; then the two clauses of that sentence are the statements to compare.
- `delete-and-compare` — Diagnostic move: mentally strike the transition, state the two bare statements separately, and name their relationship from content alone. If the two fragments around a mid-sentence transition make no standalone sense, that proves the transition points at the previous sentence instead.
- `one-idea-per-statement-summary` — Before naming the relationship, compress each statement to a one-clause gist ("life can't survive here" / "life thrives there"). The relationship is read off the gists, never off surface wording.
- `family-level-elimination` — If the precise relationship is elusive, identify at least the family and strike whole groups of choices at once (all continuers, or all contradictors + cause-effect words).
- `duplicate-choice-elimination` — If two choices are exact functional synonyms in identical grammatical roles, both must be wrong (a question can't have two right answers). Noted as rarely applicable on the digital exam.
- `causality-direction-check` — Within the cause-effect family, words differ in which side they mark as the cause: some announce "here comes the result," others announce "here comes the reason." Picking a cause-effect word without checking direction produces a logically reversed sentence.
- `concession-vs-plain-contrast` — Some contrast words carry an extra "despite that" concession meaning; others mark mere difference between two subjects/situations. Distinguishing within-family shades like this is claimed to be almost never required (see patterns below).
- `topic-jargon-is-noise` — Scientific/historical content in the passage requires zero domain knowledge; experts read past terminology and extract only the logical skeleton.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Teach the taxonomy first with one clean minimal example per family, then the method, then a large reference list, then graded exercises that make the classification step explicit (the drills literally require naming the family before choosing a word — the intermediate reasoning step is forced into the open).
- Worked examples are staged: first shown with choices hidden, to physically prevent choice-first processing and force prediction.
- Escalation order across examples: sentence-initial blank → mid-sentence between commas → sentence-final → single-comma intra-sentence → subordinating-conjunction variants. Placement complexity, not vocabulary difficulty, is the ladder.
- Rare edge cases are covered but explicitly de-weighted ("addressed to be safe"), with a provenance note on how rarely they've appeared — models calibrated teaching.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. Spot that the blank is a connective → this is a logic question, not grammar; all four choices will usually be grammatically fine.
2. Locate the two statements being connected: default is host sentence + previous sentence; only a single-comma mid-sentence transition switches scope to the sentence's own halves.
3. Compress each statement to a gist.
4. Name the relationship (family, then sub-job if possible), producing a prediction like "need a result word" or "need an example-introducer."
5. Scan choices for the one matching the prediction; sanity-check direction for cause-effect picks.
6. If prediction fails, fall back to family-level elimination.
Experts explicitly do NOT plug each choice in and listen — plugging invites rationalizing near-miss options.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why)

- **plug-and-listen** — trying each choice in the blank and judging by feel. Formed by fill-in-the-blank habits from school vocabulary work. Produces "twisting" — inventing a reading under which a wrong option sounds plausible — and falls for near-family distractors (emphasis word where a result is needed).
- **proximity-scope-error** — believing a mid- or end-of-sentence transition connects the words physically adjacent to it (its own sentence's halves, or the following sentence). Formed from the visual intuition that connectors glue what they touch. Produces confident wrong answers where the local reading is plausible but the previous-sentence relationship is contradicted.
- **within-family-deafness** — treating all continuers as interchangeable ("adding" vs "giving an example" vs "emphasizing"). Formed because school never separates these jobs. Produces errors on questions where all four choices are continuers of different sub-jobs.
- **cause-direction-blindness** — believing any causal word fits once causality is detected, ignoring which statement causes which. Produces reversed-logic answers.
- **jargon-freeze** — losing the logical thread when the passage is about unfamiliar science/history; students think content comprehension is being tested.

## Trap taxonomy from example design (design only, no items)

- Three-of-one-family + one-of-another: solvable purely by family recognition; the three same-family options exist to punish students who never classify.
- All-four-same-family with different sub-jobs: punishes family-level-only classification; requires sub-job precision (e.g., an example-introducer vs an adder vs an emphasizer).
- Mid-sentence placement trap: the local (host-sentence-only) reading supports a wrong choice; the previous-sentence reading forces the right one.
- Reversed-causality distractor: a "reason" word offered where a "result" word is needed, or vice versa.
- Concession-nuance item (rare): two contrast words, only one of which correctly implies "despite the preceding fact."
- Subordinating-conjunction variant: the same taxonomy tested with clause-level connectives (although/because/unless/when-type sets) instead of sentence adverbs — same logic, different grammar packaging.
- Temporal-word ambiguity: "at the same time" style words often function as contrast markers, not time markers; sequence words require actual temporal order between events.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim)

- Transition items are the single most common writing type: ~8–10 per test, ~15–20% of the R&W section.
- Choices are normally four grammatically valid options; the test is purely logical fit.
- Fine same-family discrimination has appeared exactly once, and only on a non-administered released form — i.e., family + sub-job classification suffices in practice.
- Contradictors move freely to beginning/middle/end of sentence; continuers mostly to beginning/middle. (Positional asymmetry — worth checking against our bank.)

## Prerequisite edges implied

- gist-compression of a sentence (reading skill) → relationship naming
- independent-clause recognition → distinguishing the single-comma intra-sentence case; also feeds conjunctive-adverb punctuation (boundaries chapter)
- transition-function-taxonomy → rhetorical-synthesis answer signatures (comparative markers, ch02) and punctuation-of-connectives rules

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the three-family model is minimal and complete; backward-anchoring is a genuinely non-obvious invariant taught with a clean delete-and-compare proof; choices-hidden staging forces the right habit; exercise format externalizes the classification step (great template for our drill UI — ask family first, word second).
- Poorly: the reference list of connectives is memorization-shaped with light organization (temporal words split oddly across families); no discussion of paragraph-level transitions spanning more than two sentences; no diagnostic mapping (which wrong choice implies which misconception) — we should build that from the trap taxonomy above.
