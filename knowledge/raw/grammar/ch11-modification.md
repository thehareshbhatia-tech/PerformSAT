# Raw extraction — Grammar source (Meltzer 6th ed), Ch. 11: Modification

Source studied: chapter on modifier placement (dangling and misplaced modifiers).
Maps to: Standard English Conventions → Form, Structure, and Sense (subject-modifier
placement). All notes are our own synthesis; no source wording or examples retained.

## Concepts taught (candidate node ids)

- `modifier-adjacency-principle` — descriptive words/phrases must sit next to what
  they describe; distance between describer and described creates ambiguity or
  accidental comedy. This is the single principle behind both error types.
- `dangling-modifier` — an introductory phrase before a comma describes the
  sentence's subject but doesn't name it; the sentence is correct only if the
  described noun appears IMMEDIATELY after the comma. Intro phrases often (not
  always) open with a participle, but the -ing marker is a hint, not the defining
  feature.
- `misplaced-modifier` — same adjacency failure occurring anywhere in the sentence,
  typically producing a literal reading that is absurd (a description attaching to
  the nearest noun instead of the intended one).
- `sentence-local-scope` — unlike pronoun questions, modifier questions are decided
  entirely inside the sentence containing the blank; surrounding passage context is
  unnecessary. (Contrast edge with ch9's cross-sentence checks.)
- `possessive-subject-trap` — a possessive form of the right person placed after the
  intro phrase still dangles: the noun after the comma is then the owned thing, not
  the person. Near-absolute test heuristic claimed: possessive noun immediately
  after an introductory phrase = wrong.
- `complete-subject-allowance` — an extra descriptor stacked before the true subject
  (a second appositive-style label) does NOT dangle, because it's part of the full
  subject phrase. Prevents over-application of the "name must be the very next
  word" rule.
- `boundary-word-checking` — the efficient solve: identify what the modifier must
  describe, then check only the words at the relevant boundary of each choice
  (first words when the blank follows the intro phrase; last words when the blank
  precedes a fixed modifier). No need to fully parse four long choices.
- `which-attaches-backward` — a which-clause describes the noun directly before it;
  moving the clause or the noun changes the claimed cause/description.
- `parenthetical-adjacency` — parentheses obey the same adjacency law as commas:
  the opening of a parenthetical must relate to the noun immediately before the
  open-paren, and symmetric versions test the sentence end instead of the start.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- One principle, two manifestations: teaches adjacency once, then shows the
  intro-phrase special case and the anywhere case as instances. Good compression.
- Repeats a question-form drill: ask aloud "who/what is doing or being the intro
  phrase?" and demand the answer be the next noun. Converts grammar into a
  who-question students can always generate.
- Explicitly teaches answer-scanning economy (check only the choice-boundary
  words), framing modifier items as among the fastest on the test IF you know
  where to look.
- Warns against choice-only solving: the trap is evaluating four grammatical
  choices in isolation; the fixed text around the blank determines everything.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. On seeing an introductory phrase + comma + blank: extract the describer, ask
   who/what it describes, then scan ONLY the first word(s) of each choice for that
   noun. Eliminate all choices not opening with it.
2. On seeing a blank + comma + fixed descriptive tail: reverse the scan — the
   choice must END with the noun the tail describes.
3. On a parenthetical: look at the noun touching the open-paren (or the content
   opening the parenthetical) and enforce the same match.
4. Treat grammatical correctness of a choice as zero evidence; adjacency to the
   fixed text is the only discriminator.
5. When a sentence reads absurdly if taken literally, trust the literal reading —
   that absurdity IS the error, not a style quibble.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **meaning-rescues-syntax** — students accept a dangling intro phrase because the
  intended meaning is obvious. Forms because listeners routinely repair speech
  errors; the test grades the literal sentence, not the repaired one. Signature:
  choosing a fluent-sounding option whose opening noun is a thing the intro phrase
  can't describe.
- **possessive-counts-as-the-person** — students accept "person's achievement"
  after an intro phrase describing the person, because the person's name is
  visually present. Forms from name-spotting rather than head-noun analysis: the
  head noun of that phrase is the achievement, not the person. This is the
  chapter's sharpest trap.
- **ing-word-required** — students only check for dangling when they see a
  participle opener, missing prepositional or appositive openers. Forms because
  textbooks drill the participle case predominantly.
- **choices-first-solving** — students compare the four choices to each other
  (style, length, tense) instead of to the fixed stem. Forms because many other
  question types ARE solvable from choices alone; modifier items punish the habit.
- **which-floats-free** — students let a which-clause attach to any earlier noun
  that makes sense. Forms from conversational relative clauses, which attach
  loosely by topic rather than adjacency.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim, verify vs our bank)

- Modifier items are sentence-local: surrounding passage never needed.
- The blank may be (a) the full main clause after an intro phrase, (b) the intro
  phrase itself, (c) a parenthetical, or (d) the clause before a fixed
  parenthetical — all four rotations of the same adjacency test.
- All four choices are usually grammatical in isolation; only boundary adjacency
  discriminates.
- The possessive-after-intro-phrase distractor is a standard plant.
- Absurd-literal-reading sentences signal the misplaced-modifier flavor.

## Prerequisite edges implied

- Requires: identifying the head noun of a noun phrase (vs an embedded possessive)

- Requires: recognizing introductory phrases and comma boundaries — links to
  earlier punctuation/boundaries chapters.
- Requires: subject identification (shared with agreement chapters).
- Feeds: parallel structure (both are shape-of-sentence checks solved by scanning

  with tense or agreement in the same choice set (seen in Cumulative Review II).

## Combination + trap evidence from exercises and Cumulative Review II

- Exercise recipe: fixed intro phrase + four choices whose openers rotate through
  {correct subject, pronoun for the subject with passive rearrangement, the
  subject's possession, an unrelated noun made subject by passive voice}. Passive
  rewrites are the standard vehicle for smuggling the wrong opener in.
- Also tested in reverse: fixed tail after the blank (a time-appositive or
  which-clause) requiring the choice to END with the matching noun class (a date/
  event noun before a time descriptor; a place noun before a place descriptor).
- Cumulative Review II combos: intro-phrase adjacency + verb-voice choice in one
  set (the correct choice both names the doer and keeps active voice); modifier
  items sit alongside tense and agreement items so students must diagnose WHICH
  convention a set is testing before applying any rule — diagnosis-first behavior
  our tutor should train.
- Relative-clause stack observed: choices differing in whether a spore/organism
  noun keeps its restrictive clause parallel and adjacent ("that are X and Y" as
  one attached unit) — adjacency plus parallel form in a single decision.

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the who-question drill ("who/what is the phrase about? demand it after the
  comma") is an ideal tutor move — cheap to generate, always applicable.
- Well: boundary-scanning strategy converts a slow reading task into a fast
  pattern check; strong candidate for our expert-reasoning steps.
- Well: the possessive-subject trap is called out explicitly with a near-absolute
  elimination heuristic.
- Poorly: "misplaced" coverage is thin relative to "dangling" — few systematic
  placements (adverb placement, only/almost, squinting modifiers) are covered; our
  graph node should broaden the taxonomy beyond the two-way split.
- Poorly: no guidance on when a modifier question is disguised as a "which choice
  best..." style item; classification cues live in the choice shapes, and we
  should teach that explicitly.
