# Raw notes — Grammar source A: explanation-writing principles (for OUR original explanations)

Source: Meltzer grammar guide, 6th ed., mixed-set answer key studied as a model of
explanation craft. These are generalized writing principles, not content.

## What makes these explanations effective (adopt)

1. **Label the skill before anything else.** Every explanation opens with the tested
   concept's name. The student instantly learns "what kind of problem was this" —
   the single highest-value metadata in mixed practice, and the hook our tutor needs
   for skill-history tracking.

2. **Evidence → requirement → verdict ordering.** The explanation first shows what
   the passage contains (the two ideas oppose; the true subject is singular), then
   names the class of answer that fact demands, and only then points at the letter.
   The answer arrives as a logical consequence, never as an assertion. Students can
   replay the derivation on new items because it starts from observable text.

3. **Category elimination over choice-by-choice slog.** Wrong answers are dismissed
   in groups ("both of those are contrast words — the passage continues an idea"),
   which teaches the taxonomy while halving explanation length. Full four-choice
   autopsies are reserved for items with a genuinely magnetic trap.

4. **Name the trap out loud.** The best moments are explicit warnings: "these two
   choices are clear, concise, and grammatically correct — and both wrong," followed
   by the precise failure (the opener describes a person, so that person's name must
   follow the comma). Validating the distractor's appeal BEFORE refuting it is what
   makes the refutation stick; a student who picked it feels diagnosed, not dumb.

5. **Teach the reusable procedure inside the answer.** Recurring moves — read from the
   sentence start, not the blank; delete the paired-marks span and recheck; read the
   choices first to identify the tested concept; find the goal phrase in a synthesis
   prompt and demand the answer satisfy every part of it — appear verbatim across many
   explanations. Repetition across items is how a method becomes a habit; do not
   deduplicate these in our content.

6. **Flag shortcuts honestly as shortcuts.** Pattern-level speedups (a period inside
   a choice signals a sentence-split item; the background-for-unfamiliar-audience
   answer tends to be the longest; only one choice belongs to the required category,
   so it wins by default) are separated from grammar reasoning. This gives fast
   students test-craft without letting weak students mistake heuristics for rules.

7. **Explain why the distraction exists.** The strongest explanations say what the
   choice-set VARIATION is trying to make you think ("the tenses differ only to
   distract — the real split is number"). This inoculates against the trap design
   itself, not just this instance.

8. **Acknowledge alternates when multiple fixes are legal.** Fill-in keys list every
   acceptable punctuation variant. For multiple-choice, explanations note when a rule
   has a legitimate exception and why it applies here. Builds trust; prevents
   over-rigid rule formation.

## Weaknesses to avoid in our explanations

1. **Overconfident absolutes.** Several heuristics are stated as near-laws ("a comma
   before that is virtually always wrong," "this transition must always be
   punctuated"). Efficient, but a student who later meets a legitimate exception
   loses trust. Ours should grade certainty: "on the SAT, treat X as wrong unless…"

2. **"Correct by default" without the positive case.** When elimination leaves one
   survivor, some explanations never show why the survivor is affirmatively right.
   Elimination-only reasoning trains process of elimination but not the concept. We
   should add one positive sentence even when elimination suffices.

3. **Wall-of-prose formatting.** Explanations are single dense paragraphs; the rule,
   the evidence, and the trap are not visually separable. Our format (per existing
   product convention) should keep the same CONTENT depth but structure it —
   goal line, why-right, why-each-wrong, takeaway — without shortening.

4. **Letter-reference dependence.** Constant "(B)…(D)" cross-referencing forces the
   reader to bounce between explanation and choices. Ours should quote the decisive
   fragment of a choice inline so the explanation reads standalone.

5. **No difficulty/priority signal.** Every item gets similar treatment; a student
   can't tell a must-know rule from a rare edge case. Ours should carry a
   frequency/importance cue when a tested pattern is niche.

6. **Second person varies between coaching ("you'll find") and lecture.** Fine in a
   book; for our tutor the coaching voice ("back up and read…") consistently
   outperforms the lecture voice and should be the default register.

## Compact template distilled for our authoring (structure, not wording)

1. Skill label (maps to our bank taxonomy).
2. One-sentence evidence from the stem (what the text structurally contains).
3. The requirement that evidence creates (category of correct answer).
4. Why the credited choice satisfies it (quote its decisive fragment).
5. Distractor dispatch — grouped where possible; the magnetic trap gets its own
   named autopsy ("looks right because… breaks because…").
6. Portable takeaway: the one procedural move to reuse next time.
