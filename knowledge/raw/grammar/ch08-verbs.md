# Raw notes — Grammar source (Meltzer 6e), Ch. 8: subject-verb agreement and tense

Source scope: full verbs chapter (agreement disguises, indefinite pronouns, tense
system, would/will, passive voice, infinitive-vs-gerund) plus its two exercises and
the cumulative-review evidence relevant to verbs.
Maps to: Standard English Conventions → Form, Structure, and Sense.

## Concepts taught

- `verb-question-two-axes` — every SAT verb item tests one of two independent axes:
  number (singular/plural) or time (tense). Choices deliberately vary both axes at
  once, but only one axis ever decides the answer.
- `identify-the-axis-from-choices` — read the choice set FIRST: if it contains both
  singular and plural forms, the item is agreement; if all forms agree in number and
  differ in time, it is tense. This classification step precedes any grammar.
- `verb-s-inversion` — present-tense verbs behave opposite to nouns: the -s form is
  the SINGULAR verb, the bare form is plural. Third-person forms are the only ones
  tested because they're the only ones that inflect.
- `true-subject-location` — the noun immediately left of the verb is almost never the
  subject on a real item (that would be free); the subject must be hunted, usually
  back at the head of the clause, or found logically by asking "what actually performs
  this action?"
- `interrupter-blindness` — agreement disguise #1: a non-essential clause sits between
  subject and verb; the fix is to strike everything between the comma pair and rejoin
  subject to verb. A disagreement can also live INSIDE the interrupter (its relative
  pronoun inherits the number of the noun it modifies), or around an essential
  "that"-clause, which works the same way without commas.
- `prepositional-phrase-interference` — agreement disguise #2: a prepositional phrase
  ends with a noun of the opposite number parked right against the verb. Strike the
  phrase; the phrase's object can never be the subject.
- `inverted-syntax-agreement` — agreement disguise #3: verb precedes subject (often
  after an opening prepositional phrase, sometimes with no preposition at all, e.g., a
  fronted comparative). The strangeness of the word order is bait; the task is to find
  the post-verb subject and match it. These are claimed to be the hardest to hear.
- `compound-subject-plural` — two nouns joined by "and" are plural, period — even when
  each noun is singular, even when the compound is buried in a long sentence or
  inverted so only its second half is visible near the verb.
- `expletive-there-agreement` — in "there + be" frames the verb agrees with the noun
  that follows it.
- `gerund-subject-singular` — an -ing verbal acting as subject is singular, even when
  a plural noun sits inside it right before the verb.
- `collective-noun-singular` — group words (committee-type nouns) are singular in the
  tested standard, regardless of everyday or British usage.
- `clause-as-subject-singular` — a that/what/whether clause used as a subject is
  singular.
- `indefinite-pronoun-number` — three classes: always singular (one/each/every-type,
  much, "the number"), always plural (few, both, several, many, others, "a number"),
  and context-dependent (some/more/most/all/neither): with "of + singular" they're
  singular, with "of + plural" or alone in plural sense they're plural. Paired
  either/or-type correlatives agree with the noun nearer the verb; a lone
  "neither" means "neither one" and is singular.
- `tense-from-context` — tense items are decided by the surrounding sentences, not the
  target sentence: several choices are usually grammatical in isolation; the default
  is parallelism with neighboring verbs unless the timeline gives a clear reason to
  shift.
- `tense-signal-words` — lexical tip-offs beat memorized conjugation: since/for/over/
  during → present perfect; explicit dates and named past periods → simple past;
  "by the time" → past perfect (or future perfect when the frame is future); a past
  date plus a not-yet-happened-from-that-viewpoint action → "would."
- `past-perfect-ordering` — with two completed actions, only the EARLIER one takes
  had + participle; also derivable by pure logic about which event must precede.
- `would-vs-will` — will = real future/predictions; would = hypotheticals, habitual
  past action, and future-in-the-past.
- `conditional-clause-form` — "would have" never sits inside the if-clause itself;
  the if-clause takes had + participle while the result clause carries would have.
- `irregular-participle-forms` — irregular simple pasts and participles differ
  (vowel-shift families); using a simple past where a participle belongs (or bare
  participle as a main verb) is a tested error class.
- `passive-voice-preference` — passive (object promoted, "by + agent", be + participle)
  is not itself an error, but between two otherwise-clean choices the active one is
  the expected answer; passivity correlates with wordiness distractors.
- `infinitive-vs-gerund-idiom` — to-form vs -ing-form after a given verb/adjective is
  pure idiom with no derivable rule; swapping to a gerund usually forces a preposition
  in front of it; when both forms are acceptable the exam won't offer them against
  each other. Explicitly flagged as low-frequency, not worth memorizing lists for.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Leads by DISENTANGLING the two axes (number vs. time) before teaching either —
  the first worked item is designed to show that fixing the wrong axis can still
  produce a grammatical sentence and a wrong mental model.
- Teaches a work-backwards protocol driven by the answer choices rather than by
  reading the passage first: classify the axis, then hunt the subject, then match.
- Agreement is taught as a taxonomy of DISGUISES (interrupter, prep phrase, inversion,
  compound, expletive, gerund…) rather than as one rule — the rule is trivial, the
  camouflage is the curriculum.
- Physical crossing-out of interference (pencil through the interrupter or phrase) is
  prescribed as a practice ritual so the reflex survives on-screen where marking isn't
  possible.
- Tense is taught signal-first (tip-off words) with formal tense names kept minimal;
  complex tenses are explicitly de-prioritized as rare correct answers.
- Ends with an explicit ROI judgment (idiom lists not worth memorizing) — the author
  models effort allocation, not just content.

## Expert reasoning patterns

1. **Axis triage from the choice set.** Scan choices for a number split; present →
   agreement problem, ignore tense variation entirely (it's decoration). No number
   split → tense problem, start reading neighboring sentences.
2. **Subject hunt with interference stripping.** Strike comma-paired interrupters and
   prepositional phrases as a single reflex; whatever noun remains welded to the verb
   position is the candidate subject. Cross-check logically: can this noun actually
   perform/hold this predicate? (A thing near the verb that can't logically do the
   action isn't the subject.)
3. **Inversion protocol.** When the verb arrives early or the sentence opens with a
   place/among/along phrase, expect the subject AFTER the verb; ask "what is [predicate]?"
   and let the answer's number set the verb.
4. **Compound scan.** Before accepting a singular verb, sweep the whole subject region
   for an "and" that widens the subject.
5. **Timeline anchoring for tense.** Collect the tenses of the verbs around the blank
   and any date/duration signals; default to matching the neighbors; deviate only when
   an ordering cue (earlier-of-two-past-events, since-duration, past-viewpoint future)
   demands a marked tense.
6. **Two-verbs-one-subject check.** In compound predicates each verb must be fully and
   correctly conjugated for its own time reference — a shared auxiliary can't serve two
   different tenses.

## Misconceptions targeted

- **fix-by-ear-with-tense** — hearing something wrong, students reach for a tense
  change because tense differences are audible, when the actual fault is number.
  Formed because casual speech tolerates agreement slips but not tense slips. Produces
  picks like a past-tense singular where a present plural is needed. The test builds
  choice sets where a tense-changed distractor "sounds fixed."
- **nearest-noun-is-subject** — students match the verb to the adjacent noun. Formed
  by proximity heuristics from everyday listening (usually right in speech!). This is
  THE central exploited misconception: every disguise structure exists to weaponize it.
- **odd-syntax-feels-wrong** — in inverted sentences students "correct" the word order
  feeling by choosing the verb that matches the fronted phrase's noun. Formed because
  inversion is rare in student writing.
- **collective-noun-sounds-plural** — a group of people doing things feels plural
  (reinforced by British media usage), so students pick plural verbs for committee-type
  subjects.
- **gerund-phrase-counts-its-nouns** — students let a plural noun inside a gerund
  subject set the verb's number, because they never identified the -ing word itself as
  the subject.
- **pause-comma before verbs** (cross-over from boundaries) — long subjects invite a
  comma before the verb; here it resurfaces as students accepting choices that both
  fix agreement and insert an illegal comma.
- **would-have-in-if-clauses** — symmetric-sounding "if X would have… Y would have…"
  feels balanced; students don't know the if-clause takes the plain past perfect.
- **complex-tense-attraction** — students assume the fancier tense (had been / would
  be) is more likely correct on a hard question; the source claims the opposite skew.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim)

- unverified-claim: at least one agreement item appears on virtually every test.
- unverified-claim: subject and verb are essentially never adjacent on real agreement
  items; some separation device is always present.
- unverified-claim: mixed-tense choice sets on agreement items are pure distraction —
  tense never needs to be resolved to answer them.
- unverified-claim: present-perfect items are decidable from the single sentence
  containing the blank (since/for signal), unlike other tense items which need
  neighboring sentences.
- unverified-claim: "would have"/"will have" choices are historically almost never
  correct answers.
- unverified-claim: infinitive-vs-gerund items are rare but legitimate on the digital
  test; when both forms are idiomatic the pair won't be offered as competing choices.
- unverified-claim: wordy passive constructions mark wrong answers when a clean active
  rewrite is among the choices.

## Cumulative testing patterns (verbs mixed with other concepts)

- **Number × time matrix choices.** The combined exercises offer 4 choices spanning
  both axes (e.g., singular-present / plural-present / plural-perfect / participle),
  so the solver must run axis triage before anything else. A bare -ing form regularly
  appears as the "creates a fragment" decoy on these sets.
- **Agreement fused with boundary errors.** Some choices fix the verb but smuggle in a
  comma between subject and verb (or break an appositive), so verb items double as
  comma audits — a strong argument for our graph edging Boundaries ↔ agreement drills.
- **Appositive-then-verb.** A subject followed by a comma-wrapped rename ("X, a series
  of…," ) baits the verb into agreeing with the rename's noun rather than the subject.
- **Habitual-time cues vs. story tenses.** Passages mix a habitual-present frame
  ("every year"-type cues) with past narration so the student must localize which
  timeframe governs the blank rather than averaging the passage's tenses.
- **Prediction frames.** A past-tense discovery sentence followed by a scientists-
  predict frame requires a future form in a passage that is otherwise past — testing
  that "parallel by default" yields to explicit timeline evidence.
- **Neither/each/one heads with plural of-phrases** recur across both the dedicated
  and cumulative exercises as the indefinite-pronoun workhorse trap.

## Prerequisite edges implied

- `parts-of-speech-recognition` → can't hunt subjects without separating nouns from
  verbals and prepositions.
- `prepositional-phrase-identification` → the strip-the-phrase reflex presupposes
  recognizing where a phrase starts and ends.
- `non-essential-interrupter-pairing` (from Boundaries) → interrupter-blindness reuses
  the same comma-pair machinery; agreement disguise #1 is literally a Boundaries skill
  reapplied.
- `clause-vs-fragment` → the -ing-decoy pattern and conditional-clause forms lean on
  knowing what makes a complete predicate.
- `timeline-construction-from-text` → past perfect and would/will are unteachable
  without ordering events from prose evidence.

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the axis-triage move (classify the question from the choices before reading)
  is the single most reusable protocol in the chapter — direct tutor material.
- Well: framing agreement as a small taxonomy of disguises gives us a ready-made
  difficulty ladder: adjacent → prep phrase → interrupter → inversion → inverted
  compound (the source itself notes inverted compounds hide errors best).
- Well: honest base-rate guidance (complex tenses rarely correct; idiom items rare)
  supports effort-weighted study plans.
- Poorly: number rules are asserted, not explained — no account of WHY collectives or
  gerunds are singular (conceptual unity), so weaker students memorize a list. Our
  node should teach "one bounded thing → singular" as the generative principle.
- Poorly: tense coverage leans on tip-off words; a student facing a signal-free tense
  item gets little method beyond "match the neighbors." Our node should teach explicit
  timeline diagramming as the fallback.
- Poorly: the irregular-forms table is reference material with no diagnostic angle
  (which confusions actually appear as items); needs verification against our bank.
