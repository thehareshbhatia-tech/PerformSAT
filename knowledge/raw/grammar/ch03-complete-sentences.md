# Raw extraction — Complete sentences vs fragments (clause types)

Source: Meltzer SAT Grammar 6th ed., ch. 3 (scans p0047–p0060).
Maps to: Standard English Conventions → Boundaries (sentence-hood layer).
Status: raw note, our words only.

## Concepts taught

- `sentence-minimum-requirements` — A complete sentence needs exactly two things: a
  subject and a conjugated verb that matches it. Length is irrelevant; a one-word
  command qualifies (subject implied), and a five-line monster can still be a
  fragment.
- `sentencehood-is-structural-not-semantic` — THE central idea: whether a string is a
  sentence has nothing to do with whether it makes sense out of context. "It grows."
  and "Many believe that." are sentences; a rich, informative 40-word string can be a
  fragment. Students must judge by structure (subject + conjugated verb), never by
  informativeness.
- `independent-clause` — A subject+conjugated-verb unit that can stand alone. Taught
  as the atom that all boundary punctuation operates on.
- `additive-elements-preserve-sentencehood` — A family of add-ons can be layered onto
  a minimal sentence without changing its status: prepositional phrases (start,
  middle, end), adverbs (zero grammatical effect anywhere), non-essential insertions,
  participial phrases, leading dependent clauses. Taught by mutating ONE base
  sentence step by step.
- `prepositional-phrase-not-a-sentence` — Preposition+noun chunks can pile up
  anywhere in a sentence but can never BE a sentence, and never contain the subject.
- `pronoun-subject-equivalence` — Swapping a noun subject for a pronoun (it/they/
  many/most) leaves sentence-hood untouched even though the sentence stops "making
  sense" in isolation. Explicitly framed as the spot where most students break.
- `quantity-pronoun-fork` — Quantity words (many/most/some/several/few/all) fork two
  ways: as a bare subject ("Many are grown...") they make an independent clause;
  followed by "of which/of whom" they open a RELATIVE clause that cannot stand alone.
  So "...world, most of them believe..." is a splice, while "...world. Most of them
  believe..." and "...world, most of whom believe..." are both correct — a
  three-way contrast worth reproducing with fresh content.
- `nonessential-insert` — Extra description dropped between subject and verb,
  fenced by a comma pair, removable without breaking the sentence. Usually opens with
  a relative word (which/who) and is followed by a verb. Two failure modes: (a)
  deleting only half the fence, (b) ending the sentence right after the insert — the
  relative word has "stolen" the verb, leaving the outer subject verb-less.
- `appositive` — A non-essential insert that begins with a noun instead of a relative
  word; can also sit at the head or tail of a sentence as a description. Same
  removability logic.
- `relative-clause-opener-shortcut` — A non-question statement that starts with
  which/who(se)/where is not a sentence (rare exception: when the w-word itself is
  the subject of the statement).
- `ing-word-is-not-a-verb` — An -ing form on its own is never the conjugated verb.
  Subject + -ing word with no conjugated verb = fragment. Fix by conjugating.
  Corollary taught as a test heuristic: answer choices containing "being" are
  usually wrong (creates fragments and flab). (unverified-claim for current exam)
- `participial-phrase` — Verb-derived modifier phrases (present -ing, past -ed/-n,
  and the "having + past form" construction) that can open, interrupt, or close a
  sentence, but can never stand alone.
- `gerund-vs-participle` — Same -ing surface, different job: participle modifies,
  gerund acts as a noun. Only distinction needed at this level: neither is a
  conjugated verb.
- `coordinating-conjunction-clause-not-alone` — For exam purposes, a lone clause
  opening with one of the seven coordinating joiners (and/but/so etc.) is treated as
  not complete.
- `subordinating-conjunction-dependence` — A clause opened by a subordinator
  (because/although/when/while/since/unless...) is dependent; it can legally START a
  sentence only if an independent clause follows the comma. Explicitly debunks the
  schoolroom "never start a sentence with because" rule as half-true.
- `subordinate-clause-needs-conjugated-verb` — If a subordinate clause contains its
  own subject, that clause needs a conjugated verb too (subject + -ing inside an
  "although..." clause is still a fragment). But subject-less -ing after TIME
  subordinators (while/when/before/after/since) is fine ("Since moving abroad, she...").

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Opens with a 15-item rapid classification drill BEFORE any instruction, with a
  seconds-per-item cap — surfaces the student's broken intuition first, so the
  following lesson lands as an explanation of their own errors.
- Immediately then shows the SAME judgment embedded in a real test-style item where
  the intuition ("that clause sounds weird alone, can't be a sentence") picks the
  wrong answer — trap-first teaching: let the misconception fail publicly, then name
  the principle.
- Single evolving example: one minimal sentence is grown element by element
  (preposition chunks → pronoun swap → adverbs → inserts → participials →
  conjunctions), isolating one variable per step. Excellent for causal understanding;
  each add-on is also shown FAILING to stand alone.
- Explicit meta-framing: this chapter is positioned as the foundation for every
  punctuation chapter after it — you cannot place periods/commas/semicolons if you
  can't tell sentence from fragment.
- Repair exercises constrain the fix to ONE word changed/added/deleted — forces
  precise structural diagnosis (find the broken verb slot) instead of paraphrase.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

- Verb-anchored parse: find conjugated verb(s) → find the subject each belongs to →
  anything left over is decoration. Sentence-hood falls out mechanically.
- Ignore meaning during structure checks: experts deliberately suppress "does this
  make sense alone?" and ask only "subject? conjugated verb attached to it?"
- Ownership tracing for verbs: when a relative word appears, ask which subject each
  verb actually belongs to — an insert's verb can't serve the outer subject.
- Deletion test for inserts: mentally remove comma-fenced material and re-read; the
  frame must still be a sentence.
- Fragment triage by opener: string opens with preposition / -ing / relative word /
  subordinator → immediately suspect fragment until an independent clause shows up.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **sentence-equals-complete-thought** — Elementary school defines a sentence as "a
  complete thought," so students judge by semantic self-sufficiency. Produces both
  false negatives (pronoun-subject sentences rejected) and false positives (long
  informative fragments accepted). The test exploits this by putting
  context-dependent but complete clauses after a blank.
- **length-implies-completeness** — Long, detail-rich strings feel sentence-like;
  the exam builds elaborate fragments (name + relative clause + participial phrase,
  no main verb) to exploit it.
- **ing-form-counts-as-verb** — -ing words look maximally verby; students accept
  "X having been..." / "the lakes being..." as complete. Exam answer choices swap
  conjugated verbs for -ing forms to harvest this.
- **never-start-with-because** — An over-taught childhood rule; students reject
  correct subordinator-first sentences and can't articulate when the pattern IS legal.
- **relative-clause-can-finish-a-sentence** — Students hear a verb near the end
  ("...which is home to 9.5 million people") and count it as the main verb, missing
  that the relative word owns it.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim)

- Boundary items always live inside short passages, where surrounding information
  DISTRACTS from the structural judgment; the skill must survive context noise.
- A common trap design: the second clause after the blank is a complete sentence that
  doesn't feel like one (pronoun subject, quantity-word subject); the student rejects
  the period option.
- Elaborate-fragment stems: proper noun + non-essential matter, with the choices
  toggling whether a conjugated main verb ever appears.

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the pre-test → trap → principle arc; one-variable-at-a-time buildout; the
  structural-not-semantic principle stated bluntly and repeatedly; the quantity-
  pronoun fork (them vs which/whom) treated as its own confusion point — most books
  skip it; one-word-fix exercise design.
- Poorly: routine is implicit — never compiled into a numbered checklist a student
  can run under time pressure (our node should ship the explicit decision
  procedure); dense paragraphs, no diagrams of clause anatomy; the rare w-word-as-
  subject exception is a footnote that could confuse more than help; no spaced
  re-testing of the opening drill after instruction.
