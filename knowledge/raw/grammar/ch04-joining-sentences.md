# Raw extraction — Joining and separating sentences (boundary punctuation)

Source: Meltzer SAT Grammar 6th ed., ch. 4 (scans p0061–p0073).
Maps to: Standard English Conventions → Boundaries (punctuation layer).
Status: raw note, our words only.

## Concepts taught

- `three-legal-joins` — Exactly three legal ways to mark the boundary between two
  independent clauses: (1) period or semicolon, (2) comma + coordinating conjunction,
  (3) colon or dash (conditional — see below). A comma alone is never one of them.
- `period-semicolon-equivalence` — For boundary purposes, period and semicolon are
  the SAME mark (capitalization aside). Students should collapse them into one
  category: "full stop."
- `comma-splice` — Comma alone between two independent clauses; categorically wrong.
  Two constructions generate most splices: (a) second clause starts with a pronoun
  subject, (b) second clause starts with an adverb/transition word.
- `comma-plus-pronoun-alarm` — Diagnostic signal: the substring ", it/he/she/they/one"
  near an underline usually marks a splice; treat as a high-prior flag to check
  clause count. (unverified-claim as to frequency)
- `conjunctive-adverb-needs-full-stop` — Transition adverbs (however, therefore,
  moreover, consequently...) and time-words (today, currently, later, now) that open
  a clause must sit after a period or semicolon, never after a comma. Grammatically
  the adverb is invisible: adding it doesn't change what punctuation the boundary
  needs.
- `fanboys-comma-rule` — The seven coordinating joiners (commonly taught under a
  well-known acronym) glue two independent clauses only WITH a preceding comma;
  without the comma it's a run-on. After a period or semicolon, a coordinating
  joiner is redundant/wrong — any such answer choice can be killed on sight.
- `compound-predicate-no-comma` — When the second verb shares the first clause's
  subject (subject not restated), no comma goes before "and". Verification move:
  since comma+and behaves like a period, replace it with a period and check whether
  BOTH halves are complete sentences; if the second half fails, the comma is wrong.
- `equivalent-choices-cancel` — Meta-rule with big leverage: period, semicolon, and
  comma+joiner are interchangeable, so a question can never hinge on choosing among
  them. If two or more answer choices are grammatically identical boundary marks,
  ALL of them are wrong, and the remaining structure (often a plain comma before/
  after a dependent clause) must be right. Double-check the survivor, but expect it
  to hold.
- `colon-dash-conditional-join` — A colon or dash CAN sit between two independent
  clauses, but only when the second clause explains, defines, or gives the result of
  the first. If the second clause merely continues the topic with more information,
  a colon is wrong and a full stop is required. Book claims College Board preliminary
  material makes the colon disproportionately likely in CORRECT answers.
  (unverified-claim — check against our bank/official items)
- `boundary-question-anatomy` — These items are really asking one question: "one
  sentence or two?" The punctuation menu is downstream of that count.
- `full-sentence-read-rule` — Procedure: wherever the blank falls, back up to the
  start of the sentence and read through to the final period before judging.
  Local-window reading is the failure mode the items are built to punish.
- `hinge-phrase-trap` — Phrase types that read naturally at the END of clause 1 but
  actually belong to the START of clause 2: prepositional phrases, -ing (participial)
  phrases, subordinator clauses (because/although...). With no punctuation the whole
  thing becomes a run-on; the correct answer cuts BEFORE the hinge phrase, which then
  feels "orphaned" to students reading locally. This is the chapter's signature trap,
  demonstrated three times with the same passage mutated (preposition version, -ing
  version, subordinator version).
- `boundary-choices-first-strategy` — When scanning answer choices, test the ones
  containing boundary marks (especially period and colon) first; if the test offers a
  two-sentence split, odds are good the split is the point. (unverified-claim)
- `transition-add-options` — Menu extension: a boundary can also be crossed with
  comma + coordinating joiner or semicolon + transition adverb, giving the writer a
  way to add explicit logic (cause, contrast) while staying legal. Presented as a
  summary equivalence table: full stop family vs logic-adding family.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Same-passage mutation: one base passage is re-punctured three ways (prepositional
  hinge, -ing hinge, subordinator hinge) so the student sees one trap ARCHITECTURE
  wearing three costumes — teaches the trap as a class, not as instances.
- Answer-choice forensics as first-class content: the chapter teaches reading the
  CHOICE SET (equivalence cancellation, boundary-marks-first) as much as the passage.
  The test's one-right-answer constraint is turned into a weapon.
- Rules are compressed into equations/identities students can chant: full stop =
  semicolon = comma+joiner; colon needs explanation; comma alone never joins.
- Prior-chapter dependency is explicit: every rule here consumes the sentence/
  fragment judgment from ch3; the book keeps saying the punctuation choice is
  trivial once the clause count is known.
- Exercises escalate: mechanical repair (fix a splice with exactly ONE punctuation
  change, some items already correct) → full test-style multiple choice. The
  one-change constraint again forces diagnosis before action.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

- Clause-count-first routine (the core expert procedure, worth making explicit in
  our graph node):
  1. Read the whole sentence, start to final period, ignoring the blank's local feel.
  2. Find each conjugated verb and its subject → count independent clauses.
  3. One clause → no boundary punctuation may appear; two → pick from the legal menu.
  4. If two clauses: does clause 2 explain/define clause 1? colon/dash eligible;
     otherwise full-stop family or comma+joiner.
  5. Cross-check the choice set: strike equivalent boundary options, strike
     period/semicolon + coordinating joiner, strike comma + transition adverb.
- Boundary placement by ownership: for a hinge phrase, ask which clause NEEDS it —
  attach modifiers to the clause they modify, not the clause they happen to sit near.
- Sound-check suppression: experts never decide by pause-feel; every choice reduces
  to clause count + menu lookup.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **comma-as-soft-period** — Students learn commas as "small pauses," so a long
  breath between two clauses feels comma-worthy. Produces splice acceptance. The
  test writes long first clauses to make the splice feel natural.
- **however-behaves-like-but** — Meaning-equivalence masks grammar-difference
  (adverb vs conjunction); produces ", however," splices. Deep cause: school never
  distinguishes word MEANING from word CLASS.
- **local-window-judgment** — Students judge the blank by the few words around it
  because reading the whole sentence feels slow; the hinge-phrase items are
  engineered so the local read endorses the run-on option.
- **semicolon-as-fancy-comma** — Semicolons are taught late and vaguely, so students
  deploy them before fragments and dependent clauses ("...; all of which...").
  The fix taught: semicolon carries the full weight of a period — both sides must
  pass the ch3 sentence test.
- **colon-only-for-lists** — Students under-use legal clause-joining colons and
  can't distinguish "explains" (colon OK) from "adds more info" (colon wrong);
  they also over-accept colons where no explanation relationship exists once told
  colons can join clauses. The book teaches the explains/continues contrast with a
  matched pair.
- **orphan-phrase-aversion** — Starting sentence 2 with a modifier ("Depicting a
  wide range of subjects, she...") feels wrong to students because the phrase
  "belongs" to the previous thought; they therefore reject the correct split. Root:
  same semantic-completeness instinct from ch3.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim)

- Boundary questions are framed as "conforms to the conventions of Standard
  English" completions with a blank at a clause seam.
- Colon appears disproportionately in correct answers (per CB preliminary
  material, as read by this author).
- The equivalence-cancellation situation (three interchangeable boundary marks + one
  structurally different option) is a recurring real item shape.
- Splice bait is most often comma+pronoun or comma+transition-adverb.

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the trap architecture (hinge phrase) taught as a reusable class; the
  equivalence-cancellation meta-strategy (rarely taught elsewhere, high leverage);
  identities/equations as memory hooks; explicit full-sentence-read procedure;
  exercise sets that include already-correct items to punish trigger-happy fixing.
- Poorly: the expert routine is scattered across bold callouts instead of compiled
  into one checklist; colon/dash treatment is thin (dash gets one line, no
  dash-specific practice); no coverage here of semicolons in complex lists; the
  "colon likely correct" claim is actuarial and may age badly — verify before the
  tutor asserts it; prose-dense pages with the key identities easy to lose.

## Cross-chapter note for synthesis

Ch3+ch4 form one unit: sentence-hood (structural judgment) → boundary menu
(punctuation lookup). Our graph should model them as two nodes with a hard prereq
edge, and the tutor's diagnostic should distinguish "can't count clauses" (ch3
failure — remediate sentence anatomy) from "counts clauses but picks illegal mark"
(ch4 failure — remediate the menu/identities). The book's own exercises support this
split: students who miss one-word-fix fragment repairs have the first problem;
students who fix splices with semicolons before fragments have the second.
