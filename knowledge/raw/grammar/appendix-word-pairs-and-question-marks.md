# Raw notes — Grammar source A, Appendices: correlative pairs / comparisons / question marks

Source: Meltzer grammar guide, 6th ed., Appendices A + B (teacher-training input only).
Extraction focus: concepts + trap logic. All wording and examples here are our own.
The source itself flags these appendix topics as paper-SAT holdovers that had NOT yet
appeared on released digital practice tests as of late 2022 — treat as low-frequency,
high-scorer-only content pending verification against our bank and newer official material.

## Concepts taught

- **correlative-pair-integrity** — Certain comparison/pairing words come in fixed
  two-part templates; the second half is not interchangeable. Candidate pairs: equality
  ("as ... as"), addition-emphasis ("not only ... but also"), inequality
  ("more/-er ... than", "less ... than"), joint negation/alternation
  ("neither ... nor", "either ... or"). The test breaks the pair by swapping the
  second element for a near-synonym conjunction (e.g. "than" where "as" is required,
  "or" after "neither", "and" after "not only").
- **like-to-like comparison** — A comparison is only valid when both sides are the
  same kind of entity (person↔person, thing↔thing, attribute↔attribute). Classic break:
  comparing someone's work/attribute to another PERSON instead of to that person's
  work/attribute.
- **comparison-repair moves** — Three legal fixes for an unbalanced comparison:
  (1) repeat the noun with a possessive on the second side, (2) restate the noun in an
  of-phrase, (3) substitute a stand-in phrase: "that of" for a singular noun,
  "those of" for a plural noun. The stand-in must agree in number with the noun it
  replaces — "that of" pointing at a plural, or "those of" pointing at a singular,
  is itself an error the test exploits.
- **attribute-vs-object mismatch** — Subtler like-to-like violation: both sides are
  "things" but not equivalent things (an object vs. a measurement of an object).
  Both sides must be lifted to the same level (attribute vs attribute) or the
  comparison fails.
- **countable-vs-mass quantity words** — "fewer"/"many" pair with countable plural
  nouns; "less"/"much" pair with uncountable singular nouns. The decision procedure
  is: find the noun being quantified, ask "can I count individual units of this?",
  then pick the counting word or the bulk word. Near-identical noun phrases can flip
  the answer (a plural "species/types" is countable; a collective "life" is not),
  which is exactly the contrast the test builds.

## Concepts taught — question marks (Appendix B)

- **direct-vs-embedded question** — A direct question stands alone as an asking
  sentence and ends with a question mark. An embedded (indirect) question is a
  clause folded inside a statement — typically introduced by "whether" or "if" —
  and the sentence ends with a period because the sentence as a whole asserts,
  it does not ask.
- **word-order signal** — Inverted order (verb before subject) signals a true
  question; statement order (subject before verb) signals an embedded clause.
  The four answer choices typically form a 2×2 grid: {inverted, statement order} ×
  {question mark, period}. Only the coherent diagonal cells are ever right: after
  "whether/if" you need statement order AND a period. Choices that mix
  statement-order with "?" or inverted-order with "." are internally inconsistent.
- **detachability test** — Expert check: can the questioning part be lifted out and
  stand alone as a sensible standalone question? If yes → direct → question mark.
  If it only makes sense welded into the host sentence → embedded → period.

## Misconceptions the source targets (our words)

- **"question content = question mark"** — students punctuate by topic (uncertainty
  mentioned → "?") instead of by sentence function. Fix: the detachability test above.
- **"any contrast word completes a pair"** — students treat correlative templates as
  mix-and-match ("not only...and", "neither...or") because each half feels independently
  fine. Fix: teach the pairs as single vocabulary items, not as compositional grammar.
- **"'that of' is fancier so it's safer"** — students sprinkle "that of/those of"
  without checking number or necessity; the phrase is only correct when it replaces a
  stated noun of matching number.
- **"less/fewer is a style choice"** — students think both are acceptable; the
  countability rule is deterministic and testable.

## SAT patterns claimed (verify before trusting)

- Faulty comparisons appeared as a small slice of the old paper Writing test and may
  surface rarely on the digital exam — the source is explicit about this uncertainty.
- Question-mark items are staged as boundaries questions ("conforms to the conventions
  of Standard English") with the 2×2 order/punctuation choice grid.

## Prerequisite edges implied

- correlative-pair-integrity ← basic conjunction awareness
- comparison-repair ← possessive forms; singular/plural noun identification
- embedded-question punctuation ← independent-clause recognition (what can stand alone)

## What this source does well / poorly here

- Well: incorrect/correct sentence contrast pairs isolate exactly one variable per
  example; the "that of/those of" number-agreement wrinkle is a genuinely sharp
  distinction most prep sources skip; honest frequency labeling (appendix = "just in
  case" content for high scorers).
- Poorly: pure rule presentation — no mnemonic or deeper why for the correlative
  pairs; no mixed drill for these appendix topics, so no diagnostic signal about
  which distractors students actually fall for.
