# Raw notes — Rhetorical Synthesis / "student notes" questions (source: Meltzer grammar guide, ch. 2)

Maps to: Expression of Ideas → Rhetorical Synthesis (bulleted research notes + stated goal + "which choice best uses the notes"). Provenance only; all wording is ours.

## Concepts taught

- `goal-phrase-primacy` — The stated goal sentence ("The student wants to …") is the ONLY evaluation criterion. The correct answer is essentially a restatement of the goal's key phrase in new words; the question contains its own answer key.
- `goal-decomposition-checklist` — Parse the goal into its verb (present / introduce / emphasize / specify) plus its focus phrase, and when the goal has multiple components (a focus AND an audience, a focus AND a rhetorical relation), turn it into a checklist. The right answer must satisfy every component; satisfying one is the classic near-miss.
- `notes-are-skippable` — The bullet list is mostly a decoy for this question type: every answer choice is faithfully drawn from the notes, so verifying choices against the bullets is not the tested skill and reading the bullets first wastes time. Truth-checking traps (right focus, but content absent from the notes) are claimed not to be part of the design.
- `feature-prediction-before-scanning` — From the goal checklist, predict observable features the right answer must display (a comparison word, background info, a range of examples, a statement of purpose) — then scan choices for those features rather than reading each choice contemplatively.
- `answer-signatures-by-goal-type` — Recurring goal types have recognizable correct-answer fingerprints:
  - similarity/difference goals → the answer contains an explicit comparative or contrastive marker (a likeness word or a difference word); merely mentioning both subjects is not comparing them.
  - unfamiliar-audience goals → the answer embeds identity/background information (who the person is, what the thing is), tends to run longer than the other choices, and typically introduces a person by full name.
  - familiar-audience goals → the answer omits background (re-explaining signals a wrong choice).
  - introduce-the-goal-of-a-study → the answer states what researchers set out to learn (intent framing), not what they found (results framing).
  - emphasize-variety/range goals → the answer enumerates or characterizes multiple kinds, not a single instance.
- `category-precision` — The focus phrase must be matched at the correct category level, not by topical association (e.g., a goal about physical signalling is not satisfied by a choice about sound-based signalling; "signals generally" is weaker than "the specific body part used"). Adjacent-category and superset choices are wrong.
- `first-match-stop` — The design guarantees exactly one choice fits the checklist, so once a choice satisfies all components, an expert commits without contemplating the rest (at most a quick glance to confirm no second candidate).
- `length-is-not-difficulty` — These items look information-dense but are among the fastest on the test once the goal-first workflow is used.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Demystification-first: the chapter's opening move is lowering threat ("looks complicated, is mechanical"), because intimidation is the primary failure mode.
- Skill is taught in isolation before full format: the first worked example presents ONLY the goal + choices (no bullets at all) to prove the bullets are unnecessary; the full format arrives after the matching habit exists. Scaffold by removing the distractor element, then reintroducing it.
- Exercises are printed as an explicit two-step protocol (mark the goal's key words → find the matching choice), again forcing the intermediate step into the open.
- Answer-explanation style models "checklist verification": each choice is judged component-by-component against the goal, with explicit reasons ("right topic, missing background," "one instance, not a range").

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. Read the goal sentence first — not the bullets.
2. Underline/extract: goal verb + focus phrase + any audience or relation qualifier.
3. Convert to a required-ingredients checklist and predict surface features (comparison word? background clause? purpose framing? plural examples?).
4. Scan the choices for the predicted features — keyword-level scan first, then verify the full checklist on the surviving candidate(s).
5. Reject choices that are on-topic but functionally wrong (true fact, wrong rhetorical job).
6. Commit at first full match.
The macro-principle: this question type tests reading the QUESTION carefully, not reading the passage carefully — an inversion of every other reading item.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **bullets-first-processing** — reading the note list top-to-bottom before the question, because layout order and school habit ("read everything, then answer") dictate it. Cost is pure time; also floods working memory with facts that all reappear in choices, making every option feel supported.
- **topic-match-equals-goal-match** — accepting a choice because it discusses the right subject, without checking that it performs the right rhetorical job. Rooted in keyword-matching strategies learned for reading comprehension. This is the misconception the majority of distractors are built on.
- **truth-equals-correctness** — believing a factually accurate, notes-supported statement must be right. Formed because most school assessment rewards true statements; here all four choices are true, so truth has zero discriminating power.
- **adjacent-category-substitution** — letting a semantically related category satisfy the focus (sound for movement, awards for materials). Formed from loose paraphrase habits; the test exploits it with near-category choices.
- **complexity-freeze** — treating the dense format as a hard question and over-investing time or skipping. Formed from surface-difficulty heuristics.

## Trap taxonomy from example design (design only, no items)

- **Right-topic-wrong-function** (dominant trap): a choice about the correct subject that informs rather than compares, describes a result rather than a purpose, or gives background when emphasis is wanted.
- **Both-subjects-no-relation**: for comparison goals, a choice naming both entities (even stating one descends from the other) without an explicit likeness/difference assertion.
- **Opposite-relation marker**: a difference-signalling choice when similarity is requested (and vice versa) — eliminable on the marker word alone.
- **Single-instance-for-range**: goal wants breadth/variety; distractor offers one vivid example.
- **Background-omitted / background-only**: for unfamiliar-audience goals, one distractor has the focus but no introduction, another has the introduction but not the focus. The correct answer welds both.
- **Findings-for-purpose swap**: goal asks to introduce what a study aimed to learn; distractors present its results or conclusions (often more interesting-sounding, which is the bait).
- **Last-name-only cue**: introducing a person to a new audience by surname alone is a subtle wrongness signal in unfamiliar-audience items.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim)

- This type is native to the digital SAT; bullet sets run about 3–5 notes.
- All four choices always restate content genuinely present in the notes — no fabricated-content distractors (verify against official items before the tutor asserts it).
- Correct answers to unfamiliar-audience goals skew longer than their distractors; familiar-audience answers skew shorter (a usable meta-cue, but verify).
- The type is claimed to be among the most mechanical/fastest on the exam and to appear on effectively every form.

## Prerequisite edges implied

- transition/relation-word taxonomy (ch01) → recognizing comparative, contrastive, and causal markers inside answer choices
- gist-compression and paraphrase recognition → seeing that a choice "rewords" the goal phrase
- question-stem parsing (verb + object + qualifier) → goal decomposition; this is the same skill as parsing math word-problem asks — potential cross-section node.

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the inversion insight (interrogate the question, not the passage) is the single best idea here; the answer-signature catalog per goal type is directly reusable as tutor diagnostic rules; the bullets-hidden first example is a superb teaching scaffold; explicit "the trap you fear (unsupported content) does not exist" removes a whole class of wasted verification.
- Poorly: "never look at the bullets" is slightly overstated — it offers no fallback for items where two choices survive the checklist and note context would arbitrate; goal-verb coverage is partial (little on goals like summarizing a process or making a recommendation); no timing data; no misconception→distractor diagnostic mapping (we built one above).
