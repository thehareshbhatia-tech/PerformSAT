# Raw notes — Meltzer Grammar 6th ed., Ch. 5 (scans p0074–p0082)
Topic: joining full sentences to fragments / mixed clause combinations.
Maps to: Standard English Conventions → Boundaries.
Provenance only; all wording below is ours.

## Concepts taught

- **sentence-fragment-join-menu** — When a complete sentence must connect to a
  fragment (dependent clause or phrase), the legal connectors are a comma, a colon,
  or a dash. A period or a semicolon is never legal there, because both of those
  marks demand a full independent clause on each side. This is the single most
  compressible rule in the chapter: period/semicolon = two-sentence marks; the
  others tolerate a fragment on one side.
- **dependent-first-comma** — A clause opened by a subordinating conjunction
  (although, because, while, since, unless, if, when, etc.) is dependent by
  definition. When that clause leads the sentence, a comma must sit at the seam
  before the independent clause. When the independent clause leads, the comma is
  normally omitted — except before strong-contrast subordinators (the
  although/even-though family), where the comma stays.
- **one-connector-rule** — Between any two clauses, exactly one connecting word
  (subordinating conjunction OR coordinating/FANBOYS conjunction) may do the job.
  Stacking two ("Although X, but Y") leaves the sentence with no main clause.
- **participial-join** — An -ing (or -ed) phrase after a comma is a legitimate way
  to attach extra action to a clause; the comma before the participle is what
  separates "modifier of the clause" from "adverb glued to the wrong word."
- **with-plus-ing-equivalence** — A "with + noun + -ing-verb" tail is a
  grammatically acceptable paraphrase of "and + conjugated verb," always set off
  by a comma. The exam won't force a choice between the two equivalents; students
  only need to stop treating the with-construction as an error.
- **which-tail-attachment** — A clause opened by *which* is always dependent and
  must hang off a comma; gluing it on with no comma is a boundary error.
- **colon-launch-completeness** — Whatever precedes a colon must stand alone as a
  sentence; what follows may be a sentence (an explanation) or a fragment (a
  list/definition). Corollary shortcut: a colon immediately after a
  "for-example"-type lead-in (the such-as/including family) is nearly always wrong,
  because those words leave the pre-colon statement incomplete.
- **single-dash-explainer** — One dash can play the colon's role (complete
  sentence before it; sentence or fragment after). The colon-vs-dash choice is
  purely stylistic, so the exam does not pit a correct colon against a correct
  dash. A dash may also mark a deliberate dramatic interruption; students need
  only to recognize this as legal so they don't eliminate it for feeling unusual.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Rules are framed as a small closed menu (which marks can touch a fragment)
  rather than a long list of comma rules — decision-procedure-first teaching.
- The chapter escalates one question design through three difficulty layers using
  a near-constant carrier passage: (1) the opening conjunction is close to the
  blank; (2) a non-essential interrupter is wedged between subject and blank so
  the opening conjunction scrolls out of the student's attention window; (3) the
  conjunction appears mid-sentence AFTER the blank, so the student must read
  ahead, not back. Varying one variable on a constant carrier isolates the skill.
- Explicit exam meta is taught alongside grammar: what the test will and will not
  ask (e.g., no colon-vs-dash coin flips), which shrinks the study surface.
- Chapter presumes and lightly re-teaches earlier material (clause vs. fragment,
  semicolon rules), signaling a prerequisite chain rather than standalone units.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. **Widen the window before judging the blank.** The blank shows a local seam,
   but the verdict depends on whether a connector already exists anywhere in the
   sentence. Experts read from the true start of the sentence and, if needed,
   past the blank to its end, before touching the choices.
2. **Count connectors, not commas.** For two clauses, first ask: how many
   joining words are present or offered? If the sentence already opens with a
   subordinator, any choice adding a FANBOYS word is dead on arrival; the
   punctuation-only choice wins.
3. **Classify each side of the proposed mark.** For every candidate mark, label
   what sits on each side (full sentence vs. fragment), then check the mark
   against the menu: period/semicolon need sentence+sentence; colon/dash need
   sentence-first; comma tolerates sentence+fragment when a connector or
   participle licenses it.
4. **Fragment-by-pronoun check.** A choice that inserts a subject pronoun
   ("…, it/they + verb") converts a would-be fragment tail into a second
   independent clause and thereby into a comma splice — a favorite disguised
   wrong answer.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **semicolon-as-strong-comma** — Students treat the semicolon as a fancier
  comma usable before any afterthought. Root: school writing feedback rewards
  semicolons without teaching the two-independent-clause requirement. Trap
  design: a semicolon choice before an obvious fragment tail.
- **contrast-content-means-but** — Students see contrasting ideas around the
  blank and reflexively insert *but/however*, even though the sentence already
  opened with a subordinator. Root: they match connectors to MEANING in the
  local window instead of to STRUCTURE of the whole sentence. This is exactly
  what the interrupter-camouflaged version exploits.
- **comma-optional-after-lead-in** — Students drop the comma after a leading
  dependent clause because speech doesn't always pause there. Root:
  punctuate-by-ear habit.
- **colon-needs-list-only / colon-anywhere-before-list** — Two opposite faulty
  schemas: some students think colons only precede lists; others think any list
  justifies a colon even mid-clause. Root: colons taught by example, not by the
  completeness-before rule. Trap: colon right after a such-as-style lead-in.
- **dash-is-informal-error** — Students eliminate correct dash choices because
  dashes were discouraged in school essays. The book explicitly inoculates
  against over-elimination.
- **ing-word-glues-to-neighbor** — Without the comma, students read a
  participle as modifying the adjacent word and don't notice the garbled
  parse; with the comma they think it's an added error. Root: never taught the
  comma-plus-participle attachment pattern.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim — check against official items + bank)

- unverified-claim: In double-connector items, the answer choices usually contain
  only ONE of the two connectors; the other lives in the un-underlined stem, so
  the tested skill is noticing it, not choosing between transitions.
- unverified-claim: Fill-in-the-blank boundary items frequently differ ONLY in
  punctuation around one seam word (e.g., noun / noun-comma / noun-colon /
  noun-dash), making side-classification the whole game.
- unverified-claim: The exam does not test colon vs. dash head-to-head when both
  are correct, nor the two equivalent "and+verb" vs. "with+ing" paraphrases.
- unverified-claim: Correct answers are sometimes the *least* punctuated choice
  (bare comma, or nothing), exploiting students' bias that more punctuation
  looks more careful.

## Prerequisite edges implied

- clause-vs-fragment-recognition → everything here (can't classify sides
  otherwise).
- comma-splice + semicolon-two-sentence rule (prior chapter) → sentence-fragment-join-menu.
- subordinating-conjunction inventory → dependent-first-comma, one-connector-rule.
- sentence-fragment-join-menu + interrupter-spotting (next chapter) → the
  camouflaged double-connector items; the book explicitly forward-references
  interrupter deletion.

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: closed-menu framing; one-variable-at-a-time escalation on a constant
  carrier passage; explicit "what won't be tested" meta; the such-as/including
  colon shortcut is a high-yield, cheap-to-teach heuristic.
- Poorly: dense prose, no visual summary of the connector menu; the with+ing
  equivalence gets more space than its exam weight likely merits; exercises mix
  several sub-skills without tagging which rule each item drills, so a student
  can't self-diagnose; difficulty calibration of exercise items is implicit.
