# Raw extraction — Grammar source (Meltzer 6th ed), Ch. 10: Apostrophes

Source studied: chapter on apostrophes (noun plurals vs possessives, contractions,
possessive pronouns and their homophones). Maps to: Standard English Conventions →
Form, Structure, and Sense / Boundaries (punctuation of possession). All notes are
our own synthesis; no source wording or examples retained.

## Concepts taught (candidate node ids)

- `noun-form-grid` — every noun has four written shapes: plain plural (-s/-es, no
  apostrophe), singular possessive ('s), plural possessive (s'), and irregular
  plurals which take 's for possession because their plural form has no -s. The
  whole topic is choosing a cell in this 2x2 grid (number × possession).
- `plural-never-apostrophe` — pluralization alone never uses an apostrophe; the
  apostrophe encodes either possession or contraction, never "more than one."
- `noun-apostrophe-s-contraction` — noun + 's can also compress "is/has" onto a
  noun; students must be able to tell that reading from the possessive reading even
  though the test rarely targets it directly.
- `next-word-test` — the load-bearing decision rule: look at the word immediately
  after the noun in question. If it's another noun, possession is happening and an
  apostrophe belongs; if it's any other part of speech (verb, preposition,
  adjective-then-clause, end of sentence), no apostrophe. Rationale taught from
  first principles: only a noun can be owned.
- `two-noun-stack` — items underlining a noun-noun pair ("owner's owned-things")
  require two independent decisions: first noun gets number + apostrophe; second
  noun is plural-or-singular only and NEVER takes an apostrophe unless a third noun
  follows it.
- `pronoun-rule-inversion` — pronouns invert the noun rule: possessive pronouns are
  formed WITHOUT an apostrophe; a pronoun + apostrophe always signals a contraction
  with is/are/has. This inversion is the deepest structural fact of the chapter.
- `impossible-forms` — some spellings simply don't exist in English (possessive-of-it
  with any apostrophe placement); recognizing a nonexistent form allows instant
  elimination without context.
- `expansion-plug-in-test` — to decide contraction vs possessive homophones, expand
  the contraction to its full phrase and re-read the sentence. Works uniformly for
  the it-pair, the they-triple, the you-pair, and the who-pair.
- `there-is-place` — the third member of the they-triple is neither possessive nor
  contraction but a location/existential word; check whether the sentence points at
  a place.
- `agreement-then-apostrophe` — combined items are solved in fixed order: first
  determine singular vs plural from the referent, eliminating half the choices; then
  apply the expansion test to what remains.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Reduces a memorization-flavored topic to two mechanical, always-available tests
  (next-word test for nouns; expansion test for pronouns) instead of rule lists.
- Grounds each rule in a one-line "why" (only nouns can be possessed; possessive
  pronouns are their own words and need no marker) so the rule survives stress.
- Explicitly scopes effort: the it-pair and they-set are named as the main tested
  forms; you- and who- pairs are completeness.
- Teaches eliminate-by-form-before-context: nonexistent spellings and
  apostrophe-final second nouns die immediately, shrinking the real decision to two
  choices.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. Read the blank's immediate right neighbor first — before reading choices. Noun
   neighbor → apostrophe somewhere; non-noun neighbor → no apostrophe on that word.
2. For noun-noun stacks, work left to right: decide the owner's number from the
   passage logic (one brain per person; many birds in a flock), place the apostrophe
   accordingly, then leave the owned noun bare.
3. For any pronoun choice containing an apostrophe, mentally substitute the full
   verb phrase and re-read aloud; accept only if the sentence still parses.
4. In combined agreement+apostrophe sets, kill wrong-number choices first —
   number evidence is cheaper to gather than punctuation evidence.
5. Never let plausible-sounding speech decide: all the homophones sound identical,
   so sound carries zero information here; only structure does.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **apostrophe-marks-plural** — students insert an apostrophe when pluralizing,
  especially on long, unfamiliar, or already-s-ending words. WHY it feels right:
  the plural -s and the possessive -s are phonetically identical, and students have
  seen countless signage errors; the apostrophe reads as a general-purpose "flag
  that this noun was modified." Wrong-answer signature: plural-with-apostrophe
  choices in any noun item.
- **possessive-pronouns-need-apostrophes** — students write the contraction form
  for the possessive because school drilled "apostrophe-s shows ownership" on
  NOUNS and they overgeneralize to pronouns. The inversion (pronoun possessive =
  no apostrophe) is genuinely counterintuitive given the noun rule. Signature:
  picking the it-contraction before a noun.
- **homophone-collapse** — the they-triple and you-pair are stored as one fuzzy
  sound with three spellings chosen by habit, not analysis. Forms from writing by
  ear; texting habits reinforce it. The expansion test is the direct antidote.
- **apostrophe-as-decoration-on-second-noun** — in two-noun stacks students
  apostrophe-ize both nouns or the second noun, believing the "possessive zone"
  extends across the phrase. Forms from pattern-matching on surface adjacency
  rather than the ownership relation's direction.
- **s-apostrophe-means-fancy-singular** — students read the plural-possessive form
  as an alternative singular possessive, missing that apostrophe position ENCODES
  number. Forms because they've never been taught the position is meaningful.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim, verify vs our bank)

- The main pronouns tested with apostrophes are the it-forms and they-forms; you-
  and who- forms are rarer.
- Noun apostrophe items typically underline one or two nouns and permute
  plural/possessive combinations across the four choices.
- Nonexistent forms (apostrophe-after-possessive-of-it, etc.) do appear as
  distractors and are free eliminations.
- Noun+'s as an is/has contraction has not traditionally been a primary target but
  can appear; students should be able to disambiguate.
- Combined agreement+apostrophe items exist: choices cross number × apostrophe
  form, requiring the two-step order of operations.

## Combination + trap evidence from exercises and Cumulative Review II

- Recurring exercise trap: choice sets of the form {plain plural, singular
  possessive, plural possessive, double-possessive} over a noun-noun stack; the
  double-apostrophe choice is always wrong and tests whether students know the
  second noun stays bare.
- The its-family item recurs with {no-apostrophe, contraction, nonexistent form,
  wrong-number pronoun} — two eliminations are form-based, the last is agreement.
- Cumulative Review II includes a plural-possessive + plural-noun stack, confirming
  the two-decision pattern travels into mixed sets.
- Latin-plural trap observed: a noun whose singular and plural are identical (e.g.
  species-type words) with fake singular back-formations as distractors —
  misconception: every plural must differ from its singular.

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the next-word test is the single best teachable heuristic in this topic —
  fast, mechanical, and grounded in a real principle (nouns own nouns). Adopt as
  the mental model for our graph node.
- Well: naming the impossible forms gives students guilt-free instant eliminations.
- Well: consistent expansion test unifies four homophone families under one move.
- Poorly: doesn't dwell on WHY the pronoun rule inverts (possessive pronouns are
  closed-class words that predate the apostrophe convention); a one-line history
  or "its = his/her for things — you'd never write hi's" contrast would cement it.
  Our node should add a stronger anchor.
- Poorly: thin on the greengrocer's-plural misconception mechanism; we articulate
  the why above for tutor diagnosis.
