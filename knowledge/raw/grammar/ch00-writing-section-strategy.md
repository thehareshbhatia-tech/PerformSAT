# Raw notes — Writing-domain strategy (source: Meltzer grammar guide, intro + cheat sheet)

Source scope: introduction + one-page rule digest. Provenance only; all wording below is ours.

## Concepts taught

- `writing-domain-predictability` — The SAT's writing/grammar territory is a small, closed set of testable rules; nearly everything on a given exam can be anticipated in advance, so preparation = learning the finite rule inventory plus how each rule gets disguised.
- `frequency-first-study-order` — An expert sequences instruction by how often each question type actually appears on the exam, not by textbook-grammar logic. The highest-frequency types (transition logic, note-synthesis) are near-guaranteed on every form and get taught first; low-frequency rules are "wild cards" studied only after the core.
- `rule-over-ear` — Answering from explicit rule knowledge rather than what "sounds right." The ear works on familiar constructions but collapses when the test presents a rule in an unfamiliar wrapper; rule knowledge also buys speed and confidence (no second-guessing).
- `classify-before-solving` — The first act on any writing question is identifying WHICH rule is being tested, then ignoring everything in the passage irrelevant to that rule. Complicated-looking questions reduce to simple terms once classified.
- `time-banking-strategy` — Writing items, once classified, resolve in seconds; the generous per-question clock is for the reading items. Experts answer fast on grammar and reinvest the surplus in reading, and may deliberately answer out of document order (all writing first) since navigation is free.
- `module-one-accuracy-stakes` — Because the exam adapts between modules, early accuracy has outsized value; easy grammar points must not be leaked.
- `whole-passage-then-replug` — Read the full mini-passage, not just the sentence hosting the blank, and re-insert the chosen answer into the passage as a final check.
- `punctuation-equivalence-classes` — A compressed expert map of boundary punctuation: {period ≡ semicolon ≡ comma + coordinating conjunction} for joining full sentences; {colon ≡ single dash} for introducing an explanation/list, requiring a complete sentence before but not after; {pair of commas ≡ pair of dashes ≡ parentheses} for removable insertions, with no mixing of mark types across the pair.
- `comma-plus-pronoun-splice-flag` — A comma directly followed by a subject pronoun or "this/it/they" is a high-probability run-on signal (not absolute — flagged as "usually").
- `check-hard-stops-first` — When answer choices join or separate sentences multiple ways, evaluate the option with a period or colon before the subtler ones; hard boundaries are easier to verify.
- `nearest-noun-is-not-always-subject` — Agreement errors are engineered by inserting a differently-numbered noun between subject and verb; -s ending = singular verb is the quick morphological check.
- `agreement-masquerading-as-tense` — When choices vary both tense and number, number is usually the real test; an expert scans the choice set's axes of variation to identify the true target.
- `verb-consistency-default` — An underlined/blank verb should match the tense and form of surrounding verbs unless the passage gives an explicit reason to shift; nearby verbs are the evidence.
- `modifier-attachment-rule` — An opening descriptive phrase set off by a comma must be immediately followed by the noun it describes.
- `transition-relationship-first` and `goal-phrase-primacy` also appear in digest form here (fully developed in ch01/ch02 notes).

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Curriculum order = exam frequency order. The author explicitly tells time-poor students to study only the near-guaranteed types — a triage doctrine.
- The one-page digest is offered as the retrieval scaffold: each rule is compressed to a single decision-ready line with a pointer to the full chapter. Expert knowledge is stored as terse invariants, not prose.
- Deep grammar is taught only in service of simplification: the goal is to make questions *smaller*, not to make students grammarians.
- Concepts that are plausible-but-never-yet-tested are labeled as such — epistemic honesty about what is verified against released material.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. Scan the choice set to see what varies (punctuation? verb number? transition word?) → that identifies the tested rule.
2. Retrieve the one-line invariant for that rule.
3. Do the local check the invariant prescribes (find the true subject, find the previous sentence, test the boundary with a period…).
4. Re-insert the answer and confirm the passage reads coherently.
This is a classification-retrieval-verification loop; nothing is judged holistically by sound.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **ear-reliance** — belief that fluent readers can hear correctness. Formed because school English rarely requires explicit rule articulation and native fluency usually suffices in casual writing. Produces second-guessing and failure on deliberately unfamiliar-sounding correct answers.
- **time-abundance-complacency** — having over a minute per question is read as license to work slowly; students don't distinguish having time from needing it, and starve their reading questions.
- **more-text-means-harder** — students weight difficulty by passage length/jargon rather than by rule identity, and so over-invest in easy items.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim — verify against official material + our bank)

- Writing question types are stable enough that a majority of any form's grammar items come from a short list; one or two "wild card" items per form.
- Transition-logic and note-synthesis items appear on effectively every form (the near-guaranteed pair).
- Digital format ≈ >1 minute/question vs ~50s on the old paper test.
- The comma+pronoun splice cue is "usually but not always" reliable.

## Prerequisite edges implied

- recognizing a complete sentence (independent clause) → all boundary-punctuation rules
- identifying the true subject → agreement rules
- transition-function taxonomy (ch01) → conjunctive-adverb punctuation rules

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: ruthless frequency-based prioritization; compressing every rule to a one-line decision invariant; separating verified exam behavior from speculation; framing grammar study as speed/confidence investment rather than pedantry.
- Poorly: the digest presumes the chapters (not self-teaching); little diagnostic guidance (how to tell WHICH misconception a student has); no difficulty-ladder view of how a rule escalates within a form.
