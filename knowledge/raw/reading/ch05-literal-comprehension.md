# Raw notes — Literal Comprehension (source: Critical Reader 5th ed, ch. 5)

Maps to: Central Ideas & Details; Command of Evidence (Textual). Digital SAT R&W.

## Concepts taught

- **paraphrase-recognition** — The core tested ability: deciding whether a re-worded
  statement expresses the same idea as a sentence in the passage. Correct answers are
  synonym-level restatements, usually at a HIGHER level of generality than the passage's
  wording.
- **detail-lookup-questions** — Question family phrased as "according to / based on the
  text, what is true about X" or "why was X significant." Tests retrieval + rephrasing,
  not inference. The idea is stated; only the wording moves.
- **concrete-to-abstract-mapping** — Sub-skill of paraphrase recognition: connecting a
  passage's specific nouns/verbs (a named apparatus, a particular action) to the answer's
  abstract academic phrasing ("a novel approach," "expanded understanding"). The
  abstraction ladder runs one direction: answers generalize, passages specify.
- **whole-sentence-reading** — Reading discipline: the qualifying half of a sentence
  (often BEFORE the clause a student scans to) frequently contains the answer-deciding
  fact. Reading from a keyword forward loses the front half.
- **answer-location-signals** — Certain positions and marks flag where the tested idea
  lives: sentence directly before a colon, the final sentence of a paragraph, an
  emphasis adverb ("especially"-type intensifiers), explicit causal verbs.
- **positive-identification-over-full-decoding** — You do not need to parse every hard
  word or archaic construction; if one choice can be positively matched to a text idea,
  unknown vocabulary elsewhere becomes irrelevant.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Frames the whole question type as a matching game between two phrasings of one idea —
  deliberately deflates it ("most straightforward type") to reduce anxiety, then shows
  the two real difficulty sources: hard syntax/vocab in the passage, and abstraction in
  the answers.
- Teaches by worked example first, then a phrase-matching micro-drill (concrete phrase ↔
  abstract equivalent) BEFORE full questions — isolates the paraphrase skill from
  passage-reading load. Good sequencing idea worth stealing as a design pattern:
  train the equivalence skill on bare phrase pairs, then reintroduce context.
- Explanations always do two jobs: positively locate/justify the credited answer, then
  classify each wrong answer (contradicted / unsupported / off-topic / wrong scope).
- Repeats one behavioral rule across examples rather than many rules once.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. Read the question stem first; identify the entity/relationship being asked about.
2. Find where that entity is introduced or discussed — not merely where its name
   appears last; back up to the start of that sentence (or the sentence before).
3. Translate the relevant text into plain language BEFORE looking at choices —
   pre-phrasing immunizes against distractor wording.
4. Match the pre-phrase to a choice; verify the match is on the RELATIONSHIP, not just
   shared topic words.
5. If the passage is dense (old fiction, science jargon), don't decode everything —
   hunt the emphasis/causal markers and the sentence boundaries around the reference.
6. Once one choice is positively confirmed, stop; unknown words in other choices don't
   need resolving.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **echo-wording-bias** — Belief that the right answer will reuse the passage's words.
  Formed by years of school quizzes where literal word-match earns credit. The SAT
  inverts this: near-verbatim choices are frequently traps; the credited answer is the
  re-worded one. Produces picks of choices that recycle vivid passage phrases with a
  twisted relationship.
- **keyword-scan-forward** — Habit of scanning for the question's keyword and reading
  only from that word onward. Formed as a speed strategy. Fails when the deciding
  qualifier sits earlier in the sentence or in the preceding sentence; produces
  confident wrong answers that match the second half of a sentence.
- **must-decode-everything** — Belief that an unknown word (archaic adjective, technical
  term) blocks the question. Produces time loss and panic on fiction/science passages.
  In reality the credited answer can usually be verified from the parts that ARE
  readable.
- **detail-magnetism** — Over-focusing on interesting specifics instead of the claim
  structure, so the student can restate details but not the idea, and then can't
  recognize the idea generalized.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim — check against official material + bank)

- Literal-comprehension items appear ~1–2 per R&W module; a minor but reliable presence.
- Correct answers are systematically MORE GENERAL/abstract than the passage wording.
- Key information disproportionately sits: before a colon, at sentence starts, in final
  sentences, and adjacent to emphasis intensifiers or causal verbs.
- Distractor mix per item typically draws from: direct contradiction, unsupported
  comparison/superlative, off-topic attribute, right-detail-wrong-referent.

## Trap taxonomy extracted from exercise design

- **scope-inflation** — Passage supports "widely read"; choice claims "among the MOST
  widely read" or "more than any other." Exploits students who match topic + polarity
  but not degree. Superlatives/comparatives the text never makes.
- **contradiction-in-costume** — Choice states the opposite of the text using enough
  shared vocabulary to feel familiar (e.g., asserting a thing blocks flow when the text
  says it relies on flow).
- **right-fact-wrong-referent** — A true passage detail attached to the wrong subject:
  the benefit belonging to the acute mechanism credited to the chronic one; a detail
  about one listed example generalized to all examples. Exploits half-sentence reading
  and list-blurring.
- **near-synonym-shift** — Swaps a passage word for a non-equivalent neighbor
  ("distinct" → "unpredictable"; "detached" → "anxious"). Exploits vague vocabulary
  knowledge; the tutor should force students to test substitutability.
- **unasked-comparison** — Choice is textually true but answers a different relation
  than the stem (stem asks how A differs from B; choice names a shared or unrelated
  quality). Exploits stem-skimming.
- **off-topic-attribute** — Introduces a dimension the passage never discusses
  (intelligence, satisfaction). Easy to kill IF the student checks the passage rather
  than plausibility.

## Prerequisite edges implied

- academic-verb-lexicon (posit, bolster, conjecture, egalitarian…) → paraphrase-recognition
- whole-sentence-reading → detail-lookup-questions
- paraphrase-recognition → textual-evidence-questions (later chapters build on it)
- sentence-boundary awareness / pronoun-referent tracking → right-fact-wrong-referent immunity

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: isolates paraphrase as THE skill; phrase-pair drill design; consistent wrong-
  answer classification; the "you don't need every word" reassurance is high-value for
  fiction-averse students; explicit anti-scan discipline.
- Poorly: no timing guidance; no difficulty ladder (all examples feel mid-band); little
  on how this skill interacts with two-passage or data items; drill volume is small;
  doesn't name the trap categories explicitly (they must be reverse-engineered from
  answer explanations, as done above).
