# Raw notes — The Critical Reader 5th Ed, Ch. 7 (Text Completions)

Source: Meltzer, *The Critical Reader* 5th ed., ch. 7 (book pp. 121–132; scans p0122–p0133).
Maps to: R&W — Information & Ideas → Inferences ("logically completes the text").
Status: extraction only; all wording below is ours.

## Concepts taught

- **text-completion-anatomy** — A short paragraph builds a theory or argument and ends
  mid-sentence at a blank; the task is to pick the statement that the paragraph's own
  logic forces. One question shape, always the same stem.
- **inference-is-a-small-step** — The required "inference" is tiny and fully text-anchored:
  every needed premise is on the page, and the student's only job is to state explicitly
  the conclusion the passage left unstated. These are literal-comprehension questions
  wearing an inference costume.
- **predict-before-peeking** — Derive the completion yourself (from the premises) before
  reading any choices, then shop for the choice that matches your prediction. The choices
  are designed to confuse; the passage is not.
- **claim-compression** — Reduce the operative claim to a 3–6 word scratch-paper note,
  ideally with arrow/shorthand notation (X↑ → Y↓ style). Externalizing the logic prevents
  working-memory overload on multi-premise passages.
- **blank-proximity-principle** — The logically load-bearing sentence usually sits directly
  before the blank; earlier sentences are mostly background scaffolding. (Corollary: a
  transition word like "therefore," "yet," or "however" immediately before or near the
  blank marks the pivot the answer must respect.)
- **paraphrase-not-echo** — The correct answer nearly always restates the passage's key
  idea in different vocabulary; there is always specific passage wording it corresponds to,
  but rarely a word-for-word match. Students must separate "differently worded but same
  idea" (valid) from "genuinely new idea" (invalid).
- **entailment-toolkit** — A small set of licensed logical moves:
  - superlative complement: "most effective X" entails every other X is less effective;
  - restriction complement: "only when A" entails "not when not-A";
  - emphasis complement: "focuses largely on A" entails "focuses less on not-A";
  - percentage complement: "14% are X" entails "86% are not-X";
  - angle-flip via negation: "older than" may reappear as "not younger than";
  - double negatives resolve to positives ("not impossible" = possible) and deserve a
    deliberate slow-down.
- **inference-boundaries** — What may NOT be concluded: that a described method is the
  *only* or *best* one (usage ≠ effectiveness); anything about actors' motives unless
  stated; anything outside the stated time window or population; any recommendation
  ("should") from a purely descriptive passage.
- **reference-chain-backtracking** — Some completions can't be resolved from the final
  sentence alone because a pointer phrase ("this possibility," "on target") refers to an
  idea defined sentences earlier; the expert traces the pronoun/reference chain backward
  until the referent is concrete, then completes.
- **list-completion-variant** — When the blank is the last item of a parallel list, the
  hidden question is "what category do the listed items share?" The answer must be another
  instance of that category, not merely something topically nearby.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Method over instinct: a fixed 4-step routine (read the claim → restate it in your own
  words → work out the implication while staying glued to the text → find the matching
  choice) presented as a flexible roadmap, not a ritual.
- Writing things down is treated as a score lever, not a crutch — even strong readers are
  told to work the argument out by hand to prevent avoidable losses.
- Reassurance against "too obvious": if the predicted completion feels trivially obvious,
  that's by design; students are warned off second-guessing an obvious-but-correct chain.
- Confidence rule: when the prediction matches one choice overwhelmingly, checking the
  rest is optional; elimination is a fallback tool, not the primary method.
- The SAT framed as a reasoning test: careful stepwise thinking is presented as reliably
  sufficient — no outside knowledge, no genius leap.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. Locate the claim under completion — usually the sentence(s) touching the blank; ignore
   decorative background until it's needed.
2. Compress the claim into shorthand, converting qualitative statements into directional
   relationships between variables where possible.
3. Apply one or two licensed entailment moves (complements, negation flips) to produce a
   predicted completion — before looking at choices.
4. Scan choices for a semantic match, expecting synonym-level rewording; verify the match
   preserves direction, scope, and subject.
5. If no confident match, eliminate by class: contradicts the passage / off-topic beyond
   passage bounds / plausible-but-unestablished speculation / right pieces glued into an
   irrelevant claim.
6. For dense passages, translate hard vocabulary from context first (a term can be decoded
   from a stated statistic or contrast), then reason on the translated version.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **inference-means-leap** — Students trained on open-ended English-class inference expect
  to add ideas of their own; they pick imaginative choices and reject the "boring" literal
  one. The test rewards the smallest possible step; anything creative is out of bounds.
- **echo-seeking** — Students match surface vocabulary rather than meaning, rejecting
  correct answers that rephrase and favoring wrong answers that recycle passage words.
  Formed by years of find-the-quote reading homework.
- **real-world-truth-substitution** — A choice that is true in life (or merely sensible)
  feels supported; students conflate plausibility with textual establishment. Trap writers
  exploit this with choices that are factually reasonable but never grounded in the text.
- **entity-mention-halo** — Choices that name people/things from the passage feel "on
  topic" and safe; students under time pressure use mention as a proxy for relevance.
- **descriptive-to-normative slide** — Students accept "should"-flavored completions after
  purely descriptive passages, importing an implied recommendation the text never makes.
- **distractor-fact absorption** — A vivid but argumentatively idle detail (an associated
  disease, a famous name) gets pulled into the completion; students don't separate the
  argument's spine from its garnish.

## Trap taxonomy observed in item design (from worked examples + exercise set)

- Direct contradiction of the passage's main mechanism (catches misreaders).
- Speculation: could-be-true extension with no textual warrant (funding requests, motives,
  future behavior).
- Half-right recombination: two true passage facts fused into a statement that sounds
  profound but is logically inert relative to the blank.
- Scope narrowing/widening: answer about one region/case when the logic requires many, or
  vice versa.
- Category miss in list completions: an item that is on-theme but not an instance of the
  list's shared property.
- Mechanism swap: attributes the effect to a related-but-different condition mentioned
  elsewhere in the passage.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim)

## Prerequisite edges implied

- claim-identification (finding the argument's spine) → text completions
- paraphrase-recognition (same idea, different words) → text completions
- transition-word logic (therefore/however/yet as pivot markers) → text completions
- vocab-from-context decoding → hard text completions
- text-completion mastery → support/undermine questions (next chapter builds on it
  explicitly: same family, one step further)

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the predict-first discipline; the scratch-paper compression notation with
  directional arrows; the explicit entailment toolkit (complements of "only/most/largely,"
  negation flips); naming the boundaries of valid inference (usage ≠ effectiveness, no
  motive inference); a usable wrong-answer taxonomy including the subtle
  "speculation" and "recombined-facts" classes.
- Poorly: no timing guidance beyond "careful is worth it"; no difficulty ladder (all
  treatment is one band); nothing on completions attached to data/graphics; the
  positioning-in-module claims aren't adapted to the adaptive digital format; only ~6
  practice items, all science/social-science flavored — no humanities completions.
