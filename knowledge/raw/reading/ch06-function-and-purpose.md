# Raw notes — Function & Purpose (source: Critical Reader 5th ed, ch. 6)

Maps to: Text Structure & Purpose (Craft & Structure). Digital SAT R&W.

## Concepts taught

- **literal-vs-function-distinction** — The chapter's spine: comprehension questions ask
  WHAT a sentence says; function questions ask WHY it's there — what job it performs for
  the surrounding argument. Understanding content is necessary but NOT sufficient;
  function is a second layer computed over content.
- **rhetorical-role-identification** — Assigning a sentence/phrase a job: introduce,
  illustrate, explain, define, compare, contrast, qualify, concede, emphasize, conclude,
  speculate. The job is signaled by connective words and punctuation more than by topic.
- **transition-signal-reading** — Connectives partition into idea-continuers (addition,
  example, explanation, definition, comparison, speculation, emphasis, conclusion,
  qualification) and idea-reversers (contrast words, skeptical questions, scare quotes).
  A reverser at a sentence head announces "new idea, likely the important one."
- **punctuation-as-function-marker** — Colon/dash = an explanation or definition
  follows; paired dashes/parentheses = an aside that qualifies or hedges; italics,
  capitals, exclamation, repetition = emphasis. Punctuation is load-bearing evidence,
  not decoration.
- **context-window-discipline** — The referenced lines only locate the material; the
  point they serve usually sits BEFORE them (sometimes after). Default move: read a
  sentence or two on each side. If the reference is near the passage top, restart from
  sentence one.
- **evidence-points-upward** — Mid-paragraph material is usually support; the claim it
  supports lives at the paragraph's start or end. Function questions are therefore
  solved by working backwards from example to the idea it serves.
- **main-point-vs-rhetorical-goal** — Point = the argument being made (a claim you could
  dispute). Purpose = the rhetorical act performed (an infinitive verb: to explain, to
  describe, to challenge). Purpose answers often re-express the point one abstraction
  level up, so students must map specific nouns to general categories.
- **first-sentence-lever** — For whole-passage purpose questions, the opening sentence
  usually telegraphs the answer; confirm with a skim rather than a reread ("trust, then
  verify" posture).
- **polarity-screening** — Match answer valence to passage tone before reading choices
  closely: positive-toned passages take positive/neutral answers; negatives get
  eliminated wholesale, and vice versa. A coarse filter that buys time for fine
  distinctions.
- **extreme-verb-skepticism** — Answers claiming a passage definitively proves,
  demolishes, or ridicules something are usually wrong: evidence-weighing prose (the
  test's house style for science/social science) doesn't do those things.
- **second-meaning-bonus** — A choice using a familiar word in its less-common sense
  (e.g., "novel" as adjective, "qualify" as hedge) is disproportionately likely to be
  credited, because second meanings are themselves a tested skill.
- **rhetorical-verb-lexicon** — Precise meanings of the function verbs answer choices
  are built from (qualify, concede, bolster, undermine, evoke, satirize, minimize…).
  Without this vocabulary the student can't even parse the choices.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Opens with a failure-empathy vignette (the "answers seem unrelated to the lines"
  experience) and diagnoses it: the student answered WHAT when the test asked WHY.
  Strong hook pattern — name the felt confusion, then reveal the category error.
- Converts the abstract question into student-language before touching choices
  ("why does the author bring this up?" / "what point does this support?"). Rephrasing
  the stem is taught as an explicit step.
- Heavy use of reference tables (signal words by function; answer verbs by polarity,
  with usually-wrong extremes flagged; a glossary of rhetorical verbs). The tables act
  as a lookup layer under the reasoning process.
- Worked examples deliberately include one where the initial hypothesis fails and the
  solver must fall back to reading further — models recovery, not just clean solves.
- Explicit meta-advice against answer-choice superstition (picking the "statistically
  common" letter when stuck).

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. Classify the question: local function (a phrase/sentence's job) vs whole-passage
   purpose. Rephrase it plainly: "why is this here?"
2. Read the referenced lines for content, then widen: sentence before, sentence after;
   if near the top, start from sentence one.
3. Inventory signals: head-of-sentence connective, colon/dash structures, hedging
   parentheticals, emphasis marks, attitude-bearing word choices.
4. Determine what claim the material serves — usually the nearest preceding general
   statement; state the function in your own words as verb + object ("expands the
   contrast," "explains the mechanism").
5. Polarity-screen the choices; discard tone mismatches and extreme verbs.
6. Match remaining choices to the pre-phrased function; verify EVERY word — one wrong
   noun ("differences" where the text draws a likeness) invalidates an otherwise
   attractive choice.
7. For purpose-of-passage: derive the main point first, then lift it one abstraction
   level into an infinitive-verb form; expect the credited answer to generalize the
   point's specifics.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **function-equals-content** — Students answer "what do the lines say" because every
  prior reading assessment rewarded restatement. Produces attraction to choices that
  accurately summarize the quoted lines but mis-state their role. The single most
  important misconception in this domain.
- **answer-lives-inside-the-lines** — Belief that the quoted range contains everything
  needed. Formed from line-reference mechanics ("they told me where to look").
  Test writers exploit it by placing the served claim before the reference and building
  a distractor from content just after it.
- **purpose-equals-restated-point** — On primary-purpose questions students hunt for
  their main-point sentence verbatim and panic when no choice matches, because purpose
  choices are cast as rhetorical verbs + generalized objects. Root cause: no schooling
  distinguishes argument from rhetorical act.
- **proximity-association** — Assuming two things mentioned near each other stand in the
  asserted relationship (items located IN a place vs items belonging to the whole
  system). Exploited by choices that fuse adjacent sentences into a false claim.
- **letter-frequency-superstition** — Guessing by remembered answer-letter statistics
  when stuck; a coping ritual that substitutes for the missing WHY-analysis.
- **local-literal-reading-of-analogies** — Treating an analogy/comparison as the topic
  itself ("compares offices to theater") rather than as a device serving an explanation.
  Students hold it because the analogy is the most vivid, memorable content on the page.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim — verify vs official items + our bank)

- Function stems: "primary purpose of the passage," "function of the underlined
  sentence/portion," "the portion serves to…"; both local and whole-passage variants
  appear regularly in every module.
- Questions cluster at idea-contact points: where new information enters or focus
  pivots — i.e., at transitions.
- The claim a referenced detail supports is more often located BEFORE the reference
  than after.
- Distractors are engineered for students who start reading AT the line reference
  (built from material just after it).
- Extreme positive/negative answer verbs (prove-type, condemn-type, mock-type) are
  usually wrong; neutral describe/indicate/introduce verbs dominate credited answers.
- Familiar-word-second-meaning choices skew correct.
- First sentences reliably encode whole-passage purpose, especially in short passages.

## Trap taxonomy extracted from exercise design

- **after-the-reference-decoy** — Choice built from content immediately following the
  underlined lines; punishes forward-only reading. Fix: context-window-discipline.
- **one-wrong-word** — Choice matches the passage's move except a single relational
  noun/verb inverted (difference↔similarity, decision↔realization). Exploits gist-level
  matching; forces word-by-word verification.
- **scope-twist-recycled-word** — Lifts a distinctive passage word and wraps it in a
  broader claim than the text makes (a single described instance → "a tendency").
  Familiarity of the recycled word sells the inflated scope.
- **too-extreme** — Overstates certainty or negativity ("cannot be attained," dangers
  never discussed). Exploits students who match direction but not degree.
- **vivid-vehicle-trap** — Names the analogy's vehicle as the purpose (the comparison
  itself) instead of the explanatory job the analogy performs. Exploits
  local-literal-reading-of-analogies.
- **wrong-altitude** — Describes a real but subordinate detail's job as the whole
  passage's purpose, or vice versa. Tests point-vs-purpose layering.

## Prerequisite edges implied

- literal-comprehension (ch05 paraphrase skill) → function questions (must know WHAT
  before WHY; function answers are also paraphrases, one level more abstract)
- transition-signal-reading + punctuation-as-function-marker → rhetorical-role-identification
- rhetorical-verb-lexicon → parsing/eliminating function answer choices
- main-point-identification → primary-purpose questions
- tone/attitude detection → polarity-screening

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the WHY-vs-WHAT reframe is crisp and diagnostic; signal-word and function-verb
  tables give a concrete lookup scaffold; polarity screening and extreme-verb heuristics
  are cheap, high-yield filters; models hypothesis-failure recovery in a worked example;
  glossary treats rhetorical verbs as vocabulary to be taught, not assumed.
- Poorly: heuristics stack up without a priority order (novice may not know which filter
  to run first); frequency/probability claims are asserted, never quantified; poetry and
  drama get thin treatment relative to science prose; no misconception-labeled feedback
  design — wrong-answer psychology must be inferred; cross-references to other chapters
  ("illustrate" questions) assume book-order study.

## Cross-cutting note for synthesis

Ch5 and ch6 are one ladder: ch5 trains same-idea recognition at matched abstraction;
ch6 reuses that machinery but asks the student to also compute the RELATION between a
statement and its neighbors. A graph node design should make paraphrase-recognition a
prereq of rhetorical-role-identification, with the shared enemy being forward-only,
keyword-anchored reading.
