# Raw notes — The Critical Reader 5th Ed, Ch. 3 (inference/abstraction foundations)

Source: SAT Reading reference book, chapter on moving between concrete wording and
abstract restatement (scans p0062–p0073). Provenance only — everything below is our
own synthesis. Maps to digital SAT R&W: **Information & Ideas → Inferences** (primary),
and as an enabling layer under Central Ideas & Details and Text Structure & Purpose.

## Concepts taught

- `concrete-abstract-shuttling` — The meta-skill of translating between a passage's
  specific wording and a more general restatement of the same idea, and back. Rarely
  the literal question being asked, but the bottleneck skill under inference,
  main-idea, and support/undermine questions.
- `pronoun-referent-tracking` — Resolving what each pronoun (it, they, this, that)
  points to, treating the pronoun as a thread that carries an idea forward through
  the text. Losing the thread = losing the argument.
- `idea-nouns` — (source coins its own label for these; our name is "idea-nouns")
  Nouns that name ideas rather than objects: claim, assumption, phenomenon,
  divergence, mismatch, conclusion. One such noun can stand in for an entire prior
  sentence or multi-sentence chunk — it packs a proposition into a single phrase.
- `referent-is-a-proposition` — The thing an idea-noun points to is often a full
  clause or a whole stated relationship, not a nearby single noun. Correctly
  "unpacking" it means restating the earlier idea, not naming an object.
- `read-backward-to-resolve` — Decision rule: the information a pronoun or idea-noun
  refers to almost always sits BEFORE it in the text (occasionally after). To resolve
  one, back up to the start of the sentence containing the earlier idea — never
  begin reading mid-sentence, and never stare at the phrase in isolation.
- `number-agreement-cue` — Grammatical number as a decoding aid: a plural pronoun
  must have a plural referent, singular must have singular. Cheap first filter when
  multiple candidate referents exist.
- `shifting-referents` — The same pronoun can switch referents from sentence to
  sentence (and a pronoun can even precede its noun within a passage). Each
  occurrence must be resolved independently; carrying the old referent forward is
  an error.
- `positional-pair-reference` — "The former / the latter" convention: first-mentioned
  vs second-mentioned of a two-item list. The pair can name processes or categories,
  not just people; the follow-up sentence may mention the second item first, so
  order of mention in the follow-up is NOT the resolver — order in the original
  list is.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Teaches an *invisible* prerequisite before any question type: the author argues the
  exam never asks this directly, yet every hard passage requires it. Sequencing
  choice: decoding machinery first, question taxonomy after.
- Motivates the existence of pronouns/idea-nouns by contrast: shows how unbearably
  repetitive prose becomes when every reference is spelled out as a full noun phrase.
  Students see WHY writers compress, so compression stops feeling adversarial.
- Escalates deliberately: single obvious pronoun → pronoun in a different sentence
  than its referent → same pronoun with changing referents → plural agreement →
  idea-nouns pointing at whole propositions → former/latter → poetry and 19th-century
  fiction where the referent is stylistically distant.
- The exercise set strips away multiple choice entirely: student marks, inside the
  passage, exactly what each highlighted pronoun/idea-noun points to. Isolates the
  decoding subskill from answer-elimination skill. Good model for our interactive
  checks (tap-the-referent widget).
- Draws material from the full genre range the digital exam uses (science abstracts,
  social-science, classic fiction, poetry) so the skill generalizes.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. On meeting a pronoun or idea-noun that matters to a question: stop, don't reread
   the phrase itself. Jump back to the previous sentence boundary and read forward
   from there.
2. Use grammatical number to shortlist candidate referents, then confirm the
   survivor by sense ("would this noun logically do what the sentence says?").
3. For idea-nouns, expect the answer to be a proposition: mentally restate the prior
   sentence's claim and check that the noun is a fair one-word compression of it.
4. For former/latter, physically locate the two-item list and index into it by
   original order, ignoring the order of the follow-up sentence.
5. Treat every new occurrence of a pronoun as unresolved until checked — no
   referent inertia.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **noun-means-object** — Elementary-school "person, place, or thing" schema makes
  students parse idea-nouns as if they named things nearby in the text. They hunt
  for a physical antecedent, fail, and conclude the passage is unreadable. Produces
  wrong answers on any question whose stem or choices hinge on the compressed idea.
- **reread-in-place-loop** — When confused by a phrase, students reread the phrase
  itself repeatedly, or read forward from it, because backing up feels like wasted
  time. The referent is behind them, so the loop never terminates; cost is both
  accuracy and pacing. (Source explicitly describes this loop as the signature
  failure behavior.)
- **referent-inertia** — Belief that one pronoun keeps one referent for the whole
  passage. Formed because easy texts behave that way. The exam exploits it by
  switching a pronoun's referent mid-description.
- **nearest-noun-grab** — Resolving a pronoun to the closest preceding noun without
  checking number or sense. Plausible heuristic (usually works in casual reading)
  that fails exactly where the test puts distance or an intervening noun between
  anaphor and referent.
- **follow-up-order-confusion** — Reading "the latter" as "whichever item was
  mentioned most recently," which breaks when a writer discusses the latter first.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim — check against official items + our bank)

- unverified-claim: Referent information nearly always precedes the anaphor; rare
  cases where it follows.
- unverified-claim: Passages sometimes place a possessive/pronoun BEFORE its noun
  within a two-sentence span.
- unverified-claim: Science/social-science passages routinely hinge a key finding on
  one idea-noun phrase (a compressed restatement of the finding), and a question
  will target the compressed idea rather than the surface detail.
- unverified-claim: Former/latter constructions appear with idea/process pairs and
  occasionally invert presentation order in the follow-up sentence.

## Prerequisite edges implied

- `pronoun-referent-tracking` → `idea-nouns` (single-word anaphora before
  proposition-level anaphora)
- `idea-nouns` → `rw-central-ideas` (can't find the main claim if you can't unpack
  its compressed restatements)
- `idea-nouns` → `rw-inferences` (inference stems often quiz exactly the unpacked
  proposition)
- `number-agreement-cue` ← basic grammar (subject/verb + noun number) from the
  grammar graph
- `concrete-abstract-shuttling` → support/undermine + text-completion reasoning
  (choosing evidence requires matching a general claim to specific data)

## What this source does well / poorly

Well: names an unexamined bottleneck skill and drills it in isolation; contrast-based
motivation (verbose vs compressed prose) builds sympathy for why texts compress;
non-MCQ exercise format is ideal for diagnosing the subskill; genre spread includes
poetry/older fiction, matching the digital exam's range.

Poorly: pure prose exposition with no intermediate self-checks until the end
exercise; no timing guidance (how much backing-up is affordable per question); the
"always back up a full sentence" rule is stated more absolutely than the exam
warrants; no bridge shown from this drill to actual scored question types — we
should build that bridge explicitly (e.g., show an Inferences item whose distractors
each encode a mis-resolved referent).

## Trap taxonomy extractable from the exercise design (no items retained)

- Distance trap: several lines and a sentence boundary between anaphor and referent.
- Decoy-noun trap: an intervening noun of matching number sits closer than the true
  referent.
- Shift trap: same pronoun used twice with different referents inside one
  description.
- Proposition trap: the "answer" is a whole prior clause; any single noun a student
  underlines is wrong.
- Poetic-syntax trap: inverted or archaic syntax hides the referent (poetry/older
  fiction), punishing students who only practice on modern nonfiction.
