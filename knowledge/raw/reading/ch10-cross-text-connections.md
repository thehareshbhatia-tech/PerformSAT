# Raw notes — Meltzer, The Critical Reader 5th Ed, Ch. 10 (Paired Passages)

## Concepts taught

- **two-voice-separation** — The core skill: hold each author's position as a distinct labeled slot ("A1 thinks X; A2 thinks Y") instead of one blended impression of "the topic." Every question in this family tests whether you kept the voices apart.
- **relationship-first-solving** — Derive the relationship between the texts (full disagreement / partial agreement with a caveat / same phenomenon, different explanation) BEFORE reading the choices. The question always probes that relationship, typically as "how would Author 2 respond to a specific element of Text 1," so pre-computing it answers the question in advance.
- **minimal-working-memory-protocol** — Fixed pipeline: (1) main point of Text 1, (2) main point of Text 2, (3) state the relationship in a few words, (4) predict the answer in your own words, (5) match to the closest choice. Rationale: dense unfamiliar texts overflow working memory; doing the argument work up front means the choices are checked against a compact summary, not re-derived per choice.
- **tone-polarity-prefiltering** — If the authors disagree, the response the question asks for must be critical in tone; positive/neutral-toned choices die on their opening words. Partial-agreement relationships predict concession-plus-caveat phrasing ("accepts X but warns about Y").
- **agreement-zone-vs-dispute-zone mapping** — Authors can agree on part of the topic and split on another; correct answers may live in either zone or straddle both. Students must mark WHERE the overlap ends, not just "they disagree."
- **detail-attribution-discipline** — Track which text a detail came from. A standard distractor quotes a real detail from the WRONG text (usually Text 1) as if the other author had said it. Re-checking the source text before accepting a detail-bearing choice is mandatory.
- **structural-prior-guessing** — Test-construction knowledge as tiebreaker: disagreement is the modal relationship; extreme quantifiers ("only," "never") usually disqualify; when theory and evidence conflict in these items, the designed answer is usually "appealing theory, unsupported in practice" rather than the reverse. Explicitly framed as a prior for educated guessing, then verified in-text.
- **strategic-deferral** — Students not chasing top scores who find dual texts disproportionately costly should skip the pair on first pass and return after banking easier points.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Load management over comprehension depth: the chapter's organizing worry is cognitive overload, and every technique reduces how much text must be "live" at once.
- Teaches the meta-game openly: a full worked example uses ONLY the question and choices (no passages shown) to rank options by tone polarity, extremeness, and theory/practice asymmetry — teaching students the test's own construction biases before teaching careful reading.
- Answer-prediction before choice-reading is the through-line (shared with the book's other chapters): choices are treated as a lineup to match against, never as a menu to reason from.
- Worked answers are structured as a fixed catechism — position 1, position 2, "logically, how would A2 respond?" — modeling the same three moves on every item regardless of topic.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. Read Text 1 for its single main point; compress to a clause. Same for Text 2. Ignore supporting detail on first pass.
2. Name the relationship explicitly (disagree / agree-partly-then-diverge / second reframes first). Note the hinge: the specific sub-question they split on.
3. Re-read the question stem to find WHICH element of the other text the response targets (a named researcher's belief, a quoted phrase, a specific example). If the stem quotes a phrase, locate that phrase in the text first — it pins the referent.
4. Predict the response in your own words BEFORE the choices: a disagreeing author responds negatively; a both-sides author responds to a one-sided example by supplying the missing side; an author whose method centers a specific measurement responds to a rival account by pointing at what that account omits.
5. Sweep choices by opening tone words; eliminate wrong-polarity and extreme-quantifier options cheaply.
6. For each survivor carrying a concrete detail, verify the detail's source text and scope. Reject choices about topics neither text addresses, and choices attributing to Author 2 a stance found only in Text 1.
7. If still tied, return to the text for the narrow confirming fact; never eliminate an uncertain choice without that check.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **blended-voices** — Students merge both texts into one "what the passage says" summary because all prior schooling treats a reading as a single source of truth. Produces answers that attribute Text 1 material to Text 2's author (the misattribution distractor exists precisely for them).
- **disagreement-means-total-opposition** — Students assume disagreeing authors reject everything the other says; they then eliminate concession-shaped correct answers ("grants the benefit, warns about the excess") for not being negative enough. Formed from debate-style framing of "disagreement."
- **choices-as-starting-point** — Overloaded students dive into the choices hoping recognition will substitute for comprehension; each choice then re-triggers a full re-read, quadrupling the load. The predict-first protocol exists to break this loop.
- **memory-overconfidence** — Students answer detail-hinging questions from recall of a first read; the pivotal detail is often NOT in a privileged position (not first/last sentence), so recall silently fails. Fix taught: always return to the text before finalizing.
- **premature-elimination-under-uncertainty** — Students discard choices they merely feel unsure about; on this question type the correct answer often initially looks uncertain because it hinges on the overlooked detail.
- **plausible-real-world-response** — Students pick what a reasonable person might say (e.g., a common-sense critique) rather than what THIS author's stated position licenses. The scope-trap distractors are built for exactly this.

## Trap taxonomy extracted from the exercise set (design only, no items)

1. **Cross-text misattribution**: a choice paraphrases a real detail from Text 1 and offers it as Text 2's author's view. Works because the detail feels familiar; only source-checking kills it.
2. **Polarity flip**: positive-toned response choice when the relationship is adversarial (or vice versa). Exploits students who never named the relationship.
3. **Concession-blindness bait**: alongside a correct grants-then-cautions answer sit purely hostile choices; students holding disagreement-means-total-opposition pick the hostile one.
4. **Scope escape**: choice raises an adjacent issue neither author discusses (motives of an industry, preferences for a different genre). Feels sophisticated; unanchored in either text.
5. **Half-right nuance**: choice matches the author's domain but overextends it (author discussed one outcome dimension; choice generalizes to life satisfaction at large).
6. **Missing-measurement pivot** (dueling-explanations pairs): when Author 2's account revolves around one diagnostic quantity that Author 1 never mentions, the correct response is "the rival account overlooks that quantity"; distractors mis-assign which author relies on which evidence, or negate a fact the text affirms.
7. **Nuanced-author inversion**: when one author holds a both-cooperation-and-competition view, distractors offer the pure single-organism reading that author explicitly rejects.

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the five-step protocol is genuinely load-reducing and directly teachable; the misattribution trap gets an explicit, memorable warning; the open discussion of test-construction priors (polarity, extremeness, theory-vs-practice asymmetry) is honest about guessing strategy while still requiring verification; exercise answers model the identical catechism every time, which builds a transferable habit.
- Poorly: leans heavily on "authors disagree" as a prior, with thin treatment of same-side pairs (complementary findings, methodological refinement) that the digital SAT also runs; no vocabulary for relationship TYPES beyond agree/disagree (no reframing, narrowing, application taxonomy); the passages-free meta-gaming example, while clever, risks teaching shortcut-first habits to weak readers; strategic-deferral advice lacks concrete timing thresholds; only four exercise items, all science/social-science — no humanities pairs.
