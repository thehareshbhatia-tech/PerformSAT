## Section-format beliefs asserted (unverified-claim — verify against Bluebook/official specs)

- unverified-claim: R&W = 2 adaptive modules × 27 questions × 30 min (54 q / 60 min); Module 1 performance sets Module 2 difficulty.
- unverified-claim: Within each module, roughly the first ~15 items are reading-skill items, the remainder writing/grammar — the two halves are blocked, not interleaved. (Cross-check: our PT4–11 flow memory says vocab first → conventions later, consistent.)
- unverified-claim: One tiny passage (~50–150 words) per question; only paired-text items carry two mini-texts.
- unverified-claim: Each module's reading run opens with vocab fill-ins, then climbs to heavier reasoning types.
- unverified-claim: Passage subject pool = fiction/poetry (rarely drama), humanities, social science, natural science; graphs/tables ride mostly on science and some social-science passages, and sometimes the graphic is decorative for the actual question.
- unverified-claim: No wrong-answer penalty; skipping/flagging is free within a module; nothing may be left blank rationally.
- unverified-claim: Types that tend to need the most chained reasoning: completion, support/undermine, graph items. Literary language is the difficulty driver for fiction/poetry rather than logic depth.

## Concepts taught

- `rw-argument-comprehension` — The section's true construct: not "did you absorb the text" but "can you see the text as an argument" — locate claims, see how evidence supports them, judge what would follow from/strengthen/weaken them. Literal comprehension is the floor, not the target.
- `rw-rhetorical-role-reading` — Reading each sentence with two questions: what does it SAY, and what JOB does it do in the argument (claim, support, concession, pivot, illustration). The job, not the content, answers function/structure questions.
- `rw-reading-is-learnable` — Framing belief: argument-reading is a trainable procedure, not fixed verbal talent. (Motivation-critical for low scorers; also true in our experience.)
- `rw-question-first-reading` — Read the stem BEFORE the passage. The stem tells you what to attend to and — equally valuable — what to ignore, preventing time-loss loops on irrelevant confusing bits.
- `rw-attention-by-question-type` — Attention allocation is type-conditional: whole-text questions → front-load attention (short passages must state their point early; first/last sentences carry disproportionate weight); local-function questions → skim opening for context, then focus the target sentence plus its immediate neighbors.
- `rw-emphasis-signal-scan` — The author's own emphasis machinery is a treasure map: goal/claim/finding language, importance words and their synonyms, italics, contrast transitions, colons/dashes, absolute terms, causal/explanatory words. The tested content usually sits adjacent to one of these signals.
- `rw-answer-in-own-words-first` — Formulate a rough answer (a few words, possibly jotted) BEFORE looking at choices; then match. Skippable on easy literal items; highest value on completion, support/undermine, and paired-text items where distractors are engineered to sound plausible.
- `rw-supported-not-stated` — The needed INFORMATION is always in the passage, but the correct ANSWER is usually a paraphrase of it — same idea, different words. The linking act (specific wording ↔ general restatement) IS the tested skill. Corollary: a choice that echoes passage wording verbatim deserves suspicion, not comfort.
- `rw-distractor-taxonomy` — Wrong answers are wrong for a specific, passage-checkable reason and cluster into knowable species (below).
- `rw-elimination-protocol` — Eliminate fast on clear wrongness only; never eliminate for "I don't understand this choice" (confusion ≠ wrong). Down to two: return to the text and verify a specific claim of one choice — an active check, not a stare-down.
- `rw-verbatim-check-anchors` — When verifying a finalist choice: hunt for a synonym of a passage keyword (presence = good sign); test one concrete detail of the choice against the referenced lines; scope-check nouns (passage about one person, choice about a class of people = overreach); more clauses in a choice = more ways to be wrong.
- `rw-guess-and-move` — Unresolvable after honest work = flag, pick a consistent fallback letter, move on. Return only if time remains.
- `rw-literalism-discipline` — Take the author at face value; interpretation beyond the text is a liability. School English rewards imaginative reading; this test punishes it. Summarize claims in language as close to the text's own meaning as possible.
- `rw-outside-knowledge-role` — Background knowledge helps comprehension and can pre-sort likely answers, but support-in-passage is the only decision criterion; also common sense legitimately kills real-world-absurd choices.
- `rw-two-authors-model` — Every item has two minds behind it: the passage author and the test writers. Experts model the TEST WRITERS' notion of correct (predictable answer aesthetics: cautious, passage-bounded, moderate) and suppress personal opinion entirely.
- `rw-patterns-not-laws` — Heuristics like "extreme wording is wrong" are priors, not rules; the test occasionally breaks them, and 750+ scorers must let the passage overrule the prior.
- `rw-every-word-binds` — One unsupported word sinks an otherwise perfect choice; grading a choice means grading its weakest word (e.g., wrong-direction tone verbs like doubt-flavored words when the author is enthusiastic).
- `rw-importance-filter` — Passages contain deliberately unimportant material; skilled reading = ranking importance and working around confusing patches using what IS understood, not decoding everything.
- `rw-adaptive-stakes-m1` — Because Module 1 gates Module 2 difficulty (and thus the score ceiling), careless early errors are disproportionately costly; warm up before the test so the first module isn't the warm-up.
- `rw-screen-reading-discipline` — Screens prime a skim-and-scroll mental state; digital-test reading must be deliberately slowed. Skim only by DESIGN (based on where value lives), never by drift. Practice on-screen; use finger-on-screen/highlighting as focus anchors if needed.
- `rw-triage-ordering` — Any within-module order is allowed; answer your reliable types first to bank energy and confidence, and know your personal easy/hard map BEFORE test day. Strong flexible readers may just go in order — strategy overhead isn't free.
- `rw-strategic-sacrifice` — Below-perfect goals: intentionally punting a couple of hardest items to fund care elsewhere can net more points than attempting everything rushed. Score goals rarely require perfection.
- `rw-fatigue-budgeting` — R&W is ~an hour of continuous short-passage reading with Math after; efficiency is partly ENERGY management (avoid reread loops, avoid overthinking easy items, keep moving).
- `rw-scratch-work-reading` — Writing 2–4 word notes (your pre-answer, what you know when stuck between two) offloads working memory exactly as scratch work does in math; top scorers treat it as part of the method, weaker scorers as beneath them.
- `rw-stuck-between-two-diagnostic` — Chronically reaching two finalists and picking the wrong one is usually a comprehension deficit (missed the passage's point), not a tie-breaking-technique deficit. Remediation is upstream: vocabulary log, more exposure to complex syntax, key-place reading — not more elimination tricks.
- `rw-iterative-hypothesis-reading` — Working an item = making a structural assumption (e.g., "point is probably near the end"), testing it, and consciously revising when it fails, rather than rereading the same lines in a loop. Trial-and-error is legitimate BECAUSE the test is standardized.
- `rw-answer-mindset-flexibility` — The right answer may be phrased in an unexpected direction; "not what I expected" ≠ wrong. Revise your expectation instead of fighting the test; post-mortems should ask "what did I misread/overextend," not "why my answer deserved credit."

## Distractor species taxonomy (for our misconception/trap index)

1. Off-topic — about something the referenced lines never address.
2. Scope inflation — generalizes a single case to a class (one researcher → researchers; one study → the field).
3. Extremity — absolute/limiting language stronger than the passage commits to (never/always/only-flavored claims). Prior, not law — occasionally correct at the top end.
4. Half-right hybrid — familiar correct-sounding pieces welded to one false or unsupported element; defeated only by whole-choice verification (`rw-every-word-binds`).
5. Plausible-but-unsupported — could be true in the world, consistent with the vibe, but the passage never establishes it. The single most common finalist-trap.
6. Right-idea-wrong-scope-of-lines — true of the passage overall but not of the specific lines asked about (or vice versa).
7. Fact-true-text-absent — factually accurate in reality; absent from the passage. Exploits students told to "use the passage" who verify against memory of the world instead.
8. Verbatim-echo lure — recycles exact passage wording to borrow trust while distorting meaning. Exploits familiarity heuristic. (Complement: correct answers are usually synonym-paraphrases.)

Claimed final-two dynamics (unverified-claim): most stuck-between-two situations pit a half-right hybrid against a plausible-but-unsupported choice; the winner is decided by going back and checking a specific concrete detail.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Demystify format first (module structure, adaptivity, no-penalty) so anxiety doesn't masquerade as skill deficit.
- One flexible master procedure (read stem slowly → targeted return to text with context margin → own-words answer → ordered choice reading → elimination → active verification → guess-and-move) rather than per-type scripts; per-type chapters then specialize it.
- Multiple entry points on purpose: author explicitly avoids one-true-method dogma; the skill ceiling is knowing WHICH approach fits the item (flexibility as the top-scorer trait).
- Honesty about cost: the careful process is exhausting and unpleasant at first; the author leans on personal narrative (rushed instinct-reading vs. exhaustive step-writing) to sell that meticulousness, not talent, moved the outcome. Speed is presented as the RESULT of process repetition.
- Mindset woven through mechanics: ego-abandonment ("the test's judgment is final; model it, don't argue"), controllables focus, permission to be imperfect at sub-1600 goals.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

- Attention is allocated top-down from the question, not bottom-up from the text; experts read LESS total text than novices but the right slices with margin (a sentence or two around any referenced span — half-sentences actively dangerous because the pivot may live in the other half).
- When a referenced span doesn't contain the answer, experts widen the window (before/after) instead of re-scanning the same lines; the information exists somewhere by construction.
- Experts hold a prior model of "what a correct SAT answer looks like" (moderate, bounded, paraphrastic) and use it to pre-rank choices, then verify top candidates in text — prediction THEN verification, never vibes alone.
- Recovery move when lost mid-passage: reread first + last sentences to re-anchor the main point, then re-attack.
- Meta-pattern: the entire method is a loop of cheap hypotheses with explicit revision, which converges because the test is standardized.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- "The answer is sitting in the passage; find it." Half-true belief taught by tutors/teachers; leads students to hunt for literal matches and fall for verbatim-echo lures. Reality: passage holds the support, answer holds the paraphrase.
- "If I don't understand a choice, eliminate it." Comfort heuristic (confusion feels like wrongness); kills correct answers written in abstract diction — a deliberate test-writer move.
- "More interpretation = deeper reading = credit." Trained by English class norms; produces overreach picks (species 2/3/5). The test wants literal, bounded restatement.
- "Digital/shorter = easier, and screen speed is fine." Device habits (scroll/skim) imported into the test; produces careless M1 errors with adaptive downside.
- "Guessing tactics can substitute for comprehension on hard items." Attractive because tactics are cheap; fails because engineered distractors specifically survive tactic-only filtering; also the down-to-two-always-wrong pattern is misread as bad luck rather than missed main point.
- "Writing notes wastes time / is for weak readers." Status belief; actually working-memory offload distinguishes top scorers; time cost is seconds.
- "Fast finishing signals mastery." Fluent strong readers especially hold this (instinct feels sufficient); the author's own before/after account exists to break it.
- "I have to read/answer in presented order." Default compliance; costs energy sequencing and triage opportunity.
- "Every question must be attempted with full effort to hit my goal." Perfectionism; for most goals, strategic sacrifice yields more points than universal haste.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim, beyond format)

- unverified-claim: In ~100-word passages, the main point is disproportionately in the first (and secondarily last) sentence; middles matter less for global questions.
- unverified-claim: Answers tend to sit near emphasis machinery (transitions, strong language, unusual punctuation, importance words) when such machinery is near the queried span.
- unverified-claim: Extreme-language choices are usually wrong but not always — frequency of exceptions rises at the hard end.
- unverified-claim: Longer, detail-rich choices fail more often (more claims = more failure surface).
- unverified-claim: Function questions usually need MORE context than the underlined span (read before and after); if the span starts near the passage top, the paragraph's opening supplies the governing point.

- `rw-literalism-discipline` → `rw-rhetorical-role-reading` → `rw-argument-comprehension` (you can't judge a sentence's job before you can state exactly what it says; you can't evaluate support/undermine before you can name the claim).

- `rw-answer-in-own-words-first` → `rw-elimination-protocol` → `rw-verbatim-check-anchors`.
