# Raw notes — Words in Context (source: Meltzer, The Critical Reader 5th Ed, ch. 2 "Vocabulary in Context")

Digital SAT mapping: Craft & Structure → Words in Context. Two sub-shapes covered:
fill-in-the-blank items ("most logical and precise word") and ask-about-an-existing-word
items ("as used in the text, X most nearly means"). Source claims blank-fill items heavily
dominate; treat count claims as unverified.

## Concepts taught

- **wic-blank-fill-vs-word-probe** — The two Words-in-Context question shapes make
  different demands: a blank-fill item requires deriving the missing idea from passage
  logic AND knowing (or morphologically guessing) the standard meanings of four
  candidate words; a word-probe item requires deriving what an already-present word must
  mean here, where its dictionary meaning is often irrelevant or actively a trap.
- **context-outranks-dictionary** — The governing invariant: a word means whatever the
  surrounding evidence forces it to mean. The question is really "what idea belongs at
  this position," with the printed word almost a placeholder variable.
- **logic-connector-reading** — Sentences broadcast their internal logic through
  connective words, which sort into three functional families: same-direction connectors
  (the idea keeps going → blank echoes nearby key words), cause/effect-and-explanation
  signals (blank must supply the cause or consequence of a stated idea; note that colons
  and dashes silently do this job too), and reversal connectors (blank must oppose or
  sit inconsistent with the stated key words). Knowing which family is present converts
  the blank from an open guess into a constrained slot.
- **anticipate-then-match** — Before reading any choices, extract the evidence phrase,
  predict your own rough filler (a sloppy approximation is fine), then scan for the
  choice matching that prediction. Crucially conditional: if you CANNOT confidently
  predict, skip prediction entirely rather than plant a half-sure guess — a wrong
  anchor primes you to reject the right answer.
- **charge-tagging** — When exact meaning is elusive, classify the slot as
  positive/negative and physically mark it. Then sweep choices in order eliminating
  every choice of the wrong or neutral charge. Typically kills 2–3 options; plug in
  survivors.
- **word-charge-vs-idea-charge** — The charge of the required WORD can differ from the
  charge of the sentence's IDEA whenever a downgrader/negation modifies the blank
  ("a smaller ___ effect", "less ___"). Two negatives compose into a positive idea and
  vice versa. Experts resolve this composition BEFORE viewing choices.
- **structural-mirroring** — Paired constructions ("both X and ___", colon elaborations,
  two-clause restatements) force the blank to run parallel to a named partner idea; the
  sentence's architecture, not just its content words, pins the answer.
- **morphology-as-fallback** — Word parts (prefixes, roots) and Romance-language
  cognates let a student make a disciplined guess at an unknown choice. Explicitly
  bounded: some words wear a negative-looking prefix while being positive, so morphology
  is a tiebreaker and plausibility check, never the primary engine.
- **second-sense-alertness** — Everyday words carry formal second senses the test loves.
  Detection cue: all four choices are always the same part of speech, so a choice that
  "looks like the wrong part of speech" is guaranteed to be operating in a second sense
  — a signal to examine it more closely, not eliminate it.
- **known-first-elimination** — Work from words you know toward words you don't. An
  unknown distractor's meaning never blocks the answer if the correct choice can be
  positively verified; park unknowns and evaluate everything else first.
- **widen-the-window** — Evidence for a word's forced meaning is sometimes not in its
  own sentence; read one sentence up and one down before deciding. Focusing only on the
  line containing the blank can lead to picking the exact opposite of the answer.
- **substitute-and-listen** — Terminal verification: drop the surviving candidate into
  the sentence and test it for logical and grammatical coherence — while remembering
  that "sounds unfamiliar to me" is not evidence against a choice.
- **ordered-sweeping** — Evaluate choices strictly A→D rather than jumping to attractive
  options; keeps elimination systematic and reduces slips. If unsure whether a choice
  fits, retain it rather than force an early kill.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Procedure-first, vocabulary-second. The chapter's core bet is that a repeatable
  decision procedure (read whole passage → find key words → predict → charge-tag →
  eliminate → plug in) recovers most items even with imperfect word knowledge.
- Vocabulary study is prescribed diagnostically, not universally: take practice tests
  first; only if misses trace to unknown words does dedicated word-learning enter the
  plan. Ongoing habit recommended instead: brief daily reading of serious nonfiction
  with a personal unknown-word log, learning words inside topics of interest.
- Confidence calibration is taught explicitly: you do not need certainty to answer;
  a solid logical process plus partial knowledge is presented as sufficient.
- Metacognitive offloading: physically write the predicted charge/definition to reduce
  working-memory strain — the source treats scratch work as part of the method.
- Scaffold-then-fade exercise design: early practice items embed intermediate prompts
  (mark key words; commit to a definition or a charge before answering), later items
  remove the prompts. Good model for our drill UI.
- Stakes framing: careless errors early matter extra because first-module accuracy
  gates access to the harder second module (and thus the score ceiling) — used to
  motivate the systematic sweep.
- Answer explanations model per-distractor reasoning (why each wrong choice tempts and
  fails), not just why the right one is right.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. **Meaning is derived, not recalled.** The expert never asks "what does this word
   mean to me?" but "what meaning does the evidence force into this slot?" They locate
   the evidence phrase, note the logical connector linking it to the slot, and compute
   direction (same/opposite/causal) before considering any candidate word.
2. **Polarity before precision.** When full meaning is uncertain, the expert extracts
   the cheapest reliable feature — charge — and spends it on elimination before doing
   any fine-grained comparison. Ordering work from cheap-and-certain to
   expensive-and-uncertain is the transferable habit.
3. **Composition of modifiers.** Experts parse "less/hardly/fails to + BLANK"
   arithmetic explicitly: the sentence's overall stance and the required word's charge
   are computed as separate quantities that multiply.
4. **Definition discipline.** A choice only wins if its DEFINITION satisfies the
   evidence; being conceptually adjacent is insufficient. If a stronger word (e.g., one
   implying newness or a first-of-kind role) were correct, the passage would have to
   contain direct evidence for that extra content — absence of that evidence eliminates
   the choice. This "would require more evidence than exists" test is a general-purpose
   distractor killer.
5. **Asymmetric certainty management.** Unknown words are quarantined, never guessed
   about prematurely; the expert proves the known choices in or out first, and picks an
   unknown only by exhaustion of knowns.
6. **Substitution as final gate, association as red flag.** Plugging the candidate back
   in tests grammatical/logical fit; simultaneously, the expert distrusts any choice
   whose appeal is topical association ("word that belongs to this subject area")
   rather than slot logic.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **single-meaning-belief** — Students assume each word has one meaning (the one they
  learned), so in word-probe items they seize the familiar definition. Formed by
  vocab-list schooling that pairs each word with exactly one gloss. Produces the
  common-meaning decoy pick. The test writes this trap by ALWAYS including the everyday
  sense among the choices; source's rule of thumb: the familiar definition should be
  presumed wrong until nothing else fits.
- **sounds-right-heuristic** — Students accept/reject words by acoustic familiarity
  ("I'd never say it that way") rather than definition. Formed by learning language
  through speech; personal usage feels like evidence. Produces elimination of correct
  second-sense answers and retention of fluent-sounding wrong ones.
- **prestige-bias** — On hard items students pick the most sophisticated-looking word,
  believing difficulty implies exotic answers. The test exploits this inversely: it
  likes obscure DISTRACTORS with plain correct answers, so choosing by impressiveness
  is worse than chance.
- **idea-charge/word-charge conflation** — Students who correctly read a sentence as
  positive then grab a positive word, missing that a downgrader before the blank
  flipped the required word's charge. Formed because polarity is usually computed
  holistically, not compositionally.
- **blank-line tunnel vision** — Reading only the sentence containing the blank/target.
  Formed as a time-saving instinct. Produces opposite-meaning picks when the decisive
  contrast lives one sentence away.
- **forced-prediction habit** — Misapplying "predict first" by planting a low-confidence
  guess, which then biases matching. The remedy taught: prediction is optional; skip to
  charge-tagging when unsure.
- **prefix-literalism** — Trusting that negative-looking word parts imply negative
  meanings; a family of positive words wearing "negative" morphology breaks this.
  Formed by over-generalizing genuinely useful root/prefix rules.
- **unknown-word panic** — Freezing or abandoning an item because some CHOICES are
  unknown, when identifying the right answer never requires defining the wrong ones.
- **idiom-misreading** — A small set of fixed phrases whose surface reading inverts
  their actual meaning (a phrase that looks universal but means "despite"; one that
  looks like exclusion but means "almost entirely") can silently flip a student's
  reading of the whole sentence.

## Trap taxonomy for WiC items (distractor design patterns)

1. **Familiar-sense decoy** — the target word's everyday meaning, offered where context
   demands a second sense. Exploits single-meaning-belief. (Signature of word-probe items.)
2. **Topic-association decoy** — a word that belongs to the passage's subject matter or
   collocates with the target, but whose definition fails the slot's logic. Feels right
   by proximity, not by meaning.
3. **Charge-flip decoy** — correct semantic field, wrong polarity; especially potent
   under negation stacking, where the naive holistic reading points at it.
4. **Overclaim decoy** — a choice whose definition adds content the passage never
   supplies (implying newness, agency, causation, or role that the evidence doesn't
   state). Attractive because the extra content is *plausible*.
5. **Reversed-dependency decoy** — states the relationship backwards (A governs B
   offered where B governs A). Tests whether the student mapped the sentence's actual
   direction.
6. **Nearby-synonym bait** — a choice that matches ANOTHER word physically present in
   the passage (or one of its senses) rather than the target's forced meaning. Feels
   textually supported because the student literally saw its cousin.
7. **Prestige bait** — rare/academic word placed as a distractor beside a plain correct
   answer, harvesting prestige-bias.
8. **Wrong-POS illusion** — a choice that reads as the wrong part of speech at first
   glance; since all four choices always share the correct POS, it is actually in a
   second sense, and reflexively eliminating it is the intended error.
9. **Intensity mismatch** — right direction, wrong strength (mildly-negative offered
   where the evidence demands strong contempt, or vice versa). Forces students to read
   degree, not just direction.
10. **Time-direction decoy** — pairs of role words distinguished only by temporal
    direction (precursor vs. descendant vs. leftover); the evidence fixes the direction
    and the decoys sit on the wrong side of it.

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim — check against official material + bank)

- ~10 vocab items per test, majority blank-fill; word-probe items comparatively rare.
- All four choices always share one part of speech (basis of the wrong-POS-illusion cue).
- Correct answers skew toward plainer words; the hardest vocabulary in a choice set is
  more often a distractor. Second-sense choices allegedly carry an elevated prior of
  being correct on non-vocab reading items too.
- Blank-fill answer choices draw on advanced high-school academic register; the test
  avoids truly outlandish vocabulary.
- Module-1 R&W accuracy gates routing to the harder module 2 (we already treat this as
  true platform-wide; consistent with our adaptive model).
- Fixed evidence guarantee: the definition of the missing word is always constructed
  into the passage — no item requires outside knowledge of the topic.
- Kinds of words favored (characterization, not a list): verbs of scholarly
  argumentation (proposing, reinforcing, disputing, inferring); quantity/scarcity and
  variety nouns; evaluative adjectives with clear charge (striking/harmless/inborn
  families); names of academic disciplines and experiment-process terms; and common
  everyday words that own a formal second sense. Rationale: the exam samples the
  register of scholarly prose rather than "SAT words" of the memorization era.

## Prerequisite edges implied

- logic-connector-reading → everything else in this chapter (both question shapes
  depend on classifying the connective before anything else).
- charge-tagging → word-charge-vs-idea-charge (composition presupposes basic polarity).
- logic-connector-reading → structural-mirroring (parallel frames are connector-anchored).
- context-outranks-dictionary → second-sense-alertness (alternate senses only make
  sense once meaning is understood as slot-driven).
- charge-tagging + known-first-elimination → prestige-bias remediation (the disciplined
  sweep is what removes the temptation).
- morphology-as-fallback depends on none of the above but is explicitly subordinate to
  logic-connector-reading in the decision order.
- Cross-chapter: logic-connector-reading is the same machinery as transition questions
  (Expression of Ideas) and contrast-based inference items — strong candidate for one
  shared graph node feeding multiple skills.

## Difficulty ladder observed

- Easy: single sentence, connector adjacent to blank, evidence phrase explicit,
  choices are common words in primary senses.
- Medium: multi-sentence context with evidence one sentence away; charge computation
  through one negation; one associated-but-wrong decoy; choice sets mixing one or two
  unfamiliar words.
- Hard: stacked negation or downgraders on the blank; second-sense correct answers;
  literary/period prose (19th-century fiction, poetry) where common words drift from
  modern usage; intensity and time-direction discriminations; hardest-looking word
  planted as bait; syntax long enough that relationship-mapping (not vocabulary) is the
  real task.

## What this source does well / poorly

Well: the cheap-feature-first procedure (charge before precision) is genuinely expert
and directly implementable in a tutor hint ladder; the per-distractor explanation style
is a model for our solution format; the scaffold-then-fade exercise design maps cleanly
to our drill progression; conditional advice (predict only when confident; keep unsure
choices) shows unusual calibration honesty; the trap inventory is rich and consistent
with digital-SAT-style items.

Poorly: leans on static word/second-meaning inventories (memorization-adjacent; we
extract only the CHARACTER of tested vocabulary, never lists); no timing/pacing
guidance for these items; the charge tool is undersold for genuinely neutral slots
(no fallback taught beyond plug-in); no retention system for the recommended word log
(no spacing/testing loop); frequency and POS-uniformity claims are asserted without
data — gate them through our bank stats before the tutor asserts any of them.
