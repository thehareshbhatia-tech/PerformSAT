# Raw notes — The Critical Reader 5th Ed, Ch. 8 (Supporting & Undermining)

Source: Meltzer, *The Critical Reader* 5th ed., ch. 8 (book pp. 133–144; scans p0134–p0145).
Maps to: R&W — Command of Evidence (textual): which finding/quotation supports, illustrates,
or weakens a claim or hypothesis.
Status: extraction only; all wording below is ours.

## Concepts taught

- **evidence-question-family** — Same inference machinery as text completions, plus one
  extra move: instead of completing an argument, the student evaluates a NEW statement's
  bearing on a stated hypothesis. Three sub-shapes: illustrate a claim (usually with a
  quotation from literature/poetry), support a claim (usually a research finding), and
  weaken/undermine a claim (find the statement most inconsistent with it).
- **claim-first-rule** — Nothing can be judged as support or attack until the claim itself
  is explicit. Step one is always to isolate and, if dense, re-say the claim plainly on
  scratch paper. The claim frequently lives at the END of the passage, and the question
  stem's verb (theorize / expect / propose / conclude) is the search key for finding it.
- **direction-prediction** — Before reading choices, characterize what KIND of evidence
  would do the job — most powerfully by converting the claim into a directional relation
  between variables (more X → more Y). Support = a finding pointing the same direction;
  weaken = a finding pointing the opposite direction OR showing no effect. The student
  predicts the direction, not the exact content.
- **weaken-includes-null** — Undermining does not require proving the claim false; a
  counterexample (the supposedly protective trait present, yet the bad outcome happened
  anyway) or a no-relationship finding is enough. "Most inconsistent with" is the real
  criterion.
- **mechanism-specificity** — A supporting finding must engage the claim's specific causal
  story, not just its topic. If the claim is "the non-obvious component causes the
  benefit," evidence that the product-in-general is beneficial is a trap; only evidence
  isolating the claimed component (e.g., the effect appearing when the famous ingredient
  is removed) supports it.
- **hypothetical-evidence-frame** — "Which finding, IF TRUE…" means the choices are new
  external facts to be weighed against the claim, not statements to be verified against
  the passage. The passage supplies the claim; the choice supplies candidate evidence.
- **quote-as-evidence-translation** — Illustration items quote poetry or old prose; the
  work is translating each quotation into its plain literal meaning, then checking whether
  that meaning enacts the claim. Functional cues let you eliminate before full translation:
  a quotation that asks a question cannot "urge"; a first-person-plural line cannot be the
  speaker addressing someone else; a neutral description cannot illustrate praise.
- **dont-rationalize-distractors** — Time spent constructing a story that makes a wrong
  choice sensible is itself diagnostic: if an option needs effort to connect to the claim,
  that effort is evidence it's wrong. Hold the predicted direction tightly and refuse to
  meet distractors halfway.
- **skip-and-return-triage** — These items are effort-dense; if the claim can't be pinned
  down or focus is failing, deliberately defer the question rather than half-work it.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Continuity: explicitly built on the text-completion chapter — students should master the
  "complete the logic" move before the "evaluate new evidence" move.
- Same three-beat routine every time (identify/rephrase claim → independently characterize
  qualifying evidence → only then read choices), with the warning that choices are written
  to confuse and cannot substitute for step 2.
- Steps are non-skippable; impatience is framed as the primary failure mode.
- Worked examples deliberately escalate: clean directional claim → mechanism-specific
  claim → dense-language literary claim, each with full four-choice autopsies.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. Read the question stem first for the task verb (support / illustrate / undermine) and
   the claim-holder (whose theory?).
2. Find the claim in the passage by scanning for the stem's verb or its cousins; expect it
   late in the passage. Rephrase it plainly; compress to a directional relation if the
   claim is empirical.
3. Write the target: "support = finding where X↑ and Y↑" or "weaken = X↑ but Y↓/no
   change." For mechanism claims, note WHICH component must carry the effect.
4. Test each choice only against that target. Sort quickly into: right direction, wrong
   direction, no bearing (off-topic), right topic but wrong mechanism/condition, right
   idea but self-disqualifying tail (a clause that breaks the comparison).
5. For quotations: translate to literal prose meaning first; use speech-act and addressee
   cues for fast elimination; require an explicit enactment of the claim (e.g., an actual
   contrast for a "contrast" claim — the presence of a pivot word like "but/yet" is a
   useful first filter, then verify the two contrasted things are the RIGHT two things).
6. Anti-sunk-cost check: if an option takes real work to justify, drop it.

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **topic-match-equals-support** — Students accept any finding about the claim's subject
  as supporting it. Formed because school "evidence" tasks usually only require topical
  relevance. The test exploits this with on-topic findings that never touch the claimed
  relation (e.g., generic praise of a product when the claim is about one ingredient).
- **direction-blindness** — Under pressure, students verify relevance but not polarity,
  picking a strengthener on a weaken question or vice versa — especially when the claim or
  choice contains negation. Root cause: holding the relation in the head instead of on
  paper.
- **passage-verification-error** — On "if true" items, students reject correct answers
  because "the passage never says that." They misunderstand the hypothetical frame: the
  choice is offered as new evidence, not as a passage fact.
- **mechanism-agnosticism** — Students treat conditions mentioned in the passage as
  interchangeable (any sleep stage, any coffee type), missing that the claim binds the
  effect to one specific condition and a choice binding it to a different one is a
  contradiction, not support.
- **charitable-overreading** — Diligent students try to make every option sensible,
  burning time and talking themselves into wrong answers. Formed by classroom habits that
  reward finding merit in every interpretation.
- **poetry-vibe-matching** — On quotation items students select the most emotionally
  resonant or thematically colored lines rather than translating literally, because
  archaic/metaphoric language pushes them to judge by feel.
- **comparison-anchor-neglect** — Students accept findings lacking the baseline the claim
  needs (a change with no comparison point, a single-group result for a comparative
  claim). They read the headline of the choice and skip its quantitative structure.

## Trap taxonomy observed in item design (from worked examples + exercise set)

- Wrong-direction finding (mirror-image of the correct answer; catches direction-blind
  students on both support and weaken stems).
- Generic-topic support: affirms the broad phenomenon while ignoring the claim's specific
  mechanism or component.
- Condition swap: correct effect attached to the wrong stage/type/subgroup explicitly
  distinguished in the passage.
- Self-disqualifying tail: a mostly-right choice whose final clause breaks the needed
  comparison or reverses its force — punishes partial reading.
- Missing baseline: a striking number with no comparative anchor, useless for a
  comparative claim.
- For quotation items: right poem, wrong speech act (question vs. exhortation); right
  sentiment, wrong subject (focuses on the other party); accurate-but-neutral description
  when the claim requires evaluation or contrast.
- Answer that would matter to a neighboring claim in the passage but not the one the stem
  names (passages often contain two claims; the stem picks one).

## SAT patterns claimed (unverified-claim)

- Simple "illustrate" items appear early in an R&W module; the hardest support/undermine
  items cluster late in the reading run, near the reading→writing seam. (unverified-claim —
  and needs re-examination for adaptive module composition)
- Illustrate-with-quotation items skew toward poetry/prose fiction; support/undermine skew
  toward science and social science. (unverified-claim, plausible)
- Key claims sit at the end of the passage often enough to make "check the last sentence
  first" a payoff heuristic for both this family and text completions. (unverified-claim)

## Prerequisite edges implied

- text-completion inference (previous chapter) → support/undermine (stated as the same
  family, one step further)
- claim-identification + claim-compression → all three sub-shapes
- directional/variable reasoning (more/less, cause/effect polarity) → support & weaken
- literal translation of archaic or figurative language → illustrate-with-quotation items
- negation handling (from the completions toolkit) → weaken items especially

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the claim→direction→choices pipeline is clean and teachable; arrow-notation for
  claims makes polarity errors visible; "weaken includes no-effect" is an important and
  commonly missed point; the mechanism-specificity idea (evidence must isolate the claimed
  component) is the sharpest insight in the chapter; the "if an option needs work, it's
  wrong" time heuristic is genuinely expert behavior.
- Poorly: no treatment of quantitative Command of Evidence (tables/graphs as the evidence
  source) — the digital SAT tests that shape heavily and it's absent here; the illustrate
  sub-shape gets one worked example; no explicit difficulty ladder; module-position claims
  are pre-adaptive; overlaps heavily with our Inferences material without flagging where
  the skills genuinely diverge (evaluating NEW evidence vs. completing GIVEN premises) —
  our graph should make that distinction the headline.
