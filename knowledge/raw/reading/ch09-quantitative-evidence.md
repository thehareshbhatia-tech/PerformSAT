# Raw notes — Meltzer, The Critical Reader 5th Ed, Ch. 9 (Graphs and Charts)
Maps to: Command of Evidence — Quantitative. Source studied p145–158 (scans p0146–p0159). All wording below is ours.

## Concepts taught

- **claim-anchored-graph-reading** — Before touching the data display, locate the exact claim/theory/conclusion the question serves; only the slice of the graphic that bears on that claim matters. The claim almost always sits at the END of the passage; openings are scene-setting.
- **checklist-decomposition-of-claims** — Convert the claim into 2–3 explicit, testable conditions (e.g., "recovered after a dip" = trace must go down then up; "highest in the period" = final point exceeds every prior point), then audit each candidate against ALL conditions. An answer is only right when every condition holds.
- **choices-before-graphic-triage** — Many quantitative items are decidable from the question stem + answer-choice wording alone; the graphic is optional verification. Signal: long, information-dense verbal choices → wording logic dominates; short label-only choices (a region, a category) → the graphic is genuinely required.
- **accuracy-vs-relevance-split** — A choice can describe the data display with perfect factual accuracy and still be wrong because it addresses the wrong aspect of the claim. Correctness = accuracy AND alignment with the claim's specific focus.
- **quantitative-magnitude-matching** — When the claim uses size language ("far lower," "much greater"), the cited gap must actually be large; a real-but-small difference fails. Conversely "roughly equal" claims need near-identical values.
- **entity-count-matching** — If the claim is about ONE subject showing an internal contrast (same drug, same animal, same month-type), choices comparing TWO different subjects are structurally disqualified regardless of their numbers.
- **completion-blank-backup-rule** — For "logically complete the text" hybrids ending in an illustration transition ("for instance"-type), the governing idea is in the sentence BEFORE the blank; a contrast transition right before the blank ("whereas"-type) means the blank must state the opposite pole of the setup.
- **undermine-by-counterexample** — Weaken items hinge on finding the data point the passage's flawed conclusion ignored (a second member of the category behaving oppositely). The conclusion's inference gap tells you what to hunt for.
- **single-option-default** — If exactly one choice satisfies the stated conditions, it is correct by elimination even without graph verification; re-checking is fine, re-litigating is not.

## Teaching philosophy observed

- Deflation first: the chapter's whole posture is "these look harder than they are." It teaches confidence against visual complexity before teaching any graph skill.
- Sequencing: text logic is taught BEFORE graph reading. The graphic is framed as the last resort, not the starting point — inverted from how students naturally attack these.
- Worked examples deliberately alternate: one where the graphic is unavoidable (bare-label choices), one where the graphic is skippable (verbose choices), one where checking the graphic first actively causes the wrong answer. The contrast is the lesson.
- No graph-literacy remediation (axes, legends, units get almost no airtime) — assumes the bottleneck is strategy, not chart decoding.

## Expert reasoning patterns (generalized)

1. Read the passage's final sentence(s) and the question stem first; extract the claim verbatim in your head and mark its key qualifiers (emphasis verbs, magnitude words, time windows).
2. Decompose the claim into a condition checklist before looking at any numbers.
3. Triage by choice shape: verbose choices → try to resolve on wording alone (eliminate off-focus, wrong-entity-count, wrong-magnitude, wrong-direction options); bare-label choices → go to the graphic with the checklist.
4. In the graphic, look ONLY for the checklist features; deliberately ignore all other series, regions, and jargon. Long technical terms are treated as opaque tokens — you never need to know what they mean.
5. If one survivor remains, commit. Optionally verify against the display, but do not reopen eliminated options because they "also match the graph" — that is the designed trap.
6. Under time pressure: if the conclusion names a causal agent, check first the one choice that mentions that agent (the others usually describe data without touching the causal link).

## Misconceptions targeted (and why students hold them)

- **complexity-equals-difficulty** — Students equate visual busyness/jargon density with question difficulty because school science rewards decoding everything. Produces panic, time-sink, or skipping. The test manufactures intimidating displays around trivially checkable claims.
- **graph-match-equals-correct** — Students treat "this choice is true of the graph" as sufficient. Formed by years of single-source comprehension checks where factual accuracy WAS the criterion. The test plants multiple graph-accurate distractors with the wrong focus; students who verify choice-by-choice against the chart pick the first accurate one they meet.
- **must-understand-everything-first** — Belief that you read the graphic fully, then answer. Wastes time and, worse, unmoors attention from the claim so all accurate-looking choices feel viable.
- **prior-knowledge-anxiety** — Students think unfamiliar science topics disadvantage them; in fact every needed fact is on the page, and jargon is deliberately inert.
- **any-gap-counts** — When a claim requires a big difference, students accept any nonzero difference in the right direction. The test exploits this with a distractor citing a small true gap (a few points/units) beside the correct large-gap choice.
- **residual-second-guessing** — After correct elimination, students reopen the problem because the graphic "has more in it." Formed by distrust of verbal-only solutions to visual-looking questions.

## Trap taxonomy extracted from the exercise set (design only, no items)

1. **Ignored-counterexample trap** (undermine items): passage draws a category-level conclusion from the category's most prominent member; the display contains a low-performing second member of the same category. Exploits reading the conclusion charitably instead of auditing its scope.
2. **Small-true-gap distractor**: correct answer needs a dramatic contrast; distractor cites a genuine but modest gap. Exploits any-gap-counts.
3. **Two-entity distractor**: claim about one subject's internal contrast; distractor compares across subjects with plausible numbers. Exploits skimming the claim's grammatical subject.
4. **Same-value distractor**: cites an entity whose two measures are equal — the exact opposite of a "big difference" requirement — but looks relevant because the entity is prominent (a 100%-row effect).
5. **Uniformity distractor** (variance claims): when the claim needs study-to-study inconsistency, distractors describe features that are constant across studies ("each," "all" are tells).
6. **Wrong-season/wrong-window distractor**: right comparison, wrong time slice than the one the contrast transition demands.
7. **Off-text plausibility distractor**: statement that is reasonable real-world knowledge (travelers prefer e-readers) but unjudgeable from the display — "not enough information" disguised as an inference.

## Prerequisite edges implied

- claim-identification-in-short-passages (find + paraphrase the governing claim) → prerequisite for claim-anchored-graph-reading
- transition-word-logic (illustration vs contrast markers) → prerequisite for completion-blank-backup-rule
- basic-data-display-literacy (series/legend/axis lookup, trend direction) → assumed, lightly exercised
- support-vs-undermine-question-logic → shared skeleton with textual command-of-evidence
- checklist-decomposition-of-claims → prerequisite for quantitative-magnitude-matching and entity-count-matching

## What this source does well / poorly

- Well: the accuracy≠relevance principle is the sharpest idea in the chapter and is demonstrated, not just asserted (an example where graph-first solving reliably produces a wrong answer). Strong, reusable trap taxonomy in the answer explanations; consistent claim-first discipline; good calibration advice against second-guessing.
- Poorly: almost no actual graph-reading instruction (multi-series legends, stacked bars, dual conditions) — weak for students whose true deficit is chart decoding; no timing guidance per item; small exercise set (5 items) with science-heavy topics; the "choices-before-graphic" heuristic is powerful but the book gives no failure-mode warning for when verbose choices DO require the graph (its own rainfall example needs the graph to distinguish two surviving choices).
