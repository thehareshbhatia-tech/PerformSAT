# Pronoun clarity: when a pronoun is too vague and the specific noun wins

## What it is
Beyond number agreement, a pronoun must point at exactly ONE reasonable referent.
When the choice set offers both a pronoun and a specific noun, and the pronoun's
referent is missing or ambiguous, the specific noun is the answer. The same logic
covers bare demonstratives: "this/that/these/those" standing alone tends to be
too vague; demonstrative + noun is the preferred repair. The operating tiebreak
is "more specific is better."

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Academic prose demands traceable reference. The exam probes whether a student can
detect that a sentence-locally fine pronoun fails at the discourse level — a
skill invisible to ear-based checking because ambiguous pronouns SOUND perfect.
These items skew medium-hard because the wrong choices are all grammatical; only
reference logic discriminates.

## Mental model
**Ambiguity is structural, not subjective.** Formalize when the test treats a
pronoun as ambiguous (the source material never does; this is our taxonomy):
- **Two candidates of the same type** — two same-gender people, or two plural
  thing-nouns, both eligible → any matching pronoun is automatically ambiguous.
- **Unique eligible candidate** → the pronoun is fine, even if other nouns are
  nearby (they must actually match in number/type to compete).
- **No stated candidate** → the pronoun fails outright (see the on-the-page rule
  in [[pronoun-antecedent-agreement]]).
Then the decision rule: given a choice between an unambiguous specific noun and a
pronoun that fails any test above, take the noun. Given a bare demonstrative vs
demonstrative + noun, take the one with the noun.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Notice the choice-set shape ([[choice-set-diagnosis]]): pronouns mixed with
   named nouns, or bare "this/these" vs "this + noun," signals a clarity item —
   not (only) an agreement item.
2. Count eligible candidates for the pronoun: nouns of matching number and
   person/thing type in range. Two or more → ambiguous; zero → unstated referent.
3. If ambiguity or absence is found, eliminate ALL pronoun-only choices at once
   and pick among the specific options for the one whose noun names the intended
   referent.
4. When two named people share a gender, don't deliberate — scan directly for the
   choice that repeats one of the names.
5. Sanity-check number on the surviving choice anyway; the test likes one
   specific-but-wrong-number distractor.

## Misconceptions
- **reader-can-figure-it-out** — the student accepts an ambiguous pronoun because
  context makes the intended referent guessable. Forms because cooperative
  listeners always disambiguate; the tested standard requires the SENTENCE to do
  it. Produces picks of fluent pronoun choices over "clunky" noun repetition.
- **repetition-phobia** — the student avoids the specific-noun choice because
  school writing instruction demonized repeating nouns ("use pronouns for
  variety"). The exam's value ranking is the reverse: clarity beats elegance.
  This is why the noun-over-pronoun rule feels wrong to good writers.
- **demonstrative-feels-precise** — bare "this" feels pointed and specific
  (it points!), so students don't register its vagueness about WHAT it points at.
  Forms from speech, where gesture and intonation disambiguate demonstratives.
- **one-axis-clarity** — the student checks only ambiguity and picks a specific
  choice with the wrong number, or checks only number and picks a clear-number
  pronoun with two candidates. The standard distractor recipe is: one choice
  right-number-but-vague, one specific-but-wrong-number, one fully correct — 
  single-axis checkers get caught either way.

## Diagnostic indicators
- Picks a grammatical pronoun where two same-type candidates exist; explanation
  appeals to what the passage "obviously means" → **reader-can-figure-it-out**.
- Visibly avoids choices that repeat a noun already in the sentence; picks
  pronouns even when referents are missing → **repetition-phobia** (listen for
  "it sounds repetitive").
- Accepts bare demonstratives but correctly rejects ambiguous personal pronouns →
  **demonstrative-feels-precise**.
- Alternating error pattern across the vague/wrong-number/correct distractor
  trio → **one-axis-clarity**; teach the two-check sequence rather than either
  rule alone.

## Remediation pathways
- **reader-can-figure-it-out**: adversarial-reading drill — for each pronoun
  choice, the student must construct the OTHER reading (the unintended referent).
  If they can build one, the choice dies. This converts a vibe into a test.
- **repetition-phobia**: reframe the value system explicitly — on SAT conventions
  items, "more specific is better" outranks elegance; show two or three items
  where the repeated noun is credited. Name it as a test-specific register, so
  their essay instincts stay intact.
- **demonstrative-feels-precise**: the "this WHAT?" probe — every bare
  demonstrative must answer "this what?" from the sentence itself; if the answer
  requires the previous sentence's whole idea, prefer the demonstrative + noun
  choice.
- **one-axis-clarity**: fixed two-check ritual (referent count, then number),
  drilled on the vague/wrong-number/correct trio until both checks fire every
  time. If number keeps failing, revisit [[pronoun-antecedent-agreement]].

## Mastery criteria
Can articulate WHY a pronoun is ambiguous (names both candidates) rather than
just picking the noun; catches the specific-but-wrong-number plant; and applies
the "this what?" probe unprompted. Proof: consistent accuracy on medium-hard
items using the three-distractor recipe, with explanations that reference
candidate-counting.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: rarely pure-easy; a missing-referent pronoun vs an obvious noun.
- **medium**: two same-type candidates; bare demonstrative vs demonstrative +
  noun.
- **hard**: the full trio recipe (vague / specific-wrong-number / correct) in
  dense prose, sometimes fused with apostrophe homophones or with the referent a
  sentence away — composing [[pronoun-antecedent-agreement]] and
  [[apostrophe-possession-grid]].

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach only "if the choices include a name/noun, check whether
  the pronoun could point at two things; if yes, take the noun." One rule, high
  yield.
- **Average**: add the ambiguity taxonomy (two candidates / one / zero) and the
  "this what?" probe; introduce the trio distractor recipe so they know both
  checks are needed.
- **Advanced**: adversarial-reading fluency — constructing the unintended
  referent quickly; drill items where the eligible-candidate count is genuinely
  arguable and the number check settles it.
- **1500+**: this skill is mostly about not over-triggering: a unique-candidate
  pronoun is FINE, and eliminating it for imagined vagueness is the high-scorer
  error. Drill "pronoun is acceptable" items alongside trap items so precision
  and recall both stay high.
