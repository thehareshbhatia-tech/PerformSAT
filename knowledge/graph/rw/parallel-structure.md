# Parallel structure: coordinated ideas share one grammatical shape

## What it is
Ideas of equal weight joined by coordination — a series of three or more, or a
two-item conjunction pair — must share one grammatical form: all nouns, all
gerunds, all infinitives, all finite verbs, all clauses. The match applies at
phrase granularity, not just first-word surface: a choice that opens with a noun
but smuggles in a finite verb has become a clause and breaks a noun-phrase
match. Correlative templates (both/and, not only/but also, either/or,
neither/nor) follow the same law: what follows the first marker must match what
follows the second.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Parallelism probes form-awareness: all four choices typically paraphrase the
same fact, so meaning cannot discriminate — only shape can. Structurally, it is
the counterexample to blank-local solving: the matching partner (the anchor) is
deliberately placed OUTSIDE the blank in fixed text, forcing a full-sentence
read. Two dominant formats [unverified-claim: verify format split vs our bank]:
the three-item series and the conjunction pair. A series stem can also be
re-tested on a different axis — separator consistency (commas throughout or
semicolons throughout, never mixed) — so one surface hosts two different
question types.

## Mental model
**Find the anchor, match its opener.** The fixed text contains at least one item
of the coordination whose form is not negotiable. Classify the anchor's form by
its first word(s) — noun? gerund? infinitive? finite verb? clause? — then keep
only choices opening in that form. Two guardrails:
- **Elision allowance**: repeating a shared function word (a second "to," a
  repeated preposition) is optional; both the repeated and elided versions count
  as parallel. Don't eliminate for a missing echo-word.
- **Phrase-integrity check**: scan past the opener for a verb that turns the
  item into a clause, and for a voice flip — passive rewrites and clause-ified
  items are the two standard distractor builds.
For separator questions on a series, ignore content entirely and scan only the
marks between items for consistency (rules live in [[three-legal-joins]] /
[[three-legal-joins]]).

## Expert reasoning process
1. Spot coordination: a series marker or coordinating/correlative conjunction
   near the blank flags parallelism before anything else
   ([[choice-set-diagnosis]]).
2. Locate the anchor item(s) in fixed text — read the whole sentence; the
   partner is placed outside the blank on purpose.
3. Classify the anchor's form by first word(s).
4. Filter choices to those opening in the anchor's form; usually one survives.
5. If several survive, run the second axis: did a choice add a subject/finite
   verb (clause-ified)? flip to passive ("was … by" shapes are suspicious)?
   mix separators?
6. For correlative pairs, place your finger after each marker and compare the
   two continuations directly.
7. Verify tense/number consistency inside the match — a series of verbs must
   also agree in tense ([[verb-tense-timeline]]), and coordinated role nouns
   must agree in number with their shared subject
   ([[grammatical-number-assignment]]).

## Misconceptions
- **meaning-over-form** — any item conveying the right idea is accepted, since
  all four choices paraphrase the same fact. Forms because school feedback
  rewards content over structure; the test isolates structure. Signature:
  choosing a clause where a noun phrase is required.
- **parallel-means-identical-words** — the inverse error: eliminating a correct
  choice because it doesn't repeat a function word the anchor used. Forms from
  overliteral "make them match"; the elision allowance is unknown. Produces
  false eliminations and second-guessing on correct answers.
- **blank-local-solving** — evaluating choices without locating the anchor,
  assuming everything needed is underlined. Forms because several other item
  types ARE blank-local (modifiers, apostrophes); parallelism is the designed
  counterexample.
- **separator-blindness** — comma vs semicolon inside a series is treated as a
  style choice, so mixed-separator options look fine. Forms because readers
  comprehend past punctuation and never re-scan the marks themselves.
- **longest-fanciest-wins** — under uncertainty, the most elaborate choice gets
  picked; parallel answers are typically the plainest, shortest form-match.
  Shares a root with [[choice-set-diagnosis]]'s style-preference-solving.

## Diagnostic indicators
- Picks a clause-ified or infinitive paraphrase of a gerund/noun anchor →
  **meaning-over-form**; ask them to name the anchor's form — inability to
  classify is the real gap.
- Eliminates the credited choice citing a missing repeated "to"/preposition →
  **parallel-means-identical-words**; a two-minute elision lesson fixes it.
- Wrong on pair items whose anchor sits early in the sentence, fine on series
  items where all items are visible near the blank → **blank-local-solving**.
- Accepts mixed comma/semicolon series → **separator-blindness**; check
  whether pure list-punctuation items also fail (then the gap is upstream in
  [[three-legal-joins]]).
- Picks the wordiest grammatical option under time pressure →
  **longest-fanciest-wins**.

## Remediation pathways
- **meaning-over-form**: form-classification micro-drill — given ten phrases,
  label each noun / gerund / infinitive / finite verb / clause. This atomic
  skill IS the chapter; multiple choice comes after. Then anchor-matching with
  the passage's content deliberately made boring so shape is all there is.
- **parallel-means-identical-words**: show one matched pair in both repeated and
  elided versions, both credited; state the allowance explicitly ("echo words
  are optional; FORM is mandatory").
- **blank-local-solving**: enforce the read-from-sentence-start ritual; drill
  pair items where the anchor is the sentence's first phrase.
- **separator-blindness**: marks-only scan training — cover the words, read
  only the punctuation between items; consistency violations become visually
  obvious. Revisit [[three-legal-joins]] for when semicolons are licensed.
- **longest-fanciest-wins**: same reframe as [[choice-set-diagnosis]]: the
  minimal correct form wins; show three past items where the shortest choice
  was credited.

## Mastery criteria
Names the anchor and its form before filtering; correctly KEEPS elided variants
(no false eliminations); catches clause-ification past the first word; and
solves both axes of a series item (form and separators) independently. Proof:
accuracy on pair items with distant anchors plus one mixed-axis series set,
with the anchor verbalized.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: three-item series, all items adjacent, one choice matches the
  visible pattern; distractors mismatch at the first word.
- **medium**: conjunction pairs with the anchor outside the blank; correlative
  templates; elision judgment calls.
- **hard**: phrase-integrity traps (noun-opening clauses), passive-voice
  rewrites, separator-consistency fused with a non-parallel item in the same
  set (only one choice is clean on BOTH axes), tense-inside-parallelism, and
  role-noun number agreement across a pair — composing [[verb-tense-timeline]],
  [[grammatical-number-assignment]], and the boundaries list rules.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: start with form classification of single phrases — nothing
  about the SAT yet. Then series-only items where the pattern is visible and
  the mismatch is at word one. The idea "match the shape, ignore the meaning"
  is the entire lesson.
- **Average**: teach the two formats (series, pair) and the anchor discipline —
  the partner lives in fixed text, so read the whole sentence. Add the elision
  allowance before it causes false eliminations.
- **Advanced**: phrase-integrity scanning (the smuggled verb), passive-rewrite
  suspicion, and dual-axis series items. Have them author one distractor of
  each build (clause-ify, passivize, mix separators) — designing traps trains
  detection.
- **1500+**: correlative continuations compared word-by-word, and the
  composition cases (tense-in-series, coordinated role nouns). Also
  anti-over-trigger work: rhetorically loose lists in Expression-of-Ideas
  contexts are NOT conventions errors; parallelism enforcement applies where
  the choice set says form is the axis.
