# Connector logic families — reading the direction arrows between ideas

## What it is
Every pair of adjacent ideas in a passage stands in one of three logical relationships: the second idea continues the first, the second idea explains or results from the first, or the second idea pushes against the first. Connective words — and some punctuation marks — are the printed markers of which relationship is in force. Classifying the connector before doing anything else converts an open-ended reading task into a constrained one.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
This is the single most reused piece of machinery in the R&W section. Words-in-Context items use it to force the meaning of a blank; transition items ask for the connector directly; inference items ask you to supply the idea a connector demands; function items use connectors as the primary signal of a sentence's job. The College Board is probing whether a reader tracks the *logical skeleton* of prose rather than just its content words. It appears at every difficulty level — easy items put the connector next to the decision point; hard items hide it a sentence away, use a rare connector, or replace it with punctuation.

## Mental model
**Every sentence boundary has an arrow on it.** Three arrow types:

1. **Same-direction (→)** — the idea keeps going: addition, another example, restatement, elaboration. Whatever comes next must *echo* nearby key words.
2. **Cause/effect & explanation (⇒)** — the next idea is the *reason for* or *consequence of* what was just said. Includes because/therefore/thus families — and, crucially, **colons and dashes, which perform this job silently**: what follows a colon explains or specifies what precedes it.
3. **Reversal (⤺)** — the next idea opposes, limits, or sits in tension with what was just said: but/however/although families, plus softer reversers like skeptical rhetorical questions and scare quotes.

The expert's first read of any decision-relevant sentence is: *find the arrow, name its family.* Once the family is named, the space of acceptable answers collapses.

**Structural mirroring** is the arrow model's operational sharpening: some constructions don't just point a direction, they name the exact partner the answer must parallel. Paired frames ("both X and ___," "not only X but also ___"), colon elaborations, and two-clause restatements all force the blank to mirror a specific stated idea — not the sentence's general drift. The move: find the partner (the X the frame names), then mirror it — matching its role, its register, and its level of generality. When a mirror frame is present, the answer is more constrained than the bare arrow family suggests, and choices that fit the direction but not the partner are exactly the engineered near-misses.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Locate the decision point (blank, underlined portion, transition slot, or referenced sentence).
2. Scan for explicit connectors touching it — sentence-initial words, mid-sentence conjunctions — AND for silent connectors: colon, dash, semicolon, parallel construction.
3. Classify the arrow: same-direction, cause/effect-explanation, or reversal.
4. Identify what the arrow connects: the specific idea on each side, not just "the sentences." (A reversal reverses *something particular* — name it.)
5. Only now generate or evaluate candidates: same-direction demands an echo of the key words; cause/effect demands a reason or result; reversal demands an opposite or an inconsistency.
6. Cross-check: substitute your answer and confirm the arrow still reads true in both directions.

What experts deliberately ignore at step 1–3: the topic, the vocabulary difficulty, and the answer choices. The arrow is computable from structure alone.

## Misconceptions
- **connector-blindness** — The student reads only content words and treats connectors as filler. Forms because school reading rewards extracting facts, not logic; connectors carry no facts. Produces same-direction answers on reversal items (the most common wrong-direction error): the student echoes nearby vocabulary that the connector actually negates.
- **punctuation-is-decoration** — The student knows "however" flips direction but doesn't register that a colon or dash imposes an explanation relationship. Forms because punctuation is taught as grammar (where commas go), never as logic. Produces failure on items where the only connector is punctuation — the student reports "there were no clue words."
- **connector-equals-one-word** — The student memorized a short list (but, however, therefore) and misses functional equivalents: "although," "granted," "admittedly," a question that challenges the previous claim, scare quotes signaling distance. Produces missed reversals whenever the test uses a connector off the memorized list.
- **reversal-scope-error** — The student correctly detects a reversal but reverses the wrong thing (flips the whole sentence's topic instead of the specific claim under contrast). Forms because naming *what* is reversed is a second, harder step that feels optional. Produces wrong-intensity and wrong-target answers that are "negative in the right general area."
- **ambiguous-connector-freeze** — Words like "while," "as," and "since" belong to two families depending on use. Students who learned one-word-one-family mappings misclassify or stall. Fix is reading the clause content, not the word alone.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **connector-blindness**: picks the choice that matches nearby key words on an item whose governing connector is a reverser; when asked to explain, retells the topic and never mentions the connector.
- **punctuation-is-decoration**: accuracy drops specifically on items where the arrow is a colon/dash; says "there was no transition word."
- **connector-equals-one-word**: handles but/however items fine, misses although/granted/rhetorical-question reversals; inconsistent within the same skill.
- **reversal-scope-error**: on reversal items, picks the wrong-polarity-adjacent distractor (right direction, wrong target or wrong strength); explanation names the contrast vaguely ("it's the opposite").
- **ambiguous-connector-freeze**: long dwell time on items headed by while/since/as; answer changes on review.

## Remediation pathways
- **connector-blindness**: contrast pair — same sentence frame with "and" vs "but," show the required filler flipping. Then a drill of arrow-tagging only (no answering). If it doesn't land, there is no prereq below this; rebuild with sentence-level pairs.
- **punctuation-is-decoration**: re-derivation — show that "X: Y" always survives the paraphrase "X, that is/because, Y." Have the student read colons aloud as "namely/because."
- **connector-equals-one-word**: taxonomy expansion by function, not list: "anything that concedes, doubts, or distances is a reverser." Sort 15 mixed connectors into the three families.
- **reversal-scope-error**: two-step protocol — after tagging a reversal, require the student to state "what exactly is being pushed against" in five words before looking at choices.
- **ambiguous-connector-freeze**: teach the disambiguation test: replace with "although" and with "because"; only one survives.

## Mastery criteria
Given any sentence pair (or blank-bearing sentence), the student can tag the arrow family and name what it connects in under ~15 seconds, across all three families, including punctuation-only and off-list connectors — demonstrated by consistent accuracy on reversal-governed medium/hard items in at least two consuming skills (e.g., words-in-context and transitions).

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: explicit, common connector adjacent to the decision point; single sentence.
- **Medium**: connector one sentence away from the decision point; silent connectors (colon/dash); "while/since" ambiguity; two connectors whose effects must be composed.
- **Hard**: reversal signaled only by a rhetorical question, scare quotes, or tonal shift; stacked arrows (a concession inside a reversal); period prose where connector conventions differ; composition with [[charge-composition-under-modifiers]] so the arrow AND a negation must both be processed. At the hard end this node composes with [[evidence-forced-word-meaning]], [[logical-text-completion]], and [[rhetorical-role-identification]].

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach only the three arrows with hand gestures / drawn arrows; drill tagging without answering questions at all. One family per session. Colon = "here comes the why."
- **Average**: full three-family sort including punctuation; introduce the "name what's being reversed" second step; mixed drills where the task is stating the required idea in their own words before choices.
- **Advanced**: off-list connectors, double-arrow sentences, arrow-tagging in 19th-century prose; timed arrow-tagging as a warm-up ritual.
- **1500+**: adversarial cases — items where the surface connector points one way but a larger structural reversal dominates; discuss how test writers build wrong-direction distractors and have the student author one.
