# Two-voice separation — keeping each author's position in its own labeled slot

## What it is
The foundational skill of paired-text items: holding each author's position as a distinct, labeled entry ("Author 1 claims X; Author 2 claims Y") instead of one blended impression of "what the passage says about the topic." Includes detail-attribution discipline — tracking which text every detail came from and re-verifying the source before accepting any detail-bearing answer choice.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Cross-Text Connections presents two short passages on one topic and asks about the relationship between them (unverified-claim: typically one paired set per R&W module with a single question — verify against bank). Every question in the family silently tests voice separation first: a student who blended the texts cannot compute any relationship. The test builds a dedicated distractor for blenders — the cross-text misattribution choice, which paraphrases a real detail from one text (usually Text 1) and offers it as the other author's view. It feels textually supported because the student genuinely read it; only source-checking exposes it.

## Mental model
**A two-column ledger.** Before any question work, fill two labeled slots: T1's main point compressed to a clause; T2's main point compressed to a clause. Every subsequent operation — naming the relationship, predicting a response, verifying a detail — reads from the ledger, not from a re-read. Details carry a source tag like a colored flag: a fact without its flag is unusable evidence. The ledger is also the working-memory strategy: two dense unfamiliar texts overflow recall, so the compression to two clauses is what makes the item tractable at all.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Read Text 1 for its single main point; compress to one clause; write or subvocalize it with its label. Ignore supporting detail on the first pass.
2. Same for Text 2. Note explicitly where T2 *mentions or targets* T1's territory, if it does.
3. Before choices: state both slots aloud in the form "A1 thinks ___; A2 thinks ___." If either slot is empty or the two have merged ("they talk about coral"), stop and rebuild — no downstream step works.
4. For any answer choice that carries a concrete detail, run attribution: which text does this detail actually appear in? Reject choices assigning a T1-only detail to Author 2's mouth (and vice versa).
5. On detail-hinging questions, return to the text before finalizing — the pivotal detail often sits mid-paragraph rather than at a structural landmark (unverified-claim), so first-read recall silently fails.

## Misconceptions
- **blended-voices** — The student merges both texts into one "what the passage says" summary, because all prior schooling treats a reading as a single source of truth; two texts on one topic feel like two paragraphs of one essay. Produces misattribution-distractor picks — the choice exists precisely for this reader — and inability to answer "which author would say...?" without rereading everything.
- **memory-overconfidence** — The student answers detail-hinging questions from first-read recall. Forms because recall usually suffices on single-text items where details sit at privileged positions (first/last sentence); here the pivotal detail may sit mid-paragraph, so recall fails silently, with full confidence. Produces confident wrong answers that cite a detail *almost* correctly — wrong text, wrong scope, or negated.
- **spokesperson-collapse** — Subtler than blending: the student keeps two slots but fills the second with "disagrees with Text 1" instead of an independent position. Author 2 becomes a mere reaction to Author 1. Produces failures whenever T2's actual claim (its own scope, method, or evidence) is what the question probes, and primes disagreement-means-total-opposition downstream in [[cross-text-relationship-taxonomy]].
- **detail-first-reading** — Reading both texts at detail grain on the first pass, overflowing working memory before the main points are secured. Forms from conscientiousness — "read carefully" applied at the wrong altitude. Produces time trouble and, paradoxically, *worse* voice separation: the slots never get filled because everything felt equally important.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **blended-voices**: picks cross-text misattribution distractors; when asked to summarize, produces one topic summary rather than two positions; uses "the passage" (singular) in explanations of a paired item.
- **memory-overconfidence**: does not look back at the texts before answering detail choices (observable in behavior/timing); high confidence on misses; explanation cites a detail that is real but altered.
- **spokesperson-collapse**: can state T1's position crisply but describes T2 only relationally ("the second one pushes back"); misses questions about T2's own commitments.
- **detail-first-reading**: long first-read times on paired sets; answers by rereading everything per choice; reports the texts felt "too dense."

## Remediation pathways
- **blended-voices**: impose the ledger as a physical ritual — two labeled lines written before choices, every paired item, no exceptions. Then dissect one misattribution distractor: show the detail is real AND the attribution is false, so familiarity is not support. If compression itself fails, drop to [[topic-point-attitude-reading]].
- **memory-overconfidence**: rule — detail-bearing choices require a return to the text, always; recall is a hypothesis, not evidence. Show them one item where their recalled detail differs from the printed one.
- **spokesperson-collapse**: require slot 2 to be a standalone claim that would make sense if Text 1 didn't exist; drill "state Author 2's position to someone who never read Text 1."
- **detail-first-reading**: teach the two-pass structure — first pass for main points only (deliberately skipping detail), second pass targeted by the question. Timing data usually shows the payoff within one session.

## Mastery criteria
On any paired set, the student produces two labeled one-clause positions before reading choices, catches misattribution distractors by explicit source-checking, and can state each author's view independently of the other — with detail-verification look-backs visible in their process and misattribution picks absent from their error log at hard difficulty.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Medium (entry)**: two short texts with overtly opposed positions stated in opening sentences; one misattribution distractor.
- **Hard**: T2 engages T1 only implicitly (never names it); positions differ in scope or method rather than direction, making the slots harder to phrase; pivotal detail mid-paragraph in dense scientific prose; both texts quote the same third party, adding a *third* voice to attribute ([[tone-attitude-reading]]'s attribution machinery under load). Composes upward into [[cross-text-relationship-taxonomy]] and [[cross-text-response-prediction]] — every hard failure there should first be checked against this node.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: make it concrete — two friends arguing about one movie; whose line is whose? Then the two-line ledger on very short pairs. Win condition: two correct labeled clauses, even if the question is missed.
- **Average**: full pipeline with the ledger ritual; misattribution-distractor dissection; the return-to-text rule for details.
- **Advanced**: implicit-engagement pairs and scope/method contrasts; third-voice attribution; enforce ledger speed (both slots in under a minute).
- **1500+**: run the ledger silently; drill on pairs where the *relationship* is subtle enough that slot phrasing determines the answer (a sloppy slot makes two compatible views look opposed); have the student write a misattribution distractor for a pair they just solved.
