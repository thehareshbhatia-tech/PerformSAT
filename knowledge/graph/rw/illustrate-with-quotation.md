# Illustrating a claim with a quotation

## What it is
A sub-shape of textual Command of Evidence: the passage states a claim *about a literary work* (its themes, its speaker's attitude, a character's situation), and the choices are four quotations from that work; the student picks the quotation that actually *enacts* the claim. The core labor is translation — converting poetic, archaic, or figurative language into plain literal prose — followed by a strict check that the translated meaning performs what the claim describes, not merely resembles its mood.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
This is where the exam tests literary reading inside the evidence framework: not "interpret the poem" but "verify that this line does the specific thing claimed." Quotation items skew toward poetry and prose fiction (unverified-claim, plausible — verify against bank). Difficulty comes from the language barrier: when students can't fully parse the lines, they fall back on emotional resonance, and the distractors are tuned to reward exactly that fallback.

## Mental model
**Translate, then check for enactment.** Every quotation gets rendered into flat modern prose before it is judged; then the claim is treated as a stage direction the quotation must literally perform. If the claim says the speaker *urges* something, the line must actually urge — a question can't urge. If the claim says a *contrast* is drawn, two things must actually be contrasted — and they must be the *right* two things. Mood-matching is not performing.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Pin the claim precisely, keeping its verb (urges, laments, contrasts, celebrates, addresses) and its participants (who speaks, to whom, about what) — these are the enactment conditions.
2. Before full translation, run **functional filters** on the choices — cheap structural cues that disqualify without deep reading: a quotation that asks a question cannot assert or urge; a first-person-plural line is not a speaker addressing another person; a neutral description cannot enact praise or criticism.
3. Translate the surviving quotations into literal prose. Untangle inverted syntax, resolve archaic pronouns and figures; if a word blocks understanding, decode it from context ([[evidence-forced-word-meaning]]).
4. Check enactment: does the translated line DO the claim's verb, with the claim's participants? For contrast claims, a pivot word (*but, yet*) is a useful first filter — then verify the two contrasted items are the ones the claim names.
5. Pick the quotation that performs all conditions; reject near-misses that perform the right act on the wrong subject or the wrong act in the right mood.

## Misconceptions
- **poetry-vibe-matching** — Students select the most emotionally resonant or thematically colored lines rather than translating literally. Formed because archaic/metaphoric language raises processing cost, and judgment-by-feel is the low-effort escape; also reinforced by classroom discussions that reward mood-sensitivity. Trap design: a gorgeous line in the claim's emotional register that performs a different act.
- **right-act-wrong-subject** — Students verify the speech act (yes, it praises) but not its object (it praises the *other* figure). Formed by treating the claim's verb as the whole condition and its participants as detail. Trap design: right sentiment aimed at the wrong party, or a line focusing on the speaker when the claim concerns the addressee.
- **pivot-word-suffices** — For contrast claims, students accept any quotation containing *but/yet/however* without checking what is contrasted. The filter gets mistaken for the test. Trap design: a pivot joining two items irrelevant to the claimed contrast.
- **translation-avoidance** — Students judge quotations from a skim of recognizable words, never producing a literal rendering, because translation feels slow. It is actually the fastest reliable path: untranslated lines make every distractor viable and force re-reads.

## Diagnostic indicators
- *poetry-vibe-matching*: wrong picks are the most lyrical/emotive options; student justifies with mood words ("it feels mournful") rather than a paraphrase; accuracy drops specifically on verse vs. prose quotations.
- *right-act-wrong-subject*: student's paraphrase is correct but their check stops at the verb; wrong picks share the claim's act with a swapped participant.
- *pivot-word-suffices*: on contrast claims, picks any *but/yet* option quickly; can't state the two contrasted items when asked.
- *translation-avoidance*: cannot paraphrase their chosen quotation when prompted; long total time with multiple re-reads of the same lines.

## Remediation pathways
- *poetry-vibe-matching* / *translation-avoidance*: make translation the entire exercise for a session — quotations only, no questions, rendered into flat prose aloud or on paper. Then reintroduce items with the rule "no judging an untranslated line." If translation itself fails on syntax, route to [[abstraction-ladder-paraphrase]] and complex-syntax work; if on vocabulary, [[evidence-forced-word-meaning]].
- *right-act-wrong-subject*: enactment checklist — verb ✓, subject ✓, object ✓ — applied to the final two choices; contrast pair of quotations differing only in participants.
- *pivot-word-suffices*: upgrade the filter to a two-step: pivot found → name both sides of the contrast and match them to the claim's two sides. One drill set of pivot-bearing decoys usually suffices.

## Mastery criteria
The student produces a literal paraphrase of any offered quotation and picks by enactment conditions, not resonance. Proof: accuracy on hard verse items where the correct quotation is plainer-sounding than a lyrical distractor, with paraphrases and condition-checks verbalizable on review.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: modern prose quotations; claim verb obvious; distractors off-topic.
- **Medium**: 19th-century prose or accessible verse; functional filters eliminate one or two choices; right-act-wrong-subject distractors appear.
- **Hard**: dense verse with inversion and archaism; claims with compound conditions (an attitude AND its object AND a shift); all four quotations in the right emotional register, separable only by literal enactment — composes with [[evidence-forced-word-meaning]] decoding and syntax untangling.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: build the translation habit on non-test material (single archaic sentences → modern English), celebrating flat, unpoetic renderings. Explicitly authorize "boring paraphrase" — many students think they're supposed to preserve the poetry.
- **Average**: teach the functional filters as the speed tool (question ≠ urging; we ≠ addressing someone; description ≠ evaluation) and the enactment frame. Their errors are mostly vibe picks under time; filters give them a defensible fast path.
- **Advanced**: participant discipline (who/whom) and compound-condition claims; drill items whose distractors all pass the mood check.
- **1500+**: speed translation of hard verse, plus adversarial review: for each distractor, name the single enactment condition it fails. At this band the only losses are rushed non-translations, so the work is keeping the ritual under pressure.
