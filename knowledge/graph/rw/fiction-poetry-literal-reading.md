# Fiction and poetry: aggressive literalism where instinct says "interpret"

## What it is
The reading stance for literary passages — excerpted fiction (often older), and poetry. Fiction passages don't argue; they foreground a character's trait, desire, or situation. Poems may deliver their central idea through image and metaphor, so it may never be stated flatly — but every credited reading stays inside the poem's wording. In both genres the required stance is aggressive literalism: track who is involved, what they want, what actually happens, and what attitude the words themselves carry. Most student failure here is being insufficiently literal, not insufficiently deep.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Literary passages are the section's register-difficulty lever: the College Board can raise difficulty without raising logical depth by using 19th-century syntax, archaic vocabulary, or poetic compression. The difficulty driver in fiction/poetry is the LANGUAGE, not the reasoning (unverified-claim as a general pattern — consistent with observed items; verify across official forms). The construct probed: can the student extract literal content from unfamiliar registers without hallucinating meaning to fill comprehension gaps?

## Mental model
**Read fiction like a detective's case notes, not like an English essay.** The "point" of a fiction excerpt is a foregrounded human fact: SHE resents the arrangement; HE is torn between duty and ambition. Key information clusters at the excerpt's beginning and end (unverified-claim; consistent with short-excerpt construction — verify). Any framing blurb above the passage is evidence too — it can outright contradict a distractor's asserted relationship. For poems: find the TURN — where the poem divides (halves, a shift in address, tense, or image system) — and characterize each part; the relation between parts usually IS the point. The wall between reading and imagining is absolute: symbolism, larger themes, biographical meaning — all off-limits unless the words state them. When comprehension gaps appear (archaic diction), work around them from what IS readable ([[detail-lookup-protocol]]'s positive-identification rule) instead of filling them with invention.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Read any blurb/context line first — it names speaker, era, situation; it's free orientation and occasionally a distractor-killer.
2. Track the human ledger: who is present, what each wants, what changes between the excerpt's start and end.
3. Convert stylized language to plain paraphrase sentence by sentence where the question demands it — resolving inverted syntax and distant referents ([[reference-compression-tracking]] at its hardest).
4. For attitude/tone: collect the actual evaluative words used; match their temperature, not your impression ([[topic-point-attitude-reading]]).
5. For poems: mark the turn; state what each side does; expect the credited main-idea answer to describe the relation between the sides in modest, bounded language.
6. Grade choices by the words-on-page standard: anything importing symbolism, moral lessons, or unstated backstory dies regardless of how "literary" it sounds ([[supported-not-stated]]).

## Misconceptions
- **symbolism-hunting** — Assuming literary passages demand deep interpretation, so speculating past the text. Trained directly by English-class norms where thematic reaching earns praise. Produces plausible-but-unsupported picks dressed in literary vocabulary — the test manufactures these distractors specifically for over-readers.
- **difficulty-panic** — "I can't read old fiction/poetry" → shutdown or wholesale guessing. Formed by thin exposure to pre-modern registers; the student concludes the GENRE is unreadable when only scattered words are. Produces abandonment of items that were answerable from readable sentences.
- **modern-schema-import** — Reading period-specific social dynamics through modern assumptions (what a visit, an inheritance, or a formal address implies), producing confident misreadings of motive and attitude. Formed because inference habits are calibrated on contemporary text.
- **tone-inflation** — Upgrading quiet literary attitudes to dramatic ones (wistfulness → despair; fondness → passion). Formed because school essays reward strong claims about tone; feeds intensity-overshoot distractors ([[distractor-species-taxonomy]] species 3-adjacent).
- **poem-paraphrase-refusal** — Treating poems as unparaphrasable art rather than sentences with line breaks. The student never converts lines into plain claims, so they judge choices on mood alone. Formed by the mystique poetry carries in school culture.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **symbolism-hunting**: picks theme-heavy unsupported choices on fiction items; explanations invoke "represents," "symbolizes," "the deeper meaning."
- **difficulty-panic**: sharp accuracy drop + dwell spike confined to literary passages; skips/flags cluster there; self-report names the genre, not the item.
- **modern-schema-import**: motive/attitude misses specifically on period fiction; explanations cite unstated social logic.
- **tone-inflation**: consistent intensity overshoots on literary attitude items while science-passage attitude items are fine.
- **poem-paraphrase-refusal**: on poetry items, cannot restate a quoted line in plain words when asked; picks by mood-match.

## Remediation pathways
- symbolism-hunting: the words-on-page gate — every answer defense must point to specific words; contrast a credited literal choice with a beautiful unsupported one and show the grading standard. Reinforce with [[supported-not-stated]].
- difficulty-panic: graded register exposure — short 19th-century excerpts with two or three archaic words glossed, demonstrating the item was answerable around them; nonsense-word drills transfer here.
- modern-schema-import: "only what's stated" motive drills — list what the text asserts about a character vs. what the student inferred; the delta is the lesson.
- tone-inflation: temperature-matching drills — map evaluative words to mild/moderate/strong bins before seeing choices.
- poem-paraphrase-refusal: line-to-sentence conversion practice — rewrite each poem line as a plain sentence, then find the turn; the poem becomes prose with structure.

## Mastery criteria
The student's accuracy gap between literary and informational passages closes to near zero at each difficulty band, and their literary answer defenses cite words, not themes. Proof: performance on period-fiction and poetry items at the hard band, with symbolism-species picks extinguished.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: modern fiction, stated feelings, simple chronology.
- **Medium**: early-20th-century prose; attitude carried by word choice; one theme-bait distractor.
- **Hard**: 19th-century syntax with inverted clauses and distant referents; poems where the point lives in the turn's contrast; motives shown through action rather than statement — composing [[reference-compression-tracking]] (stylistically distant referents), [[topic-point-attitude-reading]] (unstated point synthesis), and vocabulary-in-context under archaic usage ([[evidence-forced-word-meaning]]).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: modern fiction only; the detective frame ("who wants what?"); explicit permission to skip decoding hard words. Delay poetry until fiction literalism is stable.
- **Average**: introduce period fiction with glossing that fades; tone-temperature bins; the blurb-first habit; poem turns on accessible poems.
- **Advanced**: unglossed period prose at pace; motive-through-action passages; poetry where the turn is subtle (a tense shift, an address shift).
- **1500+**: this is where the section's ceiling usually lives for strong students. Drill the rarest registers (dramatic monologue, older translated prose), and the discipline of BOUNDED inference on poems — the credited answer for an indirect poem is the most modest reading that covers the whole text, a genuinely fine judgment worth dedicated reps.
