# Two different jobs: completing given premises vs. weighing new evidence

## What it is
The Information & Ideas evidence questions split into two logically distinct tasks that look similar on the surface. **Premise completion** (the "logically completes the text" shape): every needed fact is already on the page, and your job is to state the one conclusion those premises force — you may add nothing. **Evidence weighing** (the "which finding, if true, would support/weaken" shape): the passage supplies a claim, and the choices supply *brand-new external facts* to be judged for their bearing on that claim — the choices are not supposed to be verifiable from the passage. Same argument machinery, opposite direction of fit.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
These are the two halves of real argumentation: deriving what follows from what you know, and updating a hypothesis when new data arrives. The College Board tests them separately, and the distinction is the single most consequential frame a student can hold for this whole cluster, because each task has a rule that is a *fatal error* in the other: adding outside information ruins a completion; demanding passage verification ruins an if-true evidence item.

## Mental model
**Closed room vs. open door.** Completion questions lock you in a room with the passage: the answer is already in the room, implied by what's there — anything you bring in from outside is contamination. Support/weaken "if true" questions open the door: each choice is a visitor bringing news, and your only question is "does this news help or hurt the claim?" — never "did the passage already tell me this?" Before working any item in this cluster, name which room you're in.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Read the stem and classify the task: "logically completes" → closed room; "which finding/statement/result, if true, would most directly support/weaken/illustrate" → open door.
2. In the **closed room**: gather the premises, derive the forced conclusion yourself before reading choices, and reject anything not entailed — however plausible ([[entailment-moves-and-boundaries]]).
3. Through the **open door**: isolate the claim, predict what *kind* of new fact would bear on it ([[directional-evidence-prediction]]), and evaluate each choice as hypothetically true — its truth is granted by the stem.
4. Either way, keep the two rulebooks separate: entailment discipline inside; relevance-and-direction judgment outside.
5. Watch for hybrids: some quantitative items are completions whose forced conclusion is a data reading — the closed room extends to include the graphic (the display counts as given premises).

## Misconceptions
- **one-task-illusion** — Students treat everything with the word "support" or an academic passage as the same "evidence question," applying one habit everywhere. Formed because prep materials and school assignments rarely distinguish the direction of fit. It produces both failure modes below, alternating unpredictably.
- **passage-verification-error** — On if-true items, students reject the correct answer because "the passage never says that." They've imported the closed-room rule into the open-door task. This misconception is well-documented in raw teaching material and shows up most on weaken items, where correct answers often contradict the passage's conclusion by design.
- **outside-knowledge-import** — On completion items, students accept a choice because it is true in the real world or "makes sense," importing the open-door generosity into the closed room. Trap writers plant factually reasonable statements with no textual warrant (see [[topical-vs-logical-relevance]] for the relevance version of this error).
- **hypothetical-discount** — Students on if-true items *evaluate the plausibility* of each choice ("would that really happen?") instead of granting it and judging its bearing. Formed by critical-thinking instruction that rewards skepticism about premises. It wastes time and causes rejection of correct-but-surprising findings.

## Diagnostic indicators
- *passage-verification-error*: student explains an elimination with "it's not in the passage" on an if-true item; timing shows them re-scanning the passage per choice instead of working from the claim.
- *outside-knowledge-import*: on completions, student picks the real-world-plausible distractor and defends it with domain knowledge ("well, funding usually does get cut...") rather than passage sentences.
- *one-task-illusion*: errors distribute across both families with no stable pattern; student can't articulate how the two stems differ when asked.
- *hypothetical-discount*: student verbally challenges the truth of choices ("but that study sounds fake"); long dwell time on if-true items.

## Remediation pathways
- *one-task-illusion*: teach the two stems side by side as a contrast pair — same passage, one completion stem, one if-true stem; show that the correct answers obey different rules. This single lesson usually collapses the other misconceptions.
- *passage-verification-error*: highlight the words "if true" and re-derive what they grant. Drill: five if-true items where every correct answer is absent from the passage, so verification provably fails as a strategy.
- *outside-knowledge-import*: counterexample — a completion whose real-world-true distractor is wrong; walk the entailment chain to show the licensed answer. Revisit [[entailment-moves-and-boundaries]] if they can't see why plausibility isn't enough.
- *hypothetical-discount*: rule statement plus a timed drill: "the stem already stamped every choice TRUE; your only question is direction and relevance."

## Mastery criteria
Given a shuffled set of completion and support/weaken items, the student classifies each by task within seconds and can state, before answering, which rulebook applies. Proof: no eliminations justified by "not in the passage" on if-true items, and no selections justified by outside plausibility on completions, across a hard-difficulty sample.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: stems state the task plainly; passages short; the wrong rulebook still often gets the right answer (which hides the misconception).
- **Medium**: the wrong rulebook starts costing points — completions gain real-world-plausible distractors; if-true items gain correct answers that contradict passage statements.
- **Hard**: hybrids and stacked demands — completions requiring a data reading ([[claim-anchored-data-reading]]); weaken items whose correct answer is both absent from and inconsistent with the passage; dense claims requiring [[argument-spine-decomposition]] before the task even starts.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach only the two-room metaphor at first — no technique. Have them sort stems into "closed room / open door" piles until classification is automatic. Most of their errors vanish with classification alone.
- **Average**: they classify correctly when prompted but not spontaneously. Install a pre-answer ritual: annotate each item C (complete) or E (evidence) before reading choices, for two weeks of practice.
- **Advanced**: attack the residual passage-verification reflex on weaken items — it survives longest because it feels rigorous. Use drills where the correct weakener flatly contradicts the passage's conclusion.
- **1500+**: hybrids and speed. Give quantitative completions and if-true items back to back; the differentiator at this band is switching rulebooks without hesitation and spotting when a graphic is inside the closed room.
