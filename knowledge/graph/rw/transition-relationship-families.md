# The three jobs a transition can do

## What it is
Every connective word or phrase performs one of exactly three logical jobs: it **continues** an idea (the next statement extends, illustrates, restates, emphasizes, likens, or sequences the same line of thought), it marks **cause and effect** (one statement follows from the other), or it marks **contrast** (the statements pull in different directions). There is no fourth family. Continuation further splits into sub-jobs — adding a point, giving an example, defining or restating, emphasizing, comparing for likeness, and ordering in time — and those sub-jobs are what separate the hard items from the easy ones.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Transition questions probe whether a reader tracks the logical relationship *between* statements, not just the meaning *within* them — the core of following an argument. They live in the Expression of Ideas block of each R&W module (after the vocabulary-led opening, before the closing notes questions). Transition items are among the most frequent writing types on every form. The claim that they run roughly 8–10 per test / 15–20% of the section is **unverified-claim** — check against our bank and official forms before the tutor quotes a number.

## Mental model
A transition is a **road sign between two statements** with only three possible messages: *keep going* (continuation), *this is where that road leads* (cause-effect), or *turn* (contrast). First read the sign's family; only if several signs share a family do you read the fine print (the sub-job). Two families carry an internal arrow that must also point the right way: cause-effect words differ on **which side is the cause** (some announce "here comes the result," others "here comes the reason"), and some contrast words carry an extra concession meaning ("despite that") beyond plain difference.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Recognize the blank is a connective → this is a logic question; assume all four choices are grammatically fine.
2. Determine the relationship between the two statements being joined (see [[transition-backward-anchoring]] for which two statements those are).
3. Name the family first: continue, cause-effect, or contrast.
4. If more than one choice survives at family level, name the sub-job ("I need an example-introducer, not a mere adder").
5. For a cause-effect pick, check the arrow: does this word mark the upcoming statement as the result or the reason? Reversed direction = wrong.
6. For contrast picks between two survivors, ask whether "despite the previous fact" is actually meant, or just "these differ."

## Misconceptions
- **within-family-deafness** — the student treats all continuers as interchangeable (adding ≈ exemplifying ≈ emphasizing). Forms because school instruction stops at "transition words connect ideas" and never separates the jobs. Produces errors on choice sets where all four options are same-family with different sub-jobs; the student picks whichever continuer they've seen most often.
- **cause-direction-blindness** — the student believes detecting causality is the whole task, so any causal word fits. Forms because causal words are taught as one undifferentiated list. Produces logically reversed sentences: a "here's the reason" word slotted where a "here's the result" word belongs. The test writes distractors specifically for this: a reversed-arrow causal word among the choices.
- **temporal-word-literalism** — the student reads time-flavored phrases (of the "at the same time" type) as pure sequence markers. In practice such phrases frequently function as contrast markers, and true sequence words require actual temporal order between the events. Produces confident wrong picks on items where a time-sounding word is doing contrast work or where no real chronology exists.
- **synonym-flattening** — the student assumes words in the same family are graded only by formality, not function, so they choose by register ("this one sounds more academic"). A cosmetic-preference heuristic imported from essay writing. Produces near-miss picks that match family but not sub-job.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **within-family-deafness**: wrong answers cluster on same-family distractors (a continuer chosen where a different continuer was right); accuracy is fine on cross-family items but collapses when all four choices are one family. When explaining, the student says the right and wrong choices "mean the same thing."
- **cause-direction-blindness**: the student picks the reversed-arrow causal distractor; reading their chosen sentence aloud they don't notice the logic runs backward. Accuracy on contrast/continuation items is normal.
- **temporal-word-literalism**: errors concentrated on items with a time-flavored choice; student justifies with "the events happen at the same time / in order."
- **synonym-flattening**: student's explanation appeals to tone or formality rather than logical job.

## Remediation pathways
- **within-family-deafness**: contrast pair — one sentence pair completed twice, once with an adder, once with an example-introducer, showing the second sentence must *be* an instance of the first for the example word to work. Then drill classification-only exercises (name the sub-job, no answer choices). If it doesn't land, the gap is in compressing each statement to a gist — revisit [[transition-predict-then-match]] step 3.
- **cause-direction-blindness**: re-derivation — have the student label each statement C (cause) or R (result) before touching the choices, then match the word's arrow to the labels. One minimal pair (same two statements, both causal words, only one direction valid) usually fixes it.
- **temporal-word-literalism**: counterexample — a passage where the time-flavored phrase clearly signals opposition with zero chronology; ask what job the word is actually doing.
- **synonym-flattening**: show two same-family words in identical frames where only one works; the discovery that "synonyms" diverge under load breaks the register heuristic.

## Mastery criteria
Given any pair of adjacent statements, the student can name the family in under ~10 seconds and the sub-job when asked — *before seeing choices* — and can articulate the arrow direction for causal pairs. Proof: consistent correct answers on all-four-same-family (hard) transition items across multiple sessions, plus correct family labels in classification-first drills.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: three choices from one family, one from another; family recognition alone solves it.
- **medium**: two families represented two-and-two; requires a confident family call plus one sub-job or direction check.
- **hard**: all four choices same-family with distinct sub-jobs, or a causal set where direction is the only discriminator, or a concession-vs-plain-contrast split. Hard items also compose in [[transition-backward-anchoring]] traps (mid-sentence placement with a plausible local reading). Note: truly fine same-family discrimination is claimed to be nearly absent from administered forms (**unverified-claim** — sub-job precision suffices in practice, but verify against our bank before de-emphasizing).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach only the three families with one clean minimal pair each; forbid answer choices entirely for the first sessions — every exercise is "name the family." Don't introduce sub-jobs until family calls are automatic.
- **Average**: introduce continuation sub-jobs as *jobs*, not word lists ("what would the second sentence have to be for an example word to fit?"). Add the causal-arrow check as a mandatory step.
- **Advanced**: run all-four-same-family sets and reversed-arrow traps; require a stated prediction before choices every time.
- **1500+**: focus on the two residual leak points — concession nuance and temporal-word contrast usage — and on speed: family call in one read, no re-reading. Note the connective-word-class side (which words take which punctuation) lives in [[connector-logic-families]]; keep the logic taxonomy and the grammar taxonomy as two separate lenses on the same words.
