# Sentence + fragment connectors — the other half of the boundary menu

## What it is
The legal menu for the OTHER clause count: a complete sentence attached to a
fragment (dependent clause, phrase, list, or lone descriptor). Here the menu
inverts: comma, colon, and dash are the working marks, while period and
semicolon are NEVER legal — both of those demand a full independent clause on
each side. One compressed rule covers half the cluster: full stops are
two-sentence marks; everything else tolerates a fragment on one side.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Real prose is mostly sentences with attachments, not sentence-sentence seams —
so the exam needs students to handle mixed seams, and it gets a full second
trap family from students who learned only the two-sentence menu. The
generative logic: a fragment cannot stand alone, so its mark must SUBORDINATE
it to a host (comma) or present it as the host's payoff (colon/dash) — it can
never be sealed off as if independent (period/semicolon). Classify the sides,
and the menu follows; that's why side-classification, not mark knowledge, is
what these items actually measure.

## Mental model
**Classify each side of the proposed mark, then match the mark's demands:**
- **period / semicolon:** sentence + sentence, no exceptions;
- **colon / dash:** sentence BEFORE it (a complete launch); after it, either a
  sentence (an explanation) or a fragment (a list, a definition, a payoff);
- **comma:** tolerates sentence + fragment, when something licenses the
  attachment.
The licensed comma attachments:
- **Dependent clause first:** subordinator clause + comma + independent clause
  (*Although the vote failed, the coalition held.*). When the independent
  clause leads, the comma is normally omitted — except before strong-contrast
  subordinators (the although/even-though/whereas family), where it stays.
- **One connector only:** between two clauses, exactly one joining word may do
  the job. *Although the vote failed, but the coalition held* stacks two
  demoters and leaves no main clause standing — the sentence has no assertion
  left. This is the one-connector rule, and the exam hides the first connector
  far from the blank ([[window-widening]]).
- **Participial tail:** comma + -ing/-ed phrase attaches extra action to the
  clause (*The coalition held, surprising every analyst.*). The comma is what
  marks the phrase as modifying the clause rather than gluing to the nearest
  word. A *with + noun + -ing* tail is a legal equivalent of "and + verb"; the
  exam won't force a choice between the two equivalents (unverified-claim).
- **which-tail:** a *which*-clause is always dependent and hangs off a comma;
  bare attachment (no comma) is a boundary error.
- **Colon-launch completeness:** whatever precedes a colon must pass the
  sentence test. Corollary shortcut: a colon immediately after a
  *such as / including / for example* lead-in is nearly always wrong — those
  words leave the launch incomplete. Colon vs dash in the explainer role is
  pure style; the exam does not pit correct colon against correct dash
  (unverified-claim).

## Expert reasoning process
1. Count first ([[clause-counting]]); confirm this is a sentence+fragment seam,
   not two sentences.
2. Sweep the WHOLE sentence for connectors already present (a subordinator at
   the very start, a coordinator after the blank). One connector max; if one
   exists, choices adding a second die instantly, and the punctuation-only
   choice usually wins.
3. Classify each side of each candidate mark: full sentence or fragment. Kill
   period/semicolon on any fragment side; kill colon/dash on any incomplete
   launch.
4. Check what licenses a surviving comma: leading dependent clause,
   participial tail, which-tail, strong-contrast subordinator.
5. Watch the pronoun-insertion trap: a choice adding *it/they* after the comma
   upgrades the fragment to a clause — and the comma to a splice
   ([[comma-splice]]).

## Misconceptions
- **semicolon-as-strong-comma** — Students deploy semicolons before any
  afterthought because school feedback rewarded semicolons without teaching
  the two-sentence requirement. Trap design: a semicolon choice before an
  obviously fragmentary tail. Same root as the version in
  [[three-legal-joins]]; here it's harvested on the fragment side.
- **contrast-content-means-but** — Contrasting ideas near the blank trigger a
  reflexive *but/however*, even when the sentence already opened with
  *although*. Root: matching connectors to local MEANING instead of
  whole-sentence STRUCTURE — the one-connector rule was never installed. The
  interrupter-camouflaged version is the signature hard item.
- **comma-optional-after-lead-in** — Speech doesn't always pause after a
  leading dependent clause, so the required comma gets dropped. Pure
  [[punctuating-by-ear]] residue in the false-negative direction.
- **colon-needs-list / any-list-earns-a-colon** — Two opposite faulty schemas
  from learning colons by example: some students only accept colons before
  lists; others accept a colon anywhere a list follows, including mid-clause
  after *such as*. The completeness-before rule replaces both.
- **dash-is-informal-error** — Dashes were discouraged in school essays, so
  students eliminate correct dash choices for register reasons. Needs explicit
  inoculation: a single dash legally plays the colon's role, and may also mark
  a deliberate interruption.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **semicolon-as-strong-comma:** semicolon picks where one side fails the
  sentence test; the student classifies the sides correctly when asked
  (counts-but-illegal-mark profile).
- **contrast-content-means-but:** adds a second connector on items whose stem
  already contains one; misses spike when an interrupter separates the
  original connector from the blank; explanations cite the meaning relation,
  never the existing connector.
- **comma-optional-after-lead-in:** drops the comma after fronted dependent
  clauses; error disappears when the dependent clause is short (no pause
  pull) — a length-sensitivity tell.
- **colon schemas:** rejects clause-explainer colons ("colons are for lists"),
  or accepts the such-as colon; both verbalize list-based reasoning.
- **dash-is-informal-error:** eliminates dash choices immediately with
  register language ("too casual"); accuracy jumps when the same structure is
  shown with a colon.

## Remediation pathways
- **semicolon-as-strong-comma:** the two-sentence stamp — before any
  semicolon, both sides must independently pass the sentence test
  ([[sentence-vs-fragment]]); drill with fragment tails until reflexive.
- **contrast-content-means-but:** connector census ritual — before reading
  choices, sweep the full sentence and count connectors already present.
  Drill on stems where the subordinator opens the sentence and the blank sits
  30 words later.
- **comma-optional-after-lead-in:** teach the comma's job as marking where the
  setup ends and the assertion begins (a structural signpost, not a breath);
  contrast pairs with and without the comma.
- **colon schemas:** the launch test — cover everything after the colon and
  ask "is what's left a sentence?"; then the such-as shortcut as a fast kill.
- **dash-is-informal-error:** show official-register prose using single
  dashes; state the exam meta plainly (dash = colon here; style is not
  tested).

## Mastery criteria
Given any candidate mark, the student can classify both sides (sentence /
fragment) and give the verdict from the inverted menu in one pass; runs the
connector census unprompted on long stems. Proof: consistent kills of
semicolon+fragment and such-as-colon distractors at medium, and correct
punctuation-only picks on double-connector items at hard.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy:** leading dependent clause with the seam at the blank; semicolon +
  obvious fragment as the decoy.
- **Medium:** participial and which-tails; colon-launch checks; the
  strong-contrast subordinator comma.
- **Hard:** the double-connector design escalated by distance — the opening
  conjunction scrolls out of the attention window behind an interrupter, or
  appears AFTER the blank so the student must read ahead ([[window-widening]],
  [[delete-and-reread]]); pronoun-insertion choices converting the seam type
  mid-item; choice sets differing only in punctuation around one seam word
  (unverified-claim that this is a standard real-item shape).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling:** teach the single compression first — period/semicolon need
  two sentences; comma/colon/dash can touch a fragment — and drill ONLY
  side-classification until stable.
- **Average:** add the license list for commas and the colon launch test;
  introduce the one-connector rule with short stems before camouflaged ones.
- **Advanced:** run the escalation series: same carrier sentence with the
  connector near the blank, then hidden behind an interrupter, then after the
  blank. Teaches the trap as one architecture in three costumes.
- **1500+:** adversarial sets mixing manufactured splices, double connectors,
  and least-punctuated-correct keys (unverified-claim on that bias —
  see [[punctuating-by-ear]]); require structural justification aloud in
  under 30 seconds per item.
