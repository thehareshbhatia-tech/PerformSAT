# Rhetorical role identification — answering WHY it's there, not WHAT it says

## What it is
The skill of assigning a sentence or phrase its *job* in the surrounding argument: introduce, illustrate, define, explain, compare, contrast, qualify, concede, emphasize, speculate, pivot, conclude. Comprehending content is necessary but not sufficient — function is a second layer computed over content. The signals of a sentence's job are largely structural: head-of-sentence connectives, punctuation (colon/dash = an explanation follows; paired dashes/parentheses = a qualifying aside; repetition and typographic stress = emphasis), and position in the paragraph.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Text Structure & Purpose stems — "the function of the underlined sentence," "the portion serves to..." — appear regularly in every module at both local (one sentence's job) and whole-passage grain (unverified-claim on frequency; verify against bank). The item family exists because school reading assessments reward restatement, so "what do the lines say?" is the default question every student silently answers. The test probes whether a reader tracks argumentative *architecture*: which sentences are load-bearing claims, which are support, which redirect. Questions cluster at idea-contact points — where new information enters or the focus pivots (unverified-claim).

## Mental model
**Every sentence is an employee with a job title.** Content is what the employee *says*; function is what they were *hired to do* for the paragraph. The job title is almost always readable from uniform and position — connectives, punctuation, paragraph slot — rather than from topic. The felt confusion "the answer choices seem unrelated to the lines" is the tell that you answered WHAT when the test asked WHY: the choices describe jobs, and you were looking for content.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Classify the stem's grain: local function vs. whole-passage purpose (the latter belongs to [[point-vs-purpose-abstraction]]). Rephrase the stem in plain language: "why does the author bring this up?"
2. Read the referenced material for content, then widen the window ([[context-window-discipline]]) — the claim a detail serves usually sits BEFORE the reference (unverified-claim on the skew, but the direction to check first).
3. Inventory the structural signals: head connective and its family ([[connector-logic-families]]), colon/dash structures, hedging parentheticals, emphasis marks, attitude-bearing word choices.
4. Name the served claim: usually the nearest preceding general statement. State the function in your own words as verb + object — "expands the contrast," "explains the mechanism," "concedes a limitation" — BEFORE reading choices.
5. Screen choices by polarity and degree ([[polarity-first-elimination]]): tone mismatches and extreme verbs (proves, demolishes, ridicules) go first — measured evidence-weighing prose rarely earns them (unverified-claim as a frequency assertion; sound as a prior).
6. Verify survivors word-by-word: one wrong relational noun ("differences" where the text draws a likeness) invalidates an otherwise attractive choice. Gist-level matching is how the one-wrong-word distractor wins.
7. If the first hypothesis fails against every choice, read further and rebuild — hypothesis failure is a normal branch of the method, not a breakdown.

## Misconceptions
- **function-equals-content** — The student answers "what the lines say," because every prior reading assessment rewarded restatement. The single most important misconception in this domain. Produces attraction to choices that accurately *summarize* the referenced lines while mis-stating their role — a distractor species built precisely for this reader.
- **answer-lives-inside-the-lines** — Belief that the referenced range is self-sufficient (shared with [[context-window-discipline]], where its full treatment lives). Here it produces after-the-reference distractor picks and inability to name the served claim.
- **vivid-vehicle-literalism** — Treating an analogy or comparison as the topic itself ("compares offices to theaters") rather than as a device serving an explanation. Forms because the analogy is the most vivid, memorable content on the page — salience masquerades as importance. Produces picks that name the comparison's vehicle as the purpose.
- **proximity-association** — Assuming two things mentioned near each other stand in the asserted relationship (items located IN a place read as belonging TO the institution). Forms because spatial adjacency in text mimics logical connection. Exploited by choices that fuse adjacent sentences into a claim the passage never makes.
- **stuck-ritual-guessing** — When WHY-analysis is missing, students substitute superstition (picking a "statistically lucky" letter). Not a reasoning error but a coping ritual that marks the absence of the entire skill — diagnostic of needing this node from the ground up.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **function-equals-content**: picks the accurate-summary-wrong-role distractor; when asked to explain, retells the lines' content and never uses a function verb; reports "the choices seemed unrelated to the passage."
- **answer-lives-inside-the-lines**: picks choices built from material just after the reference; cannot point to the claim the detail serves.
- **vivid-vehicle-literalism**: on analogy items, picks the choice naming the comparison itself; explanation dwells on the vivid image.
- **proximity-association**: picks fused-adjacent-sentences distractors; paraphrases the passage with a relationship word ("because," "belongs to") the text never licensed.
- **stuck-ritual-guessing**: answer-letter patterns on function items; instant response times after long stem-dwell; says "when I don't know, I always pick C."

## Remediation pathways
- **function-equals-content**: name the category error out loud — "you answered WHAT; the question asked WHY." Drill the two-question exercise: for one sentence, write both the WHAT (paraphrase) and the WHY (verb + object job title); repeat until the layers separate. If the WHAT itself is shaky, drop back to [[abstraction-ladder-paraphrase]].
- **answer-lives-inside-the-lines**: remediate via [[context-window-discipline]]; here, add the specific habit of naming the served claim before choices.
- **vivid-vehicle-literalism**: contrast pair — same analogy serving two different explanations in two passages; the vehicle is constant, the job changes, so the job can't BE the vehicle. Ask "what would be lost if the analogy were deleted?" — the loss is the function.
- **proximity-association**: dissect one fused-sentences distractor: show each half is real, the asserted link is invented. Rule: relationships must be *stated or signaled*, never inferred from adjacency.
- **stuck-ritual-guessing**: no shortcut fixes a missing skill — restart the node at easy difficulty with untimed, scaffolded items (mark the connector; name the served claim; then answer).

## Mastery criteria
Given any referenced sentence, the student can state its job as verb + object in their own words BEFORE viewing choices, name the specific claim it serves (pointing at it), and reject accurate-summary distractors — demonstrated by consistent performance on medium/hard local-function items including analogy and concession cases.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: the job is signaled by an explicit connective adjacent to the reference; the served claim is the previous sentence; choices differ grossly.
- **Medium**: served claim two sentences up or at the paragraph head; punctuation-only signals; one accurate-summary distractor and one after-the-reference distractor.
- **Hard**: concessions and qualifications (jobs that run *against* the author's main direction — requires [[tone-attitude-reading]]); analogies serving non-obvious explanations; one-wrong-word choices demanding word-level verification; hypothesis-failure items where the natural first reading of the job is wrong. Composes with [[point-vs-purpose-abstraction]] when local function must be placed inside whole-passage purpose (wrong-altitude traps).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: begin with the felt experience — "ever feel like the choices have nothing to do with the passage?" — then reveal the WHAT/WHY layer split. Use everyday arguments (a persuasive text message) before test passages; job titles for sentences is an easy game.
- **Average**: build the signal inventory (connectives by family, punctuation jobs) and the verb + object habit; introduce per-distractor postmortems naming the species.
- **Advanced**: concession/qualification jobs, word-by-word verification against one-wrong-word choices, and the hypothesis-failure recovery branch as an explicit drill.
- **1500+**: run the method on dense scholarly prose and period fiction; practice wrong-altitude discrimination (this sentence's job vs. the passage's purpose); have them write one accurate-summary distractor for a real item — the ability to build the trap is the strongest evidence they see the layer.
