# Tone and attitude reading — separating the subject from the author's stance toward it

## What it is
The ability to compute an author's attitude — admiring, skeptical, neutral-descriptive, cautiously optimistic, critical-but-conceding — as a quantity distinct from the passage's subject matter. Stance lives in evaluative word choices, hedges and intensifiers, concessive markers, scare quotes, and what the author chooses to question versus assert. It is the raw input that [[polarity-first-elimination]] spends and that cross-text relationship work depends on.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Attitude is rarely the direct question on the digital SAT; it is the *hidden variable* inside purpose questions (answer verbs carry tone), cross-text questions (the relationship between authors is largely a tone relationship), and hard words-in-context items (the required word must match the author's stance). The exam's house style matters here: its passages are measured, evidence-weighing academic prose. Unverified-claim: extreme stances (ridicule, contempt, definitive proof) are rare in credited readings, making extremity a usable elimination signal — verify against official items and our bank before the tutor asserts it as fact.

## Mental model
**Two dials, not one.** Every passage has a *topic dial* (what is discussed — can be grim, thrilling, dry) and a *stance dial* (how the author regards it — a small set of calibrated positions: strong approval, qualified approval, neutral description, qualified doubt, strong criticism). The dials move independently: an author can write neutrally about catastrophe or critically about something pleasant. Only the stance dial answers SAT questions. Read the stance dial from *evaluative* words — the words that would change if the author's opinion changed — not from topic words.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Strip the topic: ask "if I deleted every fact, what opinion residue remains?"
2. Collect stance carriers: evaluative adjectives/adverbs (striking, dubious, elegant), verbs of endorsement or doubt (demonstrates vs. claims vs. purports), hedges (may, appears, in part), concessives (admittedly, to be sure), scare quotes, rhetorical questions.
3. Place the stance on the calibrated scale — including *mixed* positions (grants X, doubts Y), which are their own value, not an average.
4. Attribute correctly: a stance inside quoted or reported material belongs to the cited party, not the author — the author's attitude is in how they *frame* the citation (note verbs like "insists," "concedes," "assumes").
5. Check degree separately from direction: skeptical ≠ dismissive; admiring ≠ celebratory.

## Misconceptions
- **topic-tone-conflation** — The student reads a passage about extinction or disease as "negative" because the subject is sad. Forms because emotional response to content is automatic while stance-tracking is effortful. Produces wrong-polarity eliminations downstream in [[polarity-first-elimination]] and wrong-relationship calls in cross-text items.
- **binary-tone** — The student's tone vocabulary has two values, positive and negative, so qualified and mixed stances get rounded to a pole. Forms from "is the tone positive or negative?" schooling. Produces elimination of concession-shaped correct answers ("acknowledges the benefit but warns...") for not being negative enough — the same failure that drives disagreement-means-total-opposition in paired texts.
- **quoting-is-endorsing** — The student attributes any stated view to the author, missing that authors routinely present a view in order to complicate or reject it. Forms because single-voice reading is the school default. Produces answers crediting the author with the position the passage exists to push against; test writers bait this with a distractor restating the reported view.
- **intensity-blindness** — Direction read correctly, degree ignored: "critical" and "contemptuous" treated as interchangeable. Produces too-extreme picks on purpose questions (mocks, disproves) where the passage merely questions.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **topic-tone-conflation**: wrong-polarity errors clustered on passages with emotionally loaded subjects but neutral stances; explanation cites facts, not framing words.
- **binary-tone**: eliminates grants-then-cautions choices; when asked the author's attitude, answers with a bare "positive" or "negative" even for hedged passages.
- **quoting-is-endorsing**: picks distractors restating a view the passage introduces then rejects; misses the pivot at "yet recent evidence...".
- **intensity-blindness**: picks extreme-verb purpose answers (ridicules, proves) on measured passages; cannot rank "questions / criticizes / mocks" by strength.

## Remediation pathways
- **topic-tone-conflation**: the deletion drill — remove all factual content and list what's left; if nothing evaluative remains, the stance is neutral regardless of how the topic feels. Contrast pair: same facts wrapped in admiring vs. doubtful framing.
- **binary-tone**: expand the scale to five-plus calibrated positions and require placing every practice passage on it; teach "mixed" as a first-class value. If it doesn't land, prereq is vocabulary: hedge and concession words as a mini-lexicon.
- **quoting-is-endorsing**: highlight attribution verbs; drill "whose voice is this sentence in?" line by line on a view-then-rebuttal passage. Revisit [[two-voice-separation]] territory early — this is its single-text precursor.
- **intensity-blindness**: verb-ladder exercise — order stance verbs by strength (notes < questions < challenges < rejects < ridicules); then match passages to the strongest verb they *earn*.

## Mastery criteria
Given a passage, the student can state the stance in a calibrated phrase ("cautiously optimistic about X while unconvinced by Y"), point to the two or three words carrying it, and correctly attribute embedded views to their owners — proven by disappearance of wrong-polarity and too-extreme picks across purpose and cross-text items at medium/hard difficulty.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: overt evaluative vocabulary; single consistent stance; modern prose.
- **Medium**: hedged stances; one embedded reported view; stance carried by verb choice rather than adjectives.
- **Hard**: mixed stances with a genuine concession; irony or understatement; 19th-century prose where evaluative conventions differ; stance divergence between two texts that agree on facts (feeding [[cross-text-relationship-taxonomy]]). Composes with [[point-vs-purpose-abstraction]] when the answer requires matching a rhetorical verb's tone to the passage's earned intensity.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: start with exaggerated contrast pairs (openly glowing vs. openly hostile treatments of one topic); build the two-dials distinction before any subtlety.
- **Average**: hedge/concession lexicon, the five-position scale, attribution verbs; drill on science prose where stances are quiet.
- **Advanced**: mixed stances and reported-view passages; train degree calibration with verb ladders.
- **1500+**: irony, understatement, period prose, and stance divergence across paired texts; have the student rewrite a neutral passage into a subtly skeptical one to internalize which words carry stance.
