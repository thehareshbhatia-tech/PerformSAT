# Topic → point → attitude: reading a passage as an argument

## What it is
The three-layer grip an expert takes on any passage: the **topic** (the subject referenced most often, counting rephrasings through pronouns and compression-nouns), the **point** (topic + the claim being made about it — the answer to "so what?"), and the **attitude** (how the author feels about the claim). The section's true construct is not "did you absorb the text" but "can you see the text as an argument": locate claims, see how evidence supports them, judge what follows. Literal comprehension is the floor; argument comprehension is the target.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Central-ideas items ask for the point directly; structure and function items ask what job each part does FOR the point; completion, evidence, and paired-text items all require the point as the standard against which additions and evidence are judged. A crisply held point is also the elimination engine: you cannot recognize an off-topic or detail-promotion distractor without one. This mode of reading — rank information, compress to the claim — is the actual college skill the section proxies.

## Mental model
**Topic + "so what?" = point.** Three graded artifacts, not one:
- Topic: the most-referenced subject, at the RIGHT granularity — one specific artist is not "artists"; one practice is not its category. Precision here is what makes off-topic and scope distractors visible.
- Point: an argument, not a subject label and not a theme word. "It's about coral bleaching" is a topic; "warming events bleach coral faster than reefs can recover" is a point.
- Attitude: direction and intensity of the author's stance (enthusiastic, cautious, skeptical), held at the passage's actual temperature.
Also hold the mode distinction: **describing** (recounting everything in order) vs. **arguing** (compressing to the one claim the author wants to land). Only the second is usable under time pressure. Each sentence gets read with two questions: what does it SAY, and what JOB does it do (claim, support, concession, pivot, illustration) — the job, not the content, answers structure/function questions ([[rhetorical-role-identification]]).

## Expert reasoning process
1. Read the first sentence expecting the topic; confirm by noticing what keeps getting re-referenced, counting pronouns and compression-nouns ([[reference-compression-tracking]]).
2. Ask "so what?" — scan for the claim, prioritizing sentence one, the final sentence, and emphasis machinery ([[emphasis-signal-map]]). unverified-claim: on ~100-word passages the point sits disproportionately in the first sentence, secondarily the last — validate against official short passages.
3. If hedged-belief language opens the passage, pre-load the old/new template ([[belief-revision-template]]).
4. Jot the point in shorthand and CONSULT the jot while eliminating ([[shorthand-note-discipline]]).
5. Eliminate choices that are off-topic, contradict the jot, promote a detail to headline status, or overshoot the passage's intensity. Expect the credited choice to restate the key sentence in slightly more general synonyms ([[abstraction-ladder-paraphrase]]). unverified-claim: the credited main-idea answer is sometimes the ONLY choice that mentions the topic at all.
6. For overall-structure stems (long two/three-part narrations): recap the passage's action in your own words FIRST, then verify one checkable segment of each choice (commonly what it says about the ending) and eliminate on that segment alone — never adjudicate four whole narrations holistically.

## Misconceptions
- **topic-as-answer** — Asked for the main idea, the student supplies a subject label. School rewards naming topics ("what is this about?"), so subject-naming feels complete. Produces attraction to on-topic-but-claim-free choices and theme-word choices; feeds detail-promotion picks because with no point held, any true statement looks central.
- **every-detail-matters** — Years of close-reading instruction make careful students audit every odd phrase, losing the argument while inspecting the wallpaper. Shows up as slow pacing plus detail-fixated wrong answers. The transferable skill is the opposite: ranked-importance reading that treats skimming-by-design as the rigorous option.
- **vague-topic-drift** — Holding the topic a notch too broad (the category instead of the member). Even strong students do it. The test exploits it with distractors that pluralize a singular or generalize the case to the class ([[distractor-species-taxonomy]] species 2).
- **summary-equals-retelling** — Believing summarizing means recounting everything in order, because that's what "summarize" meant in school. Produces no usable claim to eliminate against and burns the clock.
- **strong-reader-overconfidence** — Fluent readers hold it all in their heads because they "remember most things"; on belief-revision passages a half-remembered map flips polarity and they confidently pick the OPPOSITE of the point (contradiction-in-costume, species 9).

## Diagnostic indicators
- **topic-as-answer**: picks claim-free on-topic choices; when asked "what's the main idea?" verbally responds with "it's about X."
- **every-detail-matters**: high per-item dwell; misses are detail-promotion picks; strong accuracy on detail-lookup items with weak accuracy on main-idea items over the same passages.
- **vague-topic-drift**: consistent scope-inflation picks specifically on main-idea stems.
- **summary-equals-retelling**: think-alouds produce chronological recaps; structure-question misses where the chosen narration mixes primary and secondary beats.
- **strong-reader-overconfidence**: fast, high accuracy on easy/medium, with polarity-flip misses on old/new passages; no jotting behavior in telemetry.

## Remediation pathways
- topic-as-answer: define-by-negation drill — for one passage, write the topic, a theme word, and the point side by side; the student learns the point must contain a VERB with a direction. Gate: they can convert "about X" into "X does/is Y" on demand.
- every-detail-matters: three-level compression exercise — full recap → one-sentence argument → ≤5-word shorthand, on the same passage; makes compression a spectrum they can feel. Reframe skimming as ranking, not laziness.
- vague-topic-drift: granularity ladder — "Is this about art, artists, this artist, or this artist's residency?" — pick the rung the passage actually occupies.
- summary-equals-retelling: ban chronology — summaries may not contain "then"; force claim-first phrasing.
- strong-reader-overconfidence: show them their own polarity flip on a missed old/new item; prescribe the two-sided jot (old vs. new) on template passages only — a minimal externalization even a proud reader will accept.

## Mastery criteria
For any passage, the student can produce topic (right granularity), point (claim with a verb), and attitude (direction + intensity) in under 20 seconds, and their main-idea/structure eliminations cite the point. Proof: sustained accuracy on central-ideas and structure items at medium/hard, with species-2/9/11 picks extinguished.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: point stated flatly in sentence one; distractors loudly off-topic.
- **Medium**: point assembled from first + last sentences; one detail-promotion and one scope distractor survive a first pass.
- **Hard**: point unstated and must be synthesized from two halves; attitude carried by quiet lexical choices; structure narrations differing only in their final clause; fiction/poetry variants where "point" means a character's foregrounded want or the poem's turn ([[fiction-poetry-literal-reading]]); composes with [[belief-revision-template]] and [[reference-compression-tracking]] (the point's restatement is a compression-noun).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: one formula, drilled: point = topic + so-what. Practice on three-sentence micro-passages where the point is always sentence one. Delay attitude entirely.
- **Average**: full three-layer grip plus describe-vs-argue; introduce the structure-question segment-check move; two-sided jots on template passages.
- **Advanced**: unstated-point synthesis, attitude temperature calibration (mild vs. strong), and per-choice adjudication discipline — name why the right one is right AND give each distractor a specific diagnosis.
- **1500+**: speed and invisibility — the three layers should form during the first read with no separate step; work the rare shapes (point in the middle, genuine counterargument after the pivot, double-claim passages) where the standard priors misfire.
