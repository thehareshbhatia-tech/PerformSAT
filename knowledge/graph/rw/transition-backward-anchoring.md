# What a transition actually points at

## What it is
A transition connects its host sentence **backward to the previous sentence**, no matter where it physically sits — sentence-initial (followed by a comma), mid-sentence (between two commas), or sentence-final (after a comma). All three placements mean exactly the same thing; position is stylistic, not semantic. The one genuine exception: a transition preceded by exactly **one** comma inside a sentence may be linking that sentence's own two clauses — then the comparison happens within the sentence.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
This is the invariant the test leans on to make medium and hard transition items. An easy item puts the blank at the start of a sentence, where intuition and the rule agree. A harder item buries the blank mid-sentence, where intuition ("connectors glue what they touch") points at the wrong pair of statements — and the test supplies a distractor that fits the wrong pair beautifully. Testing placement is how the same taxonomy gets escalated without harder vocabulary.

## Mental model
A transition is a **hook thrown backward**: wherever it rides in its sentence, it grips the previous sentence. The verification move is **delete-and-compare**: strike the transition, state the two bare statements separately, and name their relationship from content alone. If the two fragments flanking a mid-sentence transition make no standalone sense on their own, that is the proof the hook reaches back to the previous sentence rather than joining the fragments.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Find the blank's punctuation frame: start of sentence + comma, between two commas, after one comma, or sentence-final.
2. Default scope: host sentence ↔ previous sentence. Only the single-comma case triggers a scope check.
3. In the single-comma case, test whether the material before the comma stands as its own complete statement (this is where [[sentence-vs-fragment]] pays off). If yes, the two halves of the sentence are the statements to compare; if no, revert to the previous sentence.
4. Delete the transition mentally and read the two in-scope statements bare. Name their relationship ([[transition-relationship-families]]).
5. Ignore any *following* sentence entirely unless the blank is sentence-final in the paragraph's flow — the connection still runs backward.

## Misconceptions
- **proximity-scope-error** — the student believes a mid- or end-of-sentence transition connects the words physically adjacent to it: the host sentence's own halves, or whatever clause it touches. Forms from the visual intuition that connectors glue their neighbors, reinforced by school examples that are always sentence-initial. Produces confident wrong answers on mid-sentence items where the local reading is plausible but the previous-sentence relationship demands a different family — the test's favorite trap at this skill.
- **forward-anchoring** — the student links the transition to the *next* sentence ("it's setting up what comes after"). Forms from topic-sentence instruction ("transitions preview the paragraph"). Produces errors whenever the forward relationship differs from the backward one; the student's explanation cites the following sentence as evidence.
- **single-comma-blindness** — the student has learned "always look at the previous sentence" as an absolute and misses the intra-sentence case, comparing the wrong pair when the transition genuinely joins the sentence's own clauses. Forms as an over-generalization of the (correct) main rule right after it's taught. Produces a characteristic error spike immediately after backward-anchoring instruction.
- **placement-changes-meaning** — the student believes moving a transition from the front to the middle of a sentence changes what it means or what it connects, and so re-derives (or second-guesses) the relationship per placement. Forms from prosody: mid-sentence interrupters *sound* parenthetical and therefore weaker. Costs time and invites twisting rather than a specific distractor.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **proximity-scope-error**: wrong answers occur disproportionately on mid-sentence/between-two-commas items while sentence-initial accuracy is fine; the chosen distractor correctly names the relationship between the host sentence's two halves. When asked "which two statements does this connect?", the student points inside one sentence.
- **forward-anchoring**: the student's justification quotes the following sentence; errors vanish when the item is the paragraph's last sentence.
- **single-comma-blindness**: errors concentrated on single-comma items only, appearing *after* the student has otherwise improved; their explanation recites "I compared it to the previous sentence."
- **placement-changes-meaning**: slow times on mid-sentence items with correct final answers, or answer-changing behavior; explanation includes "because it's in the middle, it's more of an aside."

## Remediation pathways
- **proximity-scope-error**: the delete-and-compare demonstration is the fix — strike the transition and show the two flanking fragments are not standalone statements, so they cannot be what's being related. Then one contrast pair: identical passage, blank moved from sentence-initial to mid-sentence, same answer both times. If it doesn't land, drill "find the two statements" as its own exercise before any answer choices.
- **forward-anchoring**: cover the following sentence with a card (or truncate the passage) and show the item still resolves; then show a case where the forward reading picks the distractor.
- **single-comma-blindness**: teach the exception as a two-question checklist ("one comma or two? does the pre-comma chunk stand alone?"); revisit [[sentence-vs-fragment]] if the student can't make the stand-alone call.
- **placement-changes-meaning**: show the same transition in all three positions with identical meaning; the goal is to collapse placement into a non-signal so attention goes to content.

## Mastery criteria
Given any transition item, the student names *which two statements* are being connected before naming the relationship — correctly, including single-comma exceptions — and can demonstrate delete-and-compare on demand. Proof: parity between sentence-initial and mid-sentence item accuracy (the gap between them is the misconception's signature), sustained across sessions at medium+ difficulty.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: sentence-initial blank; anchoring is invisible because intuition agrees with the rule.
- **medium**: mid-sentence between two commas, with choices that all fit grammatically; requires deliberate backward scoping. Sentence-final placement is a mild variant.
- **hard**: mid-sentence placement where the local (host-sentence-only) reading supports a specific wrong family — a designed trap; or single-comma intra-sentence items; or subordinating-conjunction variants where the same logic arrives in clause-connective packaging (composes with [[connector-logic-families]]). A claimed positional asymmetry — contrast words roam freely to any position, continuers mostly front/middle — is **unverified-claim**; check against our bank before teaching it as a cue.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: one rule only — "the transition talks to the sentence before it" — practiced exclusively on sentence-initial and simple mid-sentence items. Delay the single-comma exception; premature exceptions destroy the fragile main rule.
- **Average**: introduce delete-and-compare as a self-check ritual and the single-comma exception as a checklist. Mixed-placement drills where the student must first say aloud which two statements are in scope.
- **Advanced**: adversarial sets built on the mid-sentence trap (local reading plausible, backward reading decisive); require them to articulate *why* the local reading is out of scope.
- **1500+**: speed integration — scoping should be a sub-second reflex; drill subordinating-conjunction variants and paragraph-final placements so no packaging of the same logic feels novel.
