# Question-first reading: let the stem allocate your attention

## What it is
On the digital SAT, every Reading question comes with its own ~25–150 word passage, and the single highest-leverage habit is to read the QUESTION before the passage. The stem tells you what kind of thinking is required, which parts of the text will decide the answer, and — just as valuable — which parts you are allowed to ignore. Reading the passage cold and then discovering what it's being used for wastes the read.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The section is really testing selective attention under time pressure: ~54 questions in ~64 minutes means roughly 71 seconds per item including reading. The test rewards students who extract exactly the needed slice of meaning and punishes completionists who decode everything. Because each question owns its passage (unlike the old shared-passage format), question-first reading is now strictly dominant — there is no "investment read" to amortize.

## Mental model
**Attention flows top-down from the question, not bottom-up from the text.** Picture the stem as a work order: it specifies the deliverable before you enter the text. An expert reads LESS total text than a novice — but the right slices, with margin. Two allocation modes, chosen by question type:
- **Global questions** (main idea, overall purpose/structure): front-load. In a ~100-word passage the point must arrive early; first and last sentences carry disproportionate weight, middles carry qualifications and examples.
- **Local questions** (function of an underlined portion, detail, vocab): skim the opening for orientation, then read the target span PLUS its immediate neighbors. Half-sentences are actively dangerous — the qualifier that decides the answer often lives in the other half. If the referenced span doesn't contain the answer, widen the window (sentence before, sentence after) rather than re-scanning the same lines; the information exists somewhere by construction.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Read the stem slowly and completely. Name (silently or on scratch paper) what it's asking for and what type it is.
2. Decide the attention plan BEFORE reading: whole-text scan vs. targeted span-plus-margin.
3. Read the planned slice at full comprehension speed — deliberately slower than screen-skimming instinct. Skim only by design, never by drift.
4. If the planned slice doesn't yield the answer, widen the window outward (previous sentence, next sentence) instead of looping on the same lines.
5. Only then look at choices, with a prediction in hand (see [[predict-then-verify]]).
6. Recovery move when lost mid-passage: reread the first and last sentences to re-anchor the point, then re-attack the target span.

## Misconceptions
- **passage-first-habit** — "You have to read the passage before you can answer anything about it." Formed by every school reading assignment ever (text arrives first, questions after). Produces an unfocused first read, then a second read once the stem is known — double time cost, and the first read primes irrelevant details. Trap design: passages front-load a vivid but untested detail that a cold reader latches onto.
- **half-sentence-reading** — Scanning to a keyword and reading only from that word forward. Formed as a speed strategy that works on easy items. The test places the deciding qualifier in the front half of the sentence or the sentence before, producing confident wrong answers that match only the back half.
- **screen-skim-drift** — Device habits (scroll, skim, bounce) imported into the test. Screens prime a skimming mental state; students believe their normal screen speed is fine. Produces careless Module 1 errors with adaptive downside (see [[writing-module-economics]]). unverified-claim: careless-error rates are higher on early-module items for screen-skimmers than paper-practicers — worth validating in our own timing data.
- **decode-everything-completionism** — "I can't answer until I understand every phrase." Formed by close-reading instruction. Produces time loss and panic on dense fiction/science passages. Skilled reading ranks importance and works around confusing patches using what IS understood (see [[detail-lookup-protocol]] and [[fiction-poetry-literal-reading]]).

## Diagnostic indicators
- **passage-first-habit**: item timing shows long dwell before first answer interaction even on short vocab items; student can retell the passage but hesitates on what was asked.
- **half-sentence-reading**: picks distractors that correctly match the second half of the key sentence but violate its opening qualifier (right-detail-wrong-referent and contradiction-in-costume species in [[distractor-species-taxonomy]]).
- **screen-skim-drift**: fast overall pace, misses concentrated on easy/medium literal items; accuracy improves markedly in untimed mode.
- **decode-everything-completionism**: extreme dwell on fiction/poetry items; student reports "I couldn't understand a word so I guessed" even when the tested idea sat in readable sentences.

## Remediation pathways
- passage-first-habit: forced-order drill — show the stem alone for 10 seconds, require the student to state the attention plan out loud, then reveal the passage. Repeat until stem-first feels default.
- half-sentence-reading: contrast pair — one item answerable from the back half, one where the front-half qualifier reverses the answer; have the student explain what changed. If it doesn't land, revisit whole-sentence discipline in [[detail-lookup-protocol]].
- screen-skim-drift: on-screen practice only, with a "finger/cursor anchor" rule and untimed-first sequencing; reintroduce the clock only after accuracy stabilizes.
- decode-everything-completionism: give a passage with two nonsense words inserted and show the item is still answerable; teaches that positive identification beats full decoding.

## Mastery criteria
Given any stem, the student can state the attention plan (global vs. local, which sentences to read) BEFORE seeing the passage, and their answer-time distribution shows targeted reading (no double-reads on local items). Proof: sustained accuracy at normal pace across mixed question types, with per-item dwell appropriate to type — not just accuracy in untimed single-type drills.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: stem names the target explicitly ("the underlined sentence"); one attention mode suffices.
- **Medium**: stem references an entity discussed across two or three sentences; window-widening required.
- **Hard**: the deciding information sits outside the span a novice would target (an opening frame, a blurb, a final-sentence reversal); composes with [[reference-compression-tracking]] (the target span is a compressed restatement of an earlier idea) and [[emphasis-signal-map]] (the answer hides next to signal machinery the student must know to seek).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach ONLY the two-mode rule first (whole-text question = read edges; pinpoint question = read the spot plus neighbors). Don't introduce window-widening until the base habit exists. Use the work-order metaphor concretely.
- **Average**: the failure is usually inconsistency, not ignorance — they read question-first on hard items but revert to passage-first on "easy-looking" ones. Drill mixed sets where they must verbalize the plan every time.
- **Advanced**: focus on window-widening and half-sentence discipline; their misses come from targeting the right area but clipping the qualifier. Introduce the recovery move (re-anchor via first/last sentences).
- **1500+**: strategy overhead itself becomes the enemy — a strong flexible reader may legitimately read short passages straight through faster than planning would take. Teach when the protocol is skippable (short, easy, literal items) and where it is never skippable (function, completion, evidence items). Flexibility in approach selection is the top-scorer trait.
