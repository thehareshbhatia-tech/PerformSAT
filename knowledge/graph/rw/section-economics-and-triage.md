# Section economics and triage — spend points, minutes, and energy on purpose

## What it is
The resource-allocation doctrine for the whole R&W section: which items to
attempt at full effort, in what order, with what energy — plus the training
doctrine that builds those decisions before test day. Where
[[writing-module-economics]] covers the writing half's time bank, this node
covers the section-level budget: strategic sacrifice, fatigue management,
triage ordering, and the two study-sequencing rules (untimed-first, and
clustered-vs-scattered miss ordering).

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The test doesn't test economics directly — it charges students who lack it.
The format sets the incentives: no wrong-answer penalty, free within-module
navigation and flagging, and adaptive routing where module 1 performance
gates module 2 difficulty and therefore the score ceiling (unverified-claim
on adaptive specifics — consistent with our platform model, but keep the
gate on numeric assertions). The section is roughly an hour of continuous
short-passage reading with all of Math still ahead (unverified-claim on
exact timing — verify against current official specs), so efficiency is
partly ENERGY management, not just clock management. Students who treat the
section as a gauntlet — every item at full effort, in presented order, cold
— leak points to fatigue, to rushed easy items, and to careless module-1
errors with adaptive downside.

## Mental model
**A budget, not a gauntlet.** Three currencies — points, minutes, attention
— and four spending rules:

1. **Strategic sacrifice.** Sub-maximum score goals do not require attempting
   everything. Deliberately punting the one or two hardest items and
   reinvesting that time as care elsewhere typically nets MORE points than
   attempting everything rushed. Perfection is a 1550+ requirement, not a
   universal one.
2. **Energy budgeting.** The known leaks are reread loops (re-scanning the
   same lines instead of widening or revising the hypothesis) and
   overthinking easy items. Keep moving; protect attention for where it pays.
3. **Triage ordering.** Answer your reliable TYPES first — banking points and
   confidence — and know your personal easy/hard family map BEFORE test day
   (built from [[stem-family-recognition]] review data, never improvised
   mid-module). Corollaries: warm up before the test so the first module
   isn't the warm-up (module 1 is the highest-stakes place to be cold), and
   students not chasing top scores should consider deferring the paired-text
   set ([[cross-text-response-prediction]]) on first pass — it is
   disproportionately expensive per point.
4. **Strategy overhead is not free.** Strong, flexible readers may do best
   simply going in order; triage is for students with a real spread between
   their reliable and unreliable families.

Two training rules feed the in-test rules:

- **Untimed mastery first.** Train in two phases: first work items untimed,
  consciously NAMING the applicable strategy before each one, until strategy
  selection is automatic — only then reintroduce the clock. Never train
  accuracy and speed at once; speed is the residue of automatic process, not
  a separate skill to force.
- **Scattered vs clustered misses set study order.** Sort every miss by stem
  family: clustered misses name the weak families — drill exactly those.
  Scattered misses mean no family is the problem — study comprehensively, in
  sequence, instead of chasing phantom weaknesses.

## Expert reasoning process
Before test day:
1. Build the personal family map from reviewed practice: reliable families,
   unreliable families, time-sink families.
2. Apply the miss-distribution rule: clustered → targeted family drilling;
   scattered → sequential comprehensive study.
3. Train untimed with strategy-naming until selection is silent; only then
   add the clock.
4. Decide the test-day plan in advance: what gets deferred (if anything),
   what the punt budget is for the goal score.

On test day:
5. Warm up before the session — a few items of each reliable family — so
   module 1 gets a running start.
6. Work reliable families at full care; flag and defer the pre-designated
   expensive types; punt within budget without guilt (a flagged guess is a
   decision, not a failure).
7. Police the energy leaks: no third read of the same lines
   ([[question-first-reading-protocol]] sets what to read at all); no
   dwelling on items already answered confidently.
8. Return to flagged items with remaining time, freshest-first.

## Misconceptions
- **attempt-everything-perfectionism** — the belief that the goal score
  requires full effort on every item. Formed by school norms where every
  point is graded and skipping reads as failure. Produces universal haste:
  the hardest items get attempted badly AND the easiest items get rushed.
  The fix is arithmetic, not courage — show the point cost of each.
- **speed-and-accuracy-at-once** — training under time pressure from day one,
  because "the test is timed." Formed by equating practice conditions with
  test conditions. Produces plateaued accuracy with fast wrong answers: the
  strategy-selection step never becomes automatic because it was never
  practiced deliberately.
- **presented-order-compliance** — the assumption that items must be worked
  in the order shown. Formed by default deference to the interface and by
  paper-test habits. Costs the energy-sequencing and triage opportunity the
  free navigation exists to provide.
- **module-one-as-warmup** — easing into the test, expecting to "find the
  rhythm" during the first module. Formed because warm-up-then-perform is how
  most practice sessions actually go. On an adaptive test the cold start is
  maximally expensive: early careless errors depress module 2 difficulty and
  cap the score (unverified-claim on the precise routing mechanics).

## Diagnostic indicators
- **attempt-everything-perfectionism:** no blank or fast-flagged items ever;
  time-per-item nearly flat across difficulty; misses concentrated at module
  ends (rushed easy items) alongside long dwells on the hardest items.
- **speed-and-accuracy-at-once:** practice history shows timed sets from the
  start; accuracy flat across weeks; the student cannot name the strategy
  they used on a missed item ("I just read it").
- **presented-order-compliance:** telemetry shows strictly sequential
  answering with no flag usage; paired-text or hardest items consumed
  mid-module at full cost.
- **module-one-as-warmup:** module 1 accuracy consistently below module 2
  accuracy on same-family items; early-item misses on families the student
  otherwise owns.

## Remediation pathways
- **attempt-everything-perfectionism:** run the budget math on their own last
  test — points lost to rushed easy items vs points the punted hard items
  would have earned. The arithmetic usually converts on the spot. Set an
  explicit punt budget tied to their goal score.
- **speed-and-accuracy-at-once:** prescribe the two-phase regime: untimed
  sets with mandatory strategy-naming (say the family and the procedure
  aloud, then solve) until naming is automatic, then a stepped clock. If
  naming itself fails, the gap is [[stem-family-recognition]], not pacing.
- **presented-order-compliance:** one supervised practice module worked
  types-first; compare score and reported energy against their sequential
  baseline. Teach flagging as a first-class move, not an admission.
- **module-one-as-warmup:** install a pre-test warm-up ritual (a handful of
  reliable-family items before the session) and show their own M1-vs-M2
  accuracy gap as the evidence.

## Mastery criteria
The student can state their own family map, punt budget, and deferral plan
before a practice test — and their telemetry matches the plan (flags used,
reliable families front-loaded, no third-read loops). Training evidence: they
refuse timed practice on families they haven't mastered untimed, unprompted.
Outcome evidence: module-end rush errors and M1-cold-start errors disappear
from their logs while total attempted items may DROP.

## Difficulty ladder
This node's "difficulty" scales with the goal score, not the item bank:
- **Sub-600 R&W goals:** sacrifice is the highest-leverage move — a generous
  punt budget plus full care on reliable families beats broad effort.
- **600s:** triage ordering and energy policing dominate; the punt budget
  shrinks to one or two items; deferral of paired-text sets is still often
  correct.
- **700+:** the punt budget approaches zero and triage collapses into mere
  ordering; the residual economics are the warm-up ritual and reread-loop
  hygiene, and the training rules (untimed-first, cluster-driven study)
  carry the remaining gains.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling:** lead with permission — sub-max goals don't require
  attempting everything, and a planned punt is a point-positive decision.
  Build the family map FOR them from their review data; asking them to
  self-assess families this early produces topic-indexing instead.
- **Average:** teach the budget as three currencies and make the plan
  explicit and written before each practice test. Enforce untimed-first
  ruthlessly — this band most wants to "practice real conditions"
  prematurely.
- **Advanced:** shift from sacrifice to ordering and energy: warm-up ritual,
  deferral decisions, reread-loop policing. Their study order should now be
  driven entirely by the clustered-vs-scattered rule on real miss data.
- **1500+:** economics shrinks to hygiene — warm-up, flag discipline, and
  recognizing time-sink stems early ([[stem-family-recognition]]). The
  differentiator at this band is refusing unplanned heroics: the plan was
  made calm; the module is not the place to renegotiate it.
