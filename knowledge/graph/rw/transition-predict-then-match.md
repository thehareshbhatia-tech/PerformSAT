# Predict the relationship before reading any choice

## What it is
The expert method for transition items: determine what relationship the two in-scope statements actually have **before looking at a single answer choice**, then scan the choices for the one whose family (and sub-job) matches the prediction. The prediction is the answer key; the choices are only checked against it. The anti-method — plugging each choice into the blank and judging by feel — is the single largest source of transition errors.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Transition distractors are engineered against choice-first processing: near-family options that *sound* fine in isolation, reversed-arrow causal words, and emphatic words where a result is needed. A student who plugs and listens gives the distractor a fair hearing it doesn't deserve; a student who predicts first never lets the distractor audition. This method is also the speed engine — a predicted item resolves in one scan, funding the time bank ([[writing-module-economics]]).

## Mental model
**Write the answer, then find it.** Treat the blank as a free-response question: compress each in-scope statement to a one-clause gist ("life can't survive here" / "life thrives there"), name the relationship between the gists, and turn it into a shopping order — "I need a result word," "I need an example-introducer." Only then open the choices, holding the order. The relationship is read off the **gists**, never off surface wording, and the passage's jargon is noise: no item requires knowing the science or history it's dressed in.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Spot that the blank is a connective → logic question; all four choices are presumed grammatically valid.
2. Scope the two statements ([[transition-backward-anchoring]]): default host + previous sentence; single-comma case switches to the sentence's own halves.
3. Compress each statement to a one-clause gist, reading past terminology ([[topic-point-attitude-reading]]).
4. Name the relationship — family, then sub-job if callable — as an explicit prediction.
5. Scan choices for the match. For a cause-effect pick, sanity-check the arrow direction.
6. If no choice matches the precise prediction, fall back to **family-level elimination**: strike whole groups at once (all continuers, or all contrast + causal words) and decide among survivors. Inside this sweep, apply **twin cancellation**: two choices that are functional synonyms doing the same logical job cannot both be right, so BOTH are wrong — the survivor from a unique category wins by default. This is the transitions face of [[boundary-equivalence-cancellation]]; students who pick either twin are shopping by tone or register, not logic.
7. Re-insert the winner and confirm the passage reads coherently — the only moment "sound" is consulted, and only as a final check.

## Misconceptions
- **plug-and-listen** — the student tries each choice in the blank and judges by feel. This is the **root misconception of the whole writing domain**: choice-first processing. It forms from years of school fill-in-the-blank vocabulary work, where trying candidates *is* the method. It produces "twisting" — inventing a reading under which a near-miss option sounds plausible — and it is precisely what near-family distractors are designed to reward. It also doubles time per item.
- **jargon-freeze** — the student loses the logical thread when the passage concerns unfamiliar science or history, believing content comprehension is being tested. Forms from conflating this question type with reading-comprehension items. Produces skipped or slow items and gists contaminated with half-understood detail; the relationship call then fails because the inputs were mush.
- **no-fallback-paralysis** — the student's prediction doesn't match any choice and they have no recovery move, so they revert to plug-and-listen under time pressure. Forms when the method is taught without its elimination fallback. Produces late-item errors and long dwell times specifically on items where the prediction was miscalibrated.
- **verification-skipping** — the student commits on the scan match without re-inserting the choice, occasionally missing that the matched word is wrong in grammatical packaging or direction. Forms from over-trusting the method once it starts working. Produces sporadic careless misses on otherwise-mastered items.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **plug-and-listen**: long per-item times with repeated choice re-reads (visible in timing telemetry as dwell on the choice region); wrong answers land on near-family distractors; when asked to explain, the student says "I tried each one and this sounded best" and cannot state the two statements' relationship without mentioning a choice.
- **jargon-freeze**: accuracy stratifies by passage topic rather than by choice-set design; the student paraphrases the passage with technical terms intact instead of gists.
- **no-fallback-paralysis**: timing spikes on a minority of items; the student reports "none of them fit what I expected."
- **verification-skipping**: fast, mostly right, with rare reversed-causality or mispackaged misses; the error items show the shortest times of the set.

## Remediation pathways
- **plug-and-listen**: the choices-hidden drill — present items with the choices physically concealed and require a written prediction before revealing them. This makes choice-first processing impossible and builds the habit in a few sessions. Our drill UI should ask for the family *first*, word second. If predictions themselves are weak, the gap is upstream in [[transition-relationship-families]].
- **jargon-freeze**: gist-translation practice — take a technical two-sentence pair and rewrite each as a six-word plain statement before any relationship call. Show that the item resolves with zero domain knowledge. Revisit [[topic-point-attitude-reading]] if compression is the blocker.
- **no-fallback-paralysis**: teach family-level elimination as an explicit plan B and drill it on items with deliberately awkward predictions.
- **verification-skipping**: require the re-insert read-through as a named final step; one caught error usually re-installs it.

## Mastery criteria
The student can state, unprompted and before seeing choices: the two in-scope statements, their gists, and a family/sub-job prediction — then answer by scan. Proof: correct answers with short choice-dwell times on medium/hard items, and the ability to justify an answer *without referencing any other choice* ("it's a result word and the second statement is the result"), consistently across sessions.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: sentence-initial blank, cross-family choice set; prediction at family level suffices.
- **medium**: mid-sentence placements and two-and-two family splits; prediction must include scoping and sometimes sub-job.
- **hard**: composes everything — mid-sentence trap placement ([[transition-backward-anchoring]]), all-same-family choice sets requiring sub-job precision, reversed-arrow causal distractors ([[transition-relationship-families]]), and dense technical passages that punish jargon-freeze. The method does not change with difficulty; only the precision demanded of the prediction does.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: run choices-hidden from day one — never let them see a choice set before the prediction habit exists. Accept family-level predictions; celebrate correct *predictions* even when the final answer is missed.
- **Average**: full method with the fallback; introduce timing awareness (target: prediction inside ~20 seconds). Diagnose twisting by making them defend an answer without mentioning other choices.
- **Advanced**: adversarial sets where the trap rewards plugging; post-mortem every miss by asking "what was your prediction?" — a missing prediction is the finding.
- **1500+**: compress the method to reflex; the visible behavior should be one pass over the passage, one pass over the choices, done. Work the residual leaks: direction checks under speed and prediction recalibration when no choice matches.
