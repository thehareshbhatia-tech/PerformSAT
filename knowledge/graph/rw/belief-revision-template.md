# The belief-revision skeleton: old idea, pivot, new idea

## What it is
The dominant architecture of SAT science and social-science passages: an established belief is set up, then challenged, revised, or overturned by newer evidence. Recognize the skeleton and the passage becomes predictable — the post-pivot material is (nearly always) the point, and the attitudes split cleanly: skeptical toward the old idea, favorable toward the new. unverified-claim: this template accounts for a large share of science/social-science passages and the post-pivot idea is "almost certainly" the point — high-confidence pattern, but compute the actual share from our bank and official material before the tutor asserts a frequency.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The template mirrors how research prose actually works (literature review → gap → finding), so College Board inherits it from its source genres. For the test writers it's load-bearing: it creates two clearly opposed ideas in under 120 words, enabling polarity-based distractors (state the OLD idea as the point; flip an attitude) and inference/completion items whose answers follow from the direction of revision.

## Mental model
**A hinge with two leaves.** Leaf one: the old belief, flagged by hedged set-up language — "many people believe," "it was long thought," "historically," "conventional wisdom held." Crucially, these hedges signal the author does NOT own the belief; distance-markers are the tell. The hinge: a pivot — "however," "but," "yet," "in fact," "recent evidence suggests" ([[transition-relationship-families]]). Leaf two: the new idea — the author's actual point. Once the first leaf is recognized, everything downstream is PREDICTABLE before it's read: expect a pivot; expect the post-pivot claim to be the point; expect attitude negative-then-positive across the hinge. Even a phrase like "historically at odds" quietly implies the conflict has since ended. Reading becomes confirmation of a forecast rather than discovery.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Opening sentence contains hedged-belief language → pre-load the template immediately.
2. Forecast: "old idea = X; a pivot is coming; the point will be some revision of X."
3. Hit the pivot; read the post-pivot material as the point. Jot BOTH sides in shorthand ("old: X / new: Y") — two-sided jots are the polarity insurance ([[shorthand-note-discipline]]).
4. Derive attitudes without further reading: author leans away from old, toward new — then verify temperature against actual word choices (the lean can be respectful revision, not demolition).
5. Eliminate: any choice crowning the old idea as the point is dead; any choice with reversed polarity is dead ([[distractor-species-taxonomy]] species 9); intensity overshoots ("proves the old view worthless") die on temperature.
6. For completion/inference stems on template passages, the credited continuation extends the NEW idea's direction; for paired texts, map each author onto the template (defender of old vs. proponent of new) before comparing ([[cross-text-relationship-taxonomy]]).

## Misconceptions
- **first-claim-is-the-point** — Treating the opening belief as the thesis because school essays put the thesis first. The template weaponizes this: the opening is the TARGET, not the thesis. Produces picks that state the old idea as the main idea — a signature miss our engine can tag precisely.
- **hedge-deafness** — Not registering distance-markers ("long thought," "widely assumed") as authorial disavowal; reading them as endorsement. Formed because casual reading skips meta-language. Without the disavowal signal, the student can't see the setup and gets ambushed by the pivot.
- **polarity-flip-from-memory** — Holding the two ideas mentally without externalizing, then swapping which was old and which was new at answer time. Especially afflicts fluent readers ("strong-reader-overconfidence" in [[topic-point-attitude-reading]]). Produces confident contradiction picks.
- **demolition-assumption** — Assuming the new idea always DESTROYS the old, when the passage may only qualify or extend it ("bleaching is driven by warming — but recovery depends on local currents too"). Produces intensity-overshoot picks and wrong-direction inferences on revision-not-refutation passages.
- **template-everywhere** — Over-applying the skeleton to passages that are pure description or that genuinely defend the established view. Formed by pattern enthusiasm after learning the template. Produces forced "pivots" and misread points on non-template passages; the template is a prior, activated by hedge language, not a universal key.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **first-claim-is-the-point**: picks main-idea choices restating the pre-pivot belief; explanation cites the first sentence as "the thesis."
- **hedge-deafness**: when asked the author's view of the opening claim, answers "they agree with it"; misses on attitude items about the old idea.
- **polarity-flip-from-memory**: correct identification of both ideas in discussion, wrong (reversed) answer on the item; no jot in scratch behavior.
- **demolition-assumption**: picks extreme-rejection choices on qualification passages; wrong-direction picks on completion items where the credited answer preserves part of the old view.
- **template-everywhere**: think-alouds invent a pivot on descriptive passages; misses concentrated on non-template science passages after template accuracy improved.

## Remediation pathways
- first-claim-is-the-point: hedge-highlighting drill — student marks every distance-marker in a set of openers and labels each opening claim "author's view / someone else's view." Contrast one template passage with one thesis-first passage.
- hedge-deafness: build the signal inventory in their own words (belief-attribution verbs, time-distance words, crowd words) and drill recognition at speed; connects to [[emphasis-signal-map]].
- polarity-flip-from-memory: mandatory two-sided jot on every template passage for two weeks; show them a flip they actually committed.
- demolition-assumption: contrast pair — full refutation vs. gentle revision passage; student states exactly how much of the old idea survives in each.
- template-everywhere: mixed deck with template and non-template passages; the student must first answer "is the skeleton even here?" — activation criteria (hedged opener) before application.

## Mastery criteria
Given only a passage's first sentence, the student can state whether the template is likely active and, if so, forecast the pivot and the point's direction; on full passages they extract old idea, new idea, and both attitudes with polarity errors extinguished. Proof: consistent accuracy on template-passage main-idea, attitude, and completion items at the hard band, plus correct NON-application on descriptive passages.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: loud hedge ("it was long believed"), loud pivot ("however"), new idea stated flatly after it.
- **Medium**: quieter hedges (a bare "historically"), pivot mid-sentence, revision rather than refutation.
- **Hard**: double revision (new idea itself qualified), template distributed across paired texts (one author per leaf), attitude temperature carried by single verbs; composes with [[support-and-weaken-textual]] (which finding would support the NEW idea) and [[reference-compression-tracking]] ("this assumption" compressing the old leaf).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach the skeleton as a story shape — "people used to think X; surprise: actually Y" — and drill only the question "which sentence is the surprise?" Point identification comes free once the surprise is found.
- **Average**: full template with the signal inventory and the two-sided jot; polarity distractor autopsies.
- **Advanced**: revision-vs-refutation temperature calibration; paired-text template mapping; forecasting practice (read the opener, predict the rest, then check).
- **1500+**: the edge cases — passages that defend the old view against a challenge (template inverted), post-pivot counterarguments (rare and brief, per the pattern — unverified-claim), and template-as-prior discipline: the forecast is held loosely and the text always gets the last word.
