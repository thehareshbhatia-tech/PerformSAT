# Finding the argument's spine: claim vs. evidence vs. mechanism

## What it is
Every evidence-and-logic passage on the SAT is a miniature argument with three separable parts: the **claim** (what someone asserts is true), the **evidence** (the observations offered for it), and the **mechanism** (the specific causal or logical story connecting them). Decomposing a passage into these parts — and compressing the claim into a few words of your own — is the entry move for every Inferences and Command of Evidence question. Everything else in the passage is scaffolding: background, credentials, scene-setting.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The College Board is probing whether a student can tell what a text is *arguing* apart from what it merely *mentions*. Distractors across three question families (text completions, support/weaken, quantitative evidence) are built from passage material that is real but argumentatively idle — you can only resist them if you have isolated the spine first. The claim frequently sits late in the passage, often in the final sentence, with openings serving as context filler (unverified-claim — plausible across all three raw treatments, but verify against official items and our bank before the tutor asserts it as a rule).

## Mental model
**A passage is a skeleton wearing a coat.** The skeleton is one claim, one or two pieces of evidence, and the mechanism bolting them together. The coat — names, dates, vivid details, jargon — is what distractors are sewn from. An expert strips the coat before answering anything. The compressed claim goes on scratch paper in 3–6 words, ideally as a directional relation ("more X → less Y") when the claim is empirical.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Read the question stem first and grab its task verb (complete / support / weaken / illustrate) and the claim-holder ("the researchers' hypothesis," "the student's conclusion"). That verb is the search key.
2. Scan the passage for the sentence where that holder asserts something — expect it late; verbs like *hypothesize, propose, conclude, suggest* mark it.
3. Restate the claim plainly in your own words. If it's dense, write it down; if it's empirical, convert it to a directional arrow relation.
4. Tag the evidence sentences and the mechanism (WHICH component or condition is said to carry the effect). Note qualifiers: magnitude words ("far greater"), time windows, populations, the grammatical subject.
5. Consciously demote everything else to background. You will not use it, and choices built from it are presumptively traps.

## Misconceptions
- **first-sentence-is-thesis** — Students expect the claim up front because school essays put the thesis first. They anchor on the opening sentence, then judge every choice against background instead of the actual claim. The test exploits this by front-loading context and burying the operative claim in the last sentence.
- **everything-is-evidence** — Students treat all passage content as equally load-bearing, so any choice touching any sentence feels "supported." Formed by comprehension-quiz habits where any detail could be asked about. Produces picks of vivid-but-idle details (see [[topical-vs-logical-relevance]]).
- **claim-holder-blindness** — Passages often contain two positions (a phenomenon and a hypothesis about it, or two competing explanations); students judge choices against the wrong one. The stem always names whose claim matters, but skimmers miss it. Trap design: an answer that would bear on the neighboring claim but not the named one.
- **qualifier-dropping** — Students compress the claim but shear off its qualifiers ("*much* greater," "*only* in winter," "*this* species"), turning a specific claim into a generic one that many distractors satisfy. Formed because summarizing in school rewards gist over precision.

## Diagnostic indicators
- *first-sentence-is-thesis*: student picks choices matching the passage's opening topic; when asked to state the claim, they recite the first sentence.
- *everything-is-evidence*: student defends a wrong answer with "but the passage says that" — true fact, wrong function.
- *claim-holder-blindness*: wrong answers cluster on the distractor keyed to the passage's *other* position; student's explanation names the wrong actor ("the study showed..." when the stem asked about the critic's claim).
- *qualifier-dropping*: student's scratch restatement (or verbal restatement to the tutor) omits magnitude/scope words; they miss items where the correct answer turns on exactly that qualifier.

## Remediation pathways
- *first-sentence-is-thesis*: contrast pair — same passage with claim first vs. claim last; have the student underline the claim in each. Then drill "find the sentence the stem's verb points to." Revisit [[topic-point-attitude-reading]] if they can't distinguish topic from claim at all.
- *everything-is-evidence*: teach the three-part sort explicitly: label each sentence C / E / M / background on two or three short passages. The act of labeling background is the fix.
- *claim-holder-blindness*: give a two-position passage and ask two versions of the same question, one per holder — the answers differ, which makes the stakes visible.
- *qualifier-dropping*: re-derivation drill — student compresses a claim, then the tutor writes a choice that satisfies the compressed version but violates the original. Repeat until their compressions keep the qualifiers.

## Mastery criteria
Given any Information & Ideas short passage, the student can (a) point to the claim sentence within ~15 seconds, (b) restate it in under 10 words preserving direction, scope, and subject, and (c) say which sentence(s) are evidence and which are background. Proof: consistent correct answers on medium+ support/weaken and completion items where at least one distractor is built from background material.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: single claim, stated plainly, early or clearly flagged; background minimal.
- **Medium**: claim late in passage; one competing position or a pointer phrase ("this possibility") requiring backtracking; qualifiers start to matter.
- **Hard**: two entangled positions with the stem naming one; dense academic diction around the claim; the claim's mechanism (not its topic) is what the answer turns on — composes with [[support-and-weaken-textual]] mechanism-specificity and [[entailment-moves-and-boundaries]] scope limits.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: physicalize it. Print short passages; three highlighter colors for claim/evidence/background. No questions at all until the sort is fast. Use everyday arguments first ("my friend claims the cafeteria is worse this year because...") before academic ones.
- **Average**: teach the stem-verb-first habit and the late-claim expectation. Their sort is usually fine; their failure is not *doing* it under time. Assign the 3–6 word written compression as a non-skippable step for two weeks.
- **Advanced**: focus on qualifiers and claim-holder discipline — the only spine errors they still make. Drill two-position passages and magnitude-qualified claims.
- **1500+**: compress the routine to a glance: stem verb → last-sentence check → mental arrow notation. Work on speed and on trusting the spine against seductive background-built distractors; introduce the "if a choice needs effort to connect, that effort is disqualifying" time rule.
