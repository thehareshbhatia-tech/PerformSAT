# Classify the question before solving it

## What it is
The first act on any writing-domain item is identifying **which rule or task is being tested** — and the choice set is the label. Scan what *varies* across the four choices (punctuation marks? verb number? connective words? rhetorical framing?) and that variation names the tested rule; everything in the passage irrelevant to that rule is then deliberately ignored. The writing domain is a small, closed inventory of testable rules, so classification maps every item, however dressed up, onto something already known.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The writing territory is finite and predictable: a majority of any form's items come from a short high-frequency list — transitions and notes-synthesis questions are effectively guaranteed (verified against our own bank and module data: transitions sit in the Expression block, notes questions close the module) — with a couple of lower-frequency "wild cards." The exact frequency distribution beyond that pair is **unverified-claim** territory; keep it flagged until our bank statistics confirm it. The test dresses simple rules in long or technical passages precisely to punish students who engage the whole passage instead of classifying first.

## Mental model
**Classification → retrieval → verification, never vibes.** The expert loop: (1) the choice-set scan names the rule; (2) retrieve that rule's one-line invariant — expert knowledge is stored as terse decision-ready invariants, not prose; (3) run the local check the invariant prescribes (find the true subject, name the previous sentence's relationship, test the boundary); (4) re-insert the answer and confirm the passage reads coherently. Nothing is ever judged holistically by ear. The ear is a liar under adversarial conditions: it approves familiar constructions and vetoes unfamiliar-but-correct ones, and the test deliberately writes correct answers that sound strange.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Read the choice set *before* deliberating on the passage: what axis (or axes) do the choices vary on?
2. Name the tested rule from that axis. When choices vary on two axes at once (say, tense and number), identify which axis actually discriminates among them — the decorative axis exists to misdirect.
3. Retrieve the rule's invariant and perform only its prescribed local check, reading exactly as much passage as the rule requires (for transitions: the previous sentence; for notes items: the goal sentence).
4. Read the whole mini-passage once when the rule demands context — then re-plug the chosen answer into the passage as the final verification.
5. Move on without second-guessing: a rule-derived answer is not re-litigated by ear.

## Misconceptions
- **ear-reliance** — the belief that a fluent reader can *hear* correctness. Forms because school English rarely requires explicit rule articulation, and native fluency genuinely suffices in casual writing. It produces two failure modes: second-guessing rule-correct answers that sound stiff, and approving distractors that sound smooth. It is also the gateway to choice-first processing — plugging in candidates and listening — the root behavioral error across the domain ([[transition-predict-then-match]] treats its transition-specific form).
- **holistic-processing** — the student engages the full passage as a comprehension task before (or instead of) identifying the tested rule, burning time on content the rule never touches. Forms from reading-class habits where everything might matter. Produces slow items and rule misidentification — answering the question they imagined rather than the one the choices define.
- **more-text-means-harder** — the student weights difficulty by passage length and jargon rather than by rule identity, over-investing in easy items wrapped in dense text and panicking on formats that look busy. Forms from surface-difficulty heuristics. Produces pacing damage more than accuracy damage (see [[writing-module-economics]]).
- **axis-confusion** — when choices vary on two features simultaneously, the student solves the salient axis rather than the discriminating one (e.g., agonizing over tense when the choices actually split on number). Forms because the decorative axis is designed to be the noticeable one. Produces systematic misses on double-axis choice sets while single-axis accuracy stays high.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **ear-reliance**: the student changes correct answers to smoother-sounding wrong ones (answer-revision telemetry); explanations use "sounds right/wrong" with no rule named; accuracy collapses specifically on items whose correct answer is an unfamiliar-but-valid construction.
- **holistic-processing**: uniformly long times across writing items regardless of difficulty; asked what the question tests, the student summarizes the passage topic instead of naming a rule.
- **more-text-means-harder**: time-per-item correlates with passage length rather than with actual difficulty band; flags/skips on long-but-easy items.
- **axis-confusion**: misses concentrate on multi-axis choice sets; the student's explanation discusses only the decorative axis.

## Remediation pathways
- **ear-reliance**: adversarial pair — one smooth-sounding wrong answer next to one stiff-sounding correct one, resolved by rule; the moment a student watches their ear lose to a rule they can state, the authority transfers. Then require a named rule before every answer for a session.
- **holistic-processing**: choices-first drill — show only the choice set and ask "what is this question testing?" before revealing the passage. Classification without content is the skill in its pure form.
- **more-text-means-harder**: relabeling exercise — sort ten items by *rule*, not by appearance, then observe that the longest item was among the fastest to solve.
- **axis-confusion**: two-axis choice sets with the instruction to list every axis of variation before solving; once listing is habitual, ask which axis all four choices can't survive.

## Mastery criteria
Given a bare choice set, the student names the tested rule in seconds; given a full item, they can state the rule, the invariant, and the local check they ran. Proof: writing items resolve well under the section's average pace with stable accuracy, and answer-revision behavior (rule → ear downgrades) disappears.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: single-axis choice sets, short passages; classification is nearly explicit.
- **medium**: rules wrapped in long or technical passages; classification must beat the urge to comprehend everything.
- **hard**: double-axis choice sets with a decorative axis, unfamiliar-sounding correct answers targeting the ear, and rule combinations where the local checks chain. At the hard end this node composes with the specific rule nodes — it routes the question; the rule node solves it.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach the loop on one rule family at a time so retrieval has something to retrieve; the classification habit without rule inventory is an empty router. Slogan-level cue: "the choices tell you the question."
- **Average**: mixed-rule drills where the first written step is naming the tested rule; grade the classification separately from the answer.
- **Advanced**: choice-set-only speed rounds (classify 20 items from choices alone), plus double-axis sets to install axis listing.
- **1500+**: the remaining value is calibration and trust — never downgrading a rule-derived answer on sound, and knowing which rare "wild card" rules exist so nothing on the form feels unclassifiable.
