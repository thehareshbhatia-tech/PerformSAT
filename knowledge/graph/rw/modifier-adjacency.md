# Modifier adjacency: describers must touch what they describe

## What it is
A descriptive word or phrase attaches to whatever it sits next to. Distance
between describer and described produces either ambiguity or an absurd literal
reading. One principle, several manifestations: the dangling opener (an
introductory phrase before a comma whose described noun must come IMMEDIATELY
after the comma), the misplaced modifier anywhere in the sentence, drifting
which-clauses, and parentheticals (which obey the same adjacency law as commas).
Modifier items are sentence-local: unlike pronoun or tense items, the
surrounding passage is never needed.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The exam probes whether a reader parses the LITERAL sentence rather than the
charitably repaired one. Its favorite build: four choices that are all
grammatical, clear, and concise in isolation — the discriminator is purely which
noun ends up touching the fixed modifier. This is explicitly the most dangerous
distractor archetype for strong students, because every quality they've learned
to check (grammar, concision, clarity) passes; only whole-sentence re-attachment
catches it.

## Mental model
**Ask the who-question, check the boundary.** Extract the modifier, ask "who or
what is doing/being this?", and demand that answer be the noun at the relevant
boundary. Because the modifier is usually in the FIXED text, you never need to
read four choices fully — scan only the boundary words:
- Intro phrase + comma + blank → check each choice's FIRST noun.
- Blank + comma + fixed descriptive tail (a which-clause, an appositive) → check
  each choice's LAST noun.
- Parenthetical → check the noun touching the open-paren (or the parenthetical's
  opening content) the same way.
Two refinements that prevent over- and under-triggering:
- **Possessive trap**: the right person's POSSESSIVE after an intro phrase still
  dangles — the head noun of "Curie's discovery" is the discovery, not Curie.
  Possessive noun immediately after an introductory phrase ≈ wrong
  [unverified-claim: near-absolute per prep-source analysis; verify vs our bank
  before the tutor states it as a law].
- **Complete-subject allowance**: an extra label stacked before the true subject
  ("a physicist, Curie…" patterns) does NOT dangle — it's part of the full
  subject phrase. The rule is "the described noun heads the next noun phrase,"
  not "the name is the very next word."

## Expert reasoning process
1. Triage ([[choice-set-diagnosis]]): choices differing in their opening (or
   closing) words around a fixed descriptive phrase → modifier item. Note the
   -ing opener is only a hint; intro phrases can be prepositional or appositive.
2. Ask the who-question of the fixed modifier; name the required noun before
   looking at choices.
3. Scan only the boundary words of each choice; eliminate every choice whose
   boundary noun isn't the required one (watching for the possessive trap — 
   check the HEAD of the boundary noun phrase, not for the name's presence).
4. Treat grammaticality, fluency, and concision of a choice as zero evidence.
5. If a literal reading of the assembled sentence is absurd, trust the
   absurdity — that IS the error, not a style quibble.

## Misplaced-modifier subtypes (beyond the dangling opener)
The dangling opener is the SAT's workhorse, but adjacency breaks appear in
other shapes worth naming for hard items [our taxonomy; the digital exam tests
these rarely — unverified-claim: verify per-subtype frequency vs our bank]:
- **Limiting words** (only, almost, just, nearly, even) modify the very next
  word; "only measured" vs "measured only the depth" make different claims.
- **Drifting relative clause** — a which/that clause landing on the nearest
  noun instead of the intended one; moving the clause or the noun changes the
  claimed cause or description (see [[relative-pronoun-and-case]]).
- **Squinting adverb** — an adverb between two verbs that could face either way
  ("students who practice often improve"); the fix is placement, not deletion.
- **Detached prepositional phrase** — a phrase at the sentence end describing a
  distant subject, producing the classic absurd literal reading.

## Misconceptions
- **meaning-rescues-syntax** — the student accepts a dangling opener because the
  intended meaning is obvious. Forms because listeners repair speech errors
  automatically; the test grades the literal sentence. Produces picks of fluent
  choices whose opening noun the intro phrase can't describe.
- **possessive-counts-as-the-person** — "the scientist's breakthrough" after a
  phrase describing the scientist feels right because the name is visually
  present. Forms from name-spotting instead of head-noun analysis. The sharpest
  planted trap in this family.
- **ing-word-required** — the student only checks for dangling when the opener
  is a participle, missing prepositional and appositive openers. Forms because
  textbooks drill the participle case almost exclusively.
- **choices-first-solving** — the student compares the four choices to each
  other (style, length, tense) instead of re-attaching each to the fixed stem.
  Forms because many item types ARE choice-local; modifier items punish the
  transfer. This is the habit behind the clean-but-dangling archetype.
- **which-floats-free** — a which-clause is allowed to attach to any earlier
  noun that makes sense. Forms from conversational relative clauses, which
  attach by topic; in the tested standard, which attaches BACKWARD to the noun
  it touches.

## Diagnostic indicators
- Picks grammatical, concise choices whose boundary noun mismatches the fixed
  modifier → **choices-first-solving** / **meaning-rescues-syntax**. On a
  high-accuracy student this is THE signature miss; message it as
  "whole-sentence integration," not as a grammar gap.
- Picks the possessive-opener choice specifically → 
  **possessive-counts-as-the-person**; ask "what's the head noun here?" — if
  they answer with the owner, the head-noun concept is the gap.
- Catches participle openers but misses prepositional/appositive ones →
  **ing-word-required**.
- Lets a which-clause modify a distant noun and defends it by meaning →
  **which-floats-free**.
- Slow, full-parse solving with correct answers → not an error pattern but an
  efficiency gap; teach boundary-scanning (these should be among the fastest
  items on the test).

## Remediation pathways
- **meaning-rescues-syntax / choices-first-solving**: the literal-reading drill —
  read the assembled sentence deadpan-literally and say what it claims (the
  absurd image does the teaching). Then install the ritual: name the required
  noun BEFORE looking at any choice.
- **possessive-counts-as-the-person**: head-noun drills on possessive phrases
  ("whose discovery? — so the phrase IS the discovery"); links to head-noun work
  in [[apostrophe-possession-grid]]. Contrast pair: "Curie's discovery…" vs
  "Curie, whose discovery…" after the same opener.
- **ing-word-required**: expose them to a sorted set of openers (participial /
  prepositional / appositive) all running the same who-question; the question
  generalizes, the -ing marker doesn't.
- **which-floats-free**: the touch-rule — which describes the noun it touches,
  period; drill by moving the clause around one sentence and describing how the
  claim changes.
- If boundary-noun identification itself fails, revisit subject identification
  in [[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]] — same underlying parse.

## Mastery criteria
Names the required noun before reading choices; solves via boundary-scan (fast);
rejects clean-but-dangling choices and possessive openers; correctly ACCEPTS
stacked-label subjects (the allowance) — over-triggering on those is the
mirror-image failure. Proof: hard-band accuracy on all four rotations of the
item (main clause after intro phrase / the intro phrase itself / parenthetical /
clause before a fixed tail).

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: participial opener, one choice starts with the obvious right noun,
  others are category-absurd.
- **medium**: passive-voice rewrites smuggle the wrong opener in; reverse items
  where the choice must END with the matching noun; non-participial openers.
- **hard**: the possessive trap; clean-but-dangling sets where all four are
  grammatical; parenthetical rotations; and composites — modifier placement +
  voice choice in one set, or modifier + clause-boundary decisions (composing
  the boundaries cluster), or adjacency + parallel form of an attached relative
  clause (composing [[parallel-structure]]).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: lead with the accidental-comedy examples — absurd literal
  readings are memorable and teach the literal-parse habit painlessly. One
  question to install: "who's doing this? — then they must come right after the
  comma."
- **Average**: the who-question plus boundary-scanning economy; teach all four
  item rotations so the skill isn't anchored to intro-phrase-then-blank shape.
- **Advanced**: the possessive trap, the complete-subject allowance (when NOT to
  flag), and passive rewrites as the standard smuggling vehicle for wrong
  openers.
- **1500+**: this is disproportionately a 1500+ node — the clean-but-dangling
  archetype specifically targets students whose isolation-checking is perfect.
  Message it as such ("your grammar is fine; re-attach to the frame"), and drill
  minimal pairs where the ONLY difference among two clean choices is the
  boundary noun. Add the rare subtypes (limiting words, squinting adverbs) for
  completeness.
