# Essential vs non-essential — commas as claims about meaning

## What it is
The semantic principle underneath the deletion test: whether a modifier is
bracketed is a claim about MEANING, not decoration. No brackets = the modifier
RESTRICTS — it narrows which thing you're talking about, and the claim is only
about that narrowed set. Brackets = the modifier is an aside — the head noun is
meant in full, and the modifier just adds commentary. This node covers the
cases where that meaning choice is the whole question: who/which clauses,
that-clauses, appositives, and names/titles.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
This is the one place in Boundaries where punctuation carries propositional
content — the commas change what the sentence asserts. *Students who slept
well outperformed the rest* (only the sleepers did) vs *Students, who slept
well, outperformed the rest* (all of them did, and by the way they slept).
Because the rule is grounded in meaning, it is fully re-derivable: a student
who forgets "rules about appositive commas" can reconstruct the answer by
asking what the sentence intends to claim. The exam tests it precisely because
rote comma rules fail here — the same surface structure takes commas in one
passage and refuses them in the next, and only the context's logic decides.

## Mental model
**Brackets = "I mean the whole thing; this is an aside." No brackets = "I mean
only the ones this picks out."** The case book, each case being this principle
plus one discriminator:
- **that-clauses:** definitionally restrictive — *that* only ever narrows, so
  no comma before or after it in ordinary use. If *that* is dropped (legal
  after many verbs/nouns), no comma may take its place. Rare carve-out: a true
  interrupter may sit right after *that*, bringing its own comma pair.
- **which/who clauses:** swing both ways; the commas flip the claim. The
  passage's logic picks: does the statement only make sense for the subgroup
  the clause picks out (→ restrictive, no commas), or is the head noun meant
  generally (→ aside, comma pair)?
- **Appositives and names — the uniqueness question:** brackets assert the
  descriptor picks out a UNIQUE referent; no brackets assert one-of-many.
  *Her only sister, Maren, lives abroad* (only one sister → the name is extra
  → brackets) vs *her colleague Maren* (several colleagues → the name is
  doing the picking → bare). Mid-sentence names allow exactly two normal
  options: comma pair or nothing. A single comma BEFORE a name is always
  wrong; a single comma AFTER one can be earned only for an independent
  structural reason.
- **Titles before names:** a profession/label used restrictively
  (*microbiologist Ana Reyes*) takes no commas — students who learned
  "appositives take commas" without the distinction comma it anyway. And the
  sanctioned exception worth knowing: an essential title can still be followed
  by a single STRUCTURAL comma earned by something else (a trailing
  appositive, a leading dependent chunk). An official digital-SAT release has
  tested exactly this shape (unverified-claim — verify the item citation
  against our records before the tutor asserts it).

## Expert reasoning process
1. Run [[delete-and-reread]] for grammatical removability — that's the entry
   ticket, not the verdict.
2. Ask the meaning question: with the modifier gone, does the discourse still
   know exactly who/what is meant? Yes → bracketing is available.
3. For names/appositives, interrogate uniqueness: "is this the only such
   thing in context?" Only-one → brackets; one-of-many → bare.
4. For who/which, read the surrounding claim: would it be false or absurd
   applied to the whole head-noun set? Then the clause must restrict → no
   commas.
5. For *that*: no decision needed — kill any comma touching it (checking only
   for the rare interrupter-after-that carve-out).
6. Cross-check with [[pair-completeness]]: whatever meaning verdict you reach,
   the marks must come as a matched pair or not at all.

## Misconceptions
- **which-and-who-always-take-a-comma** — A half-remembered that/which school
  rule hardened into "relative clause → comma." Reality: *which* asides do
  bracket, but *who* clauses bracket only when non-restrictive, and the
  meaning flips with the commas. Produces confident wrong picks where context
  demands restriction.
- **appositives-always-take-commas** — Learned as a punctuation rule with the
  uniqueness condition stripped. Produces commas around one-of-many
  descriptors (falsely asserting uniqueness) and around restrictive titles
  before names — a recurring distractor design.
- **comma-before-that-feels-polite** — After a long noun phrase, a comma
  before *that* relieves the "sentence feels long" pressure. Root: pause
  intuition ([[punctuating-by-ear]]); the cure is categorical (that =
  restrictive, no comma).
- **commas-are-meaning-neutral** — The deepest version: students believe
  commas here are stylistic, so they pick by feel and can't use context at
  all. They treat bracketed and bare versions as interchangeable "both fine"
  options — the exam makes exactly one consistent with the passage's logic.
- **single-comma-before-a-name** — Students open a bracket before a name and
  never close it, or treat *"X, Name"* as a politeness convention — conflating
  direct-address and list commas with appositive brackets. Cheap, frequent
  trap; overlaps [[pair-completeness]].

## Diagnostic indicators
- **which-and-who-always-take-a-comma:** commas added to every relative
  clause; on flip items (context demands restriction), they miss and justify
  with the school rule verbatim.
- **appositives-always-take-commas:** commas around titles/names regardless of
  uniqueness; when asked "how many such people are there in this context?"
  they've never considered the question.
- **comma-before-that-feels-polite:** comma-before-that picks specifically on
  long-subject stems; length-sensitivity is the tell.
- **commas-are-meaning-neutral:** answers "both sound fine" on
  bracketed-vs-bare contrast items; explanations never reference what the
  sentence claims.
- **single-comma-before-a-name:** half-bracket picks around names; can't say
  what job the single comma does when probed.

## Remediation pathways
- **which-and-who-always-take-a-comma:** meaning-flip pair — one sentence
  where the commas make the claim absurd (*Employees, who missed the
  deadline, were warned* when only some missed it). Let the absurdity teach
  the stakes; then the subgroup question as the routine.
- **appositives-always-take-commas:** the only-one vs one-of-many drill: ten
  descriptor+name pairs, sole task is answering "unique in context?" before
  any punctuation. Punctuation follows for free.
- **comma-before-that-feels-polite:** categorical rule plus
  [[punctuating-by-ear]] confrontation on long stems.
- **commas-are-meaning-neutral:** paraphrase task — restate the bracketed and
  bare versions as different claims in their own words; if they can't, drop
  back to [[delete-and-reread]] to rebuild the aside concept first.
- **single-comma-before-a-name:** route to [[pair-completeness]] (two-or-none
  discipline), then return for the uniqueness layer.

## Mastery criteria
The student can (a) state the claim difference between the bracketed and bare
versions of a sentence in their own words, (b) answer name/title items via the
uniqueness question, and (c) treat *that* categorically. Proof: correct picks
on hard who/which flip items where the passage's logic, not a rule, decides —
with meaning-based justifications.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Medium:** that vs which contrasts; unique-referent appositives with clear
  context (a work's author, someone's only sibling).
- **Hard:** who-clause meaning flips requiring passage-level logic; restrictive
  titles with an earned structural comma after them (the two-rules-at-once
  shape); uniqueness calls needing world-knowledge-free contextual inference.
  Composes with [[pair-completeness]] (the marks) and [[delete-and-reread]]
  (the span) — the hardest items make all three fire at once.
This node has no easy band on the exam: purely mechanical bracket items are
tested via [[pair-completeness]]; meaning enters at medium.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling:** teach only two things — that-never-commas, and the
  only-one/one-of-many question for names. Skip who-flips entirely until the
  deletion test is second nature.
- **Average:** the meaning-flip pair is the anchor lesson; build the habit of
  asking "what does this sentence CLAIM with and without the commas?" on
  every relative-clause item.
- **Advanced:** who-clause flips against context; the essential-title +
  structural-comma exception; explain the system top-down (brackets are a
  restrictiveness signal) so every case is derivable.
- **1500+:** the residual risk is over-confidence in rules where meaning
  decides. Drill items where their memorized rule and the context disagree,
  and the context wins; require them to articulate the claim difference in
  one sentence before answering.
