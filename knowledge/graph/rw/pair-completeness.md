# Pair completeness — interrupter marks come two-or-none, and matched

## What it is
The mechanical law of interrupters: bracketing punctuation is a two-part
enclosure. If a span is non-essential, BOTH brackets must exist, and whatever
mark opens the bracket must close it. Comma pair, dash pair, and parenthesis
pair are grammatically interchangeable enclosures (dashes read as a harder
break, parentheses as a whisper — pure style); the iron rules are: never half
a pair, never a mixed pair, never a doubled pair. One carve-out: a
non-essential TRANSITION word takes commas specifically.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The generative why: brackets are a signal with two jobs — telling the reader
where the aside STARTS and where the spine RESUMES. Half a bracket does only
one job, leaving the reader unable to find the spine again; mismatched marks
break the signal's identity (the closing mark is recognized as a closer only
because it repeats the opener). The SAT loves this territory because the traps
are cheap to write and lethal to ear-punctuators: a single comma placed where
the voice dips is the cheapest wrong answer in the section. The exam tests
broken, mismatched, and redundant pairs — but never asks students to PREFER
commas vs dashes vs parens when all are correct (unverified-claim, consistent
across sources; verify against bank).

## Mental model
**Two-or-none, and like closes like.** The complete trap inventory this rule
kills:
1. **Half-open bracket** — opening mark present, closing absent (or vice
   versa).
2. **Mismatched pair** — comma...dash, dash...paren, paren...comma hybrids.
   The pair-level equivalence ("commas, dashes, parens all work") was learned
   correctly but applied at the level of the single mark.
3. **Redundant double bracket** — commas wrapped around parentheses; each
   system already says "removable," stacking is wrong.
4. **Sentence-strength mark closing an interrupter** — a semicolon or period
   where the closing bracket should be leaves a fragment on one side
   ([[sentence-fragment-connectors]]'s two-sentence stamp kills it).
5. **Earned single mark after a close** — a comma may follow a closing
   parenthesis only when required for an INDEPENDENT reason (a coordinator
   joining clauses next, a leading dependent chunk ending) — never merely to
   "re-close" the bracket.
The transition-word exception (commas only) exists because dashes/parens would
promote a one-word connective into a dramatic aside — the mark's weight must
fit the span's weight. (Style rationale; the testable rule is just: bracketed
transitions take commas.)

## Expert reasoning process
1. **Left-scan first** (inherited from [[delete-and-reread]]): before judging
   the blank, sweep left for an unpaired comma, dash, or open paren. Found →
   the blank almost certainly closes it with the SAME mark; every choice with
   a different mark or no mark dies immediately — often three of four.
2. Confirm the span with delete-and-reread (the pair must fence a genuinely
   removable span — see [[essential-vs-nonessential]] for the meaning layer).
3. Verify two-and-only-two: count the bracket marks the candidate answer
   leaves around the span. One → dead. Three (double bracket) → dead.
4. Check the close's aftermath: any additional mark after the closing bracket
   must earn its place independently.
5. Style is never the question: if two choices differ only comma-pair vs
   dash-pair around the same span, something else in them must differ — find
   it ([[boundary-equivalence-cancellation]]).

## Misconceptions
- **half-bracket-by-feel** — The student places one comma where the voice
  dips and never thinks "where does this span END?" Root: commas learned as
  point-events (pauses) rather than as paired fences —
  [[punctuating-by-ear]]'s cheapest harvest. Produces single-comma answers on
  both edges of interrupters.
- **marks-are-freely-swappable** — Having learned the pair equivalence,
  students accept comma-open/dash-close hybrids: the equivalence was learned
  at pair level, applied at mark level. Distinctively, this one afflicts
  BETTER-taught students — it's a misapplied truth, not ignorance.
- **more-brackets-more-careful** — Redundant double bracketing (commas around
  parentheses) reads as extra-diligent. Cousin of the
  more-punctuation-looks-more-correct bias ([[punctuating-by-ear]]).
- **paren-close-needs-a-comma** — Reflexively re-closing a parenthesis with a
  comma "to be safe," instead of asking whether anything independent earns
  one. Both directions get tested: the unneeded comma, and the NEEDED one
  (before a coordinator) that students omit because the paren "already
  closed things."

## Diagnostic indicators
- **half-bracket-by-feel:** single-comma picks around interrupters; when asked
  "where does the aside end?" they haven't located an endpoint; sound
  vocabulary in explanations.
- **marks-are-freely-swappable:** accepts hybrid open/close pairs while
  correctly explaining that "dashes and commas both work" — the correct rule
  cited in defense of the wrong answer is the signature.
- **more-brackets-more-careful:** picks the most-marked choice on paren items;
  eliminates the bare-but-correct option early.
- **paren-close-needs-a-comma:** systematic on close-paren items in both
  directions; can't articulate a JOB for the comma they added.

## Remediation pathways
- **half-bracket-by-feel:** the fence image — one fence post doesn't make a
  pen. Drill: given the opening mark, FIND the closing position before
  looking at choices. If spans can't be found, drop to [[delete-and-reread]].
- **marks-are-freely-swappable:** name the level error explicitly ("the pair
  is swappable; the halves are not") and drill open→close prediction: shown
  an opening dash, say what the closing mark must be before reading on.
- **more-brackets-more-careful:** counterexample audit — three items where the
  least-marked choice is keyed; then the rule: every mark must name its job,
  redundancy is a job-less mark.
- **paren-close-needs-a-comma:** the earned-comma question as a ritual:
  "what OTHER rule requires a mark here?" No answer → no mark.

## Mastery criteria
Given an item with one bracket edge visible, the student predicts the closing
mark and its position before reading the choices; kills hybrids and half-pairs
on sight; adds post-close marks only with a named independent reason. Proof:
near-perfect accuracy on medium pair items and correct handling of
close-paren+coordinator compositions at hard.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy:** short interrupter, both edges near the blank, half-open comma
  bracket as the decoy.
- **Medium:** dash and parenthesis pairs; mismatched-pair decoys; the
  bracketed transition (commas required).
- **Hard:** long spans where the opener sits far left of the underline
  ([[window-widening]] + left-scan); earned-comma-after-paren compositions;
  pair questions stacked on uniqueness meaning calls
  ([[essential-vs-nonessential]]); post-close sabotage (-ing form or fresh
  pronoun after the close, manufacturing a fragment or splice once the span
  is stripped — [[delete-and-reread]]'s verb-after-close check).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling:** the fence-pen image plus two-or-none as a chant. Drill only
  comma pairs first; one mark family, one rule, high reps.
- **Average:** add the like-closes-like law and the left-scan habit — the
  left-scan alone converts many misses, because the decisive evidence was
  never near the blank.
- **Advanced:** the full trap inventory as a checklist they can name; hybrids
  and redundant brackets as "misapplied truth" errors worth intellectual
  respect (this framing lands with strong students).
- **1500+:** compositions only: earned commas after closes, post-close
  sabotage, and spans opened 25+ words back. Also teach the meta: style
  choices among correct pairs are untested (unverified-claim), so any
  apparent comma-vs-dash coin flip means they've mis-read one of the choices.
