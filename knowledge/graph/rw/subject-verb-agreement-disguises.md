# Subject-verb agreement: hunting the real subject through six disguises

## What it is
A conjugated verb must match its subject in number. The rule itself is trivial —
what the SAT actually tests is FINDING the subject, because on real items the
subject is never sitting next to the verb. Some separation or reordering device is
always in the way [unverified-claim: "never adjacent" is a strong empirical claim
from prep-source analysis; verify against our bank before the tutor asserts it].
Note the inversion quirk of present-tense verbs: the -s form is the SINGULAR verb
("the result suggests"), the bare form is plural — opposite to how nouns work.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Agreement probes whether a reader can track a sentence's skeleton (who does what)
through academic-register prose full of embeddings. It appears at every difficulty
band; the disguise used — not the rule — sets the difficulty. Choice sets usually
vary tense as well as number, but on agreement items the tense variation is pure
distraction [unverified-claim: verify the "tense never decides agreement items"
absolute against our bank].

## Mental model
**Strip the camouflage, weld subject to verb.** Everything between the subject and
the verb is one of a small set of removable disguises. Mentally cross out the
disguise; the noun left standing in the subject slot decides the verb. The six
disguises, in rough difficulty order:

1. **Prepositional phrase** — "the range of results" → strike "of results"; the
   phrase's object can never be the subject.
2. **Comma-paired interrupter** — a non-essential clause between subject and verb;
   strike everything between the comma pair (same machinery as
   [[essential-vs-nonessential]] in the boundaries cluster).
3. **Essential that-clause** — works like an interrupter but with no commas to
   mark it; also, a relative pronoun INSIDE such a clause inherits the number of
   the noun it modifies, so a disagreement can live inside the insert itself.
4. **Compound subject** — two nouns joined by "and" are plural, period, even when
   each half is singular and the compound is long or split.
5. **Expletive "there"** — in "there is/are" frames the verb agrees with the noun
   AFTER it.
6. **Inversion** — the verb arrives before the subject (often after a fronted
   place/among/along phrase, sometimes with no preposition at all). The odd word
   order is the bait; hunt the post-verb subject and match it. These are the
   hardest to hear.

A logical cross-check backs the mechanical one: ask "what actually performs this
action / holds this predicate?" A nearby noun that can't logically do the action
is not the subject.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Choice-set triage ([[choice-set-diagnosis]]): a number split among the verb
   choices means agreement; ignore tense variation from that point on.
2. Hunt the subject: strike prepositional phrases and comma-paired inserts as one
   reflex; check for inversion if the verb comes early or the sentence opens with
   a location phrase.
3. Compound scan: before accepting a singular verb, sweep the whole subject region
   for an "and" that widens the subject.
4. Assign number to the survivor using [[grammatical-number-assignment]] (gerund
   subjects, collectives, indefinite pronouns, clause subjects).
5. Match, then plug in and reread the full sentence — also confirming the choice
   didn't smuggle in an illegal comma between subject and verb
   ([[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]]).

## Misconceptions
- **nearest-noun-is-subject** — the student matches the verb to the adjacent noun.
  Forms from a proximity heuristic that is usually right in casual speech; every
  disguise structure exists specifically to weaponize it. This is the same
  proximity/adjacency root that produces the boundaries cluster's
  **comma-equals-pause** habit — both are "trust the local surface" heuristics;
  link the remediation, don't teach them as unrelated.
- **fix-by-ear-with-tense** — hearing that something is off, the student reaches
  for a tense change because tense differences are audible and number slips are
  not. Forms because casual speech tolerates agreement errors but not tense
  errors. Produces confident picks of a wrong-number verb in a different tense —
  the test's number-flip-with-tense-noise distractor is built for exactly this.
- **odd-syntax-feels-wrong** — on inverted sentences the student "repairs" the
  strange word order by matching the verb to the fronted phrase's noun. Forms
  because inversion is rare in student writing, so unfamiliarity reads as error.
- **appositive-steals-the-number** — a comma-wrapped rename after the subject
  ("X, a series of…," ) baits the verb into agreeing with the rename's noun.
  A sub-case of nearest-noun, worth its own name because students who've learned
  to strike prep phrases still fall for it.
- **agreement-plus-comma-bundle** — the student accepts a choice that fixes the
  verb but inserts a comma between subject and verb, because the verb was the only
  thing they were checking. Forms from one-axis solving; the exam fuses agreement
  with boundary errors in one choice set.

## Diagnostic indicators
- Picks the wrong-number verb whose number matches the noun physically nearest the
  blank → **nearest-noun-is-subject**. Strongest signal when the item has a prep
  phrase or appositive; if the miss is specifically on comma-wrapped renames →
  **appositive-steals-the-number**.
- Picks a wrong-number verb and explains with time reasoning ("the passage is in
  past tense") → **fix-by-ear-with-tense**; the student solved the decoration axis.
- Accuracy collapses only on inverted or "there is/are" items → 
  **odd-syntax-feels-wrong**; subject-hunting works, but only left-to-right.
- Picks choices with a comma splitting subject from verb → 
  **agreement-plus-comma-bundle**; also log evidence against the boundaries
  cluster's comma-placement skills.
- Correct on short items, wrong on long ones with the same structure → not a rule
  gap but a parsing-stamina gap; the disguise-stripping reflex isn't automatic yet.

## Remediation pathways
- **nearest-noun-is-subject**: the strike-through ritual — physically (or
  verbally, on-screen) cross out every prep phrase and comma-paired insert, then
  read subject and verb welded together. Counterexample pair: same sentence with
  the disguise removed, where the student's ear gets it right instantly — proves
  the rule was never the problem. If it doesn't land, revisit
  prepositional-phrase identification (foundations).
- **fix-by-ear-with-tense**: show the same choice set twice — once as given, once
  with all choices forced into one tense — and have them notice the item didn't
  change. Then re-teach axis triage ([[choice-set-diagnosis]]).
- **odd-syntax-feels-wrong**: teach the inversion protocol as a question: "what IS
  [predicate]?" — the answer is the subject; its number sets the verb. Drill a set
  of fronted-phrase sentences only.
- **appositive-steals-the-number**: contrast pair with the appositive deleted.
- **agreement-plus-comma-bundle**: two-pass checking — verb first, marks second —
  and revisit [[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]].

## Mastery criteria
Can name the subject out loud before choosing, on all six disguise types, and gets
hard-band items (inversion, inverted compounds, disagreement inside an insert)
right consistently — including fused agreement + comma items where they must
reject a right-verb/wrong-comma choice. Evidence: no nearest-noun picks across a
mixed set that includes at least two disguise types they previously missed.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: single prep phrase between subject and verb; distractors are category
  mismatches.
- **medium**: comma-paired interrupter or essential that-clause; compound subjects
  split across a long line; tense noise added to the choice set.
- **hard**: inversion (especially inverted compound subjects, which hide the "and"
  behind the verb), disagreement inside the interrupter, expletive frames in dense
  prose, and fused items where the choice must be right on number AND boundary at
  once — composing [[grammatical-number-assignment]], [[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]],
  and [[choice-set-diagnosis]].

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: one disguise at a time, starting with prep phrases. Teach the -s
  inversion fact explicitly ("verb -s = singular — opposite of nouns"); many weak
  students have never articulated it. Physical crossing-out on paper first, then
  rehearse doing it mentally for the on-screen exam.
- **Average**: teach the six disguises as a checklist and drill mixed sets. The
  key habit shift is choices-first triage so tense noise stops costing them.
- **Advanced**: live mostly on inversion and inside-the-insert disagreements. Have
  them predict where the trap is before solving ("the subject will be after the
  verb here — why?").
- **1500+**: errors are almost always fused-axis items or speed. Drill
  agreement + comma bundles and inverted compounds; train the reflex of verifying
  the full sentence after plugging in, since their single-axis accuracy is already
  ceiling.
