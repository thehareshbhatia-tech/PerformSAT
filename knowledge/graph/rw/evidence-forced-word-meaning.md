# Evidence-forced word meaning — the word is a variable; the passage is the equation

## What it is
The governing invariant of Words in Context: a word (or blank) means whatever the surrounding evidence forces it to mean. The question is never "what does this word mean in general?" but "what idea belongs at this position?" This holds for both question shapes — blank-fill items ("most logical and precise word") where the idea must be derived and matched to a candidate, and word-probe items ("as used in the text, X most nearly means") where the printed word's dictionary meaning is often irrelevant or an active trap.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Words in Context is a recurring item family in every R&W module (unverified-claim: roughly ten per test, with blank-fill heavily dominating over word-probe — verify against our bank before asserting). The design guarantee that makes the skill testable: the passage always constructs the definition of the required word — no item requires outside topic knowledge (unverified-claim; consistent with observed items but gate through bank stats). The exam is probing evidence-driven reading, not vocabulary recall: it samples the register of scholarly prose — argumentation verbs, quantity/variety nouns, charged evaluative adjectives — rather than exotic memorization-list words (unverified-claim on the distribution).

## Mental model
**Solve for x.** Treat the blank or target word as a variable in an equation whose terms are: (a) the evidence phrase — the specific nearby words that constrain the slot, and (b) the connector family ([[connector-logic-families]]) linking evidence to slot — echo it, explain/cause it, or oppose it. An expert computes the slot's required content from structure first and only then looks at candidate words. The printed word in a word-probe item is close to a placeholder; loyalty to its familiar meaning is the trap.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Read the full context window ([[context-window-discipline]]), not just the slot's sentence.
2. Find the evidence phrase and classify the connector joining it to the slot: same-direction → the slot echoes the evidence; cause/effect or colon/dash → the slot supplies reason or consequence; reversal → the slot opposes the evidence.
3. Predict a rough filler in your own words — **only if confident**. A sloppy approximation is fine; a half-sure guess is worse than none, because a planted anchor primes you to reject the right answer. When unsure, skip straight to polarity work ([[polarity-first-elimination]]).
4. Sweep choices in order. Work from words you know toward words you don't: quarantine unknowns, prove knowns in or out first, and pick an unknown only by exhaustion. An unknown distractor's meaning never blocks a verifiable correct answer.
5. Apply the overclaim test to survivors: a choice wins only if its full definition is *paid for* by the evidence. If a candidate implies extra content — newness, agency, causation, a first-of-its-kind role — the passage must state that content; absence of that evidence eliminates the choice.
6. Final gate — substitute and listen: drop the survivor into the sentence and verify logical and grammatical coherence. "Sounds unfamiliar to me" is not evidence against a choice; slot-logic failure is.
7. Red flag throughout: distrust any choice whose appeal is topical association (a word that belongs to the passage's subject area) rather than slot logic.

## Misconceptions
- **single-meaning-belief** — Each word has the one meaning I learned. Formed by vocab-list schooling pairing every word with exactly one gloss. On word-probe items it produces the familiar-sense decoy pick — and the test writes that decoy into essentially every such item. Working rule: presume the everyday sense wrong until nothing else fits.
- **sounds-right-heuristic** — Accepting or rejecting words by acoustic familiarity ("I'd never say it that way"). Formed because language was learned by ear, so personal usage feels like evidence. Produces elimination of correct second-sense answers and retention of fluent-sounding wrong ones.
- **forced-prediction-habit** — Misapplying "predict first" by planting a low-confidence guess that then biases matching. Formed by treating prediction as a mandatory step rather than a conditional one. Produces rejection of the credited answer for failing to match a wrong anchor.
- **topic-association-trust** — Believing a word is supported because it belongs to the passage's subject matter or matches another word physically present nearby. Produces topic-association and nearby-synonym-bait picks that "feel textually supported" because the student literally saw a cousin of the word.
- **unknown-word-panic** — Freezing or guessing randomly because some *choices* are unknown, when identifying the right answer never requires defining the wrong ones. Produces abandonment and time hemorrhage on items that were solvable from the known choices alone.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **single-meaning-belief**: on word-probe items, picks the choice matching the target's everyday sense; explanation is a dictionary recital, no passage evidence cited.
- **sounds-right-heuristic**: eliminates the credited answer and says "you can't use it that way"; picks collocationally fluent wrong choices; errors concentrate on second-sense items.
- **forced-prediction-habit**: scratch work shows a predicted word unrelated to the evidence; rejects the credited answer as "not what I was looking for."
- **topic-association-trust**: picks the choice sharing semantic field with the passage topic (or matching a nearby word) over the choice matching the slot's logic; fast, confident wrong answers.
- **unknown-word-panic**: disproportionate time on items with rare distractors; blank or changed answers there; says "I didn't know two of the words so I guessed."

## Remediation pathways
- **single-meaning-belief**: show three sentences forcing three different meanings from one common word — meaning is location-dependent, full stop. Then the presumption rule for word-probe items. If unfixed, prereq is [[connector-logic-families]] (they can't compute the slot's demand).
- **sounds-right-heuristic**: replace "does it sound right?" with "does its definition satisfy the evidence?" — drill items where the credited answer sounds stilted but verifies, and a fluent choice fails.
- **forced-prediction-habit**: make prediction explicitly conditional: confident → predict; else → charge-tag only. Show a worked case where a planted guess caused rejection of the right answer.
- **topic-association-trust**: dissect a topic-association decoy — the word belongs to the subject but its definition breaks the slot's logic. Rule: association is a red flag, not support.
- **unknown-word-panic**: teach quarantine explicitly — park unknowns, verify knowns; demonstrate an item solved with two choices never defined. Morphology enters only as a bounded tiebreaker (see [[second-sense-vocabulary]]).

## Mastery criteria
The student can, on demand: name the evidence phrase and connector family for any WiC item; state whether they predicted or fell back to polarity and why; justify the winner by definition-vs-evidence (including running the overclaim test); and solve items containing unknown distractors without defining them. Proof: consistent accuracy on medium/hard blank-fill AND word-probe items, with familiar-sense and topic-association decoys absent from their error log.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: single sentence; connector adjacent to the blank; explicit evidence phrase; common words in primary senses.
- **Medium**: evidence one sentence away; one negation to compose; one associated-but-wrong decoy; a choice set with one or two unfamiliar words.
- **Hard**: stacked negations/downgraders ([[charge-composition-under-modifiers]]); second-sense correct answers ([[second-sense-vocabulary]]); literary or period prose where common words drift from modern usage; intensity and time-direction discriminations; syntax long enough that mapping the sentence's relationships — not vocabulary — is the real task.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach the equation metaphor with cloze sentences using easy words; the win condition is *pointing at the evidence phrase*, not knowing words. Delay word-probe items until blank-fill logic is solid.
- **Average**: full pipeline (window → connector → conditional predict → sweep → substitute); introduce the overclaim test as a named tool; start mixing word-probe items with the presumption rule.
- **Advanced**: hard-mode composition — negation stacking, second senses, quarantining unknowns under time pressure; per-distractor postmortems naming the decoy species.
- **1500+**: period prose and poetry, where the method must run without modern-usage intuition; have them author a blank-fill item with one topic-association and one overclaim decoy — designing traps proves the model.
