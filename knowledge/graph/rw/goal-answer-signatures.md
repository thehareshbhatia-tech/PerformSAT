# Answer signatures: what the right choice looks like, per goal type

## What it is
Recurring goal types have recognizable correct-answer **fingerprints** — observable surface features the right choice must display. Knowing them converts checklist verification into a fast feature scan: instead of contemplating each choice, you predict the fingerprint from the goal and hunt for it.

- **Similarity/difference goals** → the answer contains an explicit comparative or contrastive marker (a likeness word or a difference word). Merely *mentioning both subjects is not comparing them*.
- **Unfamiliar-audience goals** ("for readers unfamiliar with X") → the answer embeds identity/background information (who the person is, what the thing is) and typically introduces a person by full name. Correct answers here tend to run longer than the distractors (**unverified-claim** as a meta-cue — verify against official items before the tutor uses length as evidence).
- **Familiar-audience goals** → the answer omits background; a choice that re-explains what the audience already knows is signalling its own wrongness.
- **Introduce-the-goal/aim-of-a-study** → the answer states what the researchers *set out to learn* (intent framing), not what they *found* (results framing).
- **Emphasize-variety/range goals** → the answer enumerates or characterizes multiple kinds; a single vivid instance fails the goal.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The distractor set for each goal type is built by deleting or swapping exactly one fingerprint feature, so the signatures are simultaneously the recognition key and the trap map: right-topic-wrong-function (informs instead of compares); both-subjects-no-relation (names both entities — even stating one descends from the other — without a likeness/difference assertion); opposite-relation marker (a difference word where similarity was asked — eliminable on the marker alone); single-instance-for-range; background-omitted vs background-only (one distractor has the focus but no introduction, the other the introduction but not the focus — the answer welds both); findings-for-purpose swap (results offered where intent was asked — often the more interesting-sounding option, which is the bait); and the last-name-only cue in unfamiliar-audience items.

## Mental model
**Every goal type has a uniform.** Comparison answers wear a comparison word; new-audience answers wear a background clause; study-aim answers wear intent verbs. Distractors are the same body in the wrong uniform. You check the uniform before you check the body — the marker-word scan alone kills half a choice set. The comparative/contrastive/causal markers themselves are the same word families catalogued in [[transition-relationship-families]], now spotted *inside answer choices* instead of blanks.

## Expert reasoning process
1. From the decomposed goal ([[goal-decomposition-checklist]]), classify the goal type.
2. Recall the fingerprint: which surface features are mandatory, which are disqualifying.
3. Scan choices at feature level first — presence/absence of the marker word, the background clause, the plural enumeration — before reading any choice for full meaning.
4. On feature survivors, run the full checklist.
5. Use the trap map in post-mortem: a wrong pick names which feature the student failed to demand.

## Misconceptions
- **mention-equals-relation** — the student credits a comparison goal to any choice naming both subjects, because school "compare and contrast" work accepts discussing two things side by side. Produces picks of the both-subjects-no-relation distractor. The fix-defining fact: a comparison must *assert* a likeness or difference with a marker, not just co-mention.
- **results-are-the-point** — on study-aim goals, the student gravitates to findings because results feel like the newsworthy content; school science writing rewards leading with conclusions. Produces the findings-for-purpose swap miss. The intent/result distinction is a framing read, not a content read.
- **background-is-filler** — the student trims mentally toward the "cleanest" choice, treating identity/background clauses as padding, so on unfamiliar-audience goals they pick the focused-but-unintroduced distractor. Forms from concision training ("cut needless words"). Audience qualifiers make background *mandatory*, not optional.
- **audience-blindness** — the student ignores the audience qualifier entirely, treating familiar- and unfamiliar-audience goals as identical. Forms because audience is a rhetoric concept rarely operationalized in school grading. Produces symmetric misses: background-only picks for familiar audiences, background-free picks for unfamiliar ones.

## Diagnostic indicators
Each trap is a labeled probe — distractor choice maps to root cause:
- picks **both-subjects-no-relation** → mention-equals-relation; explanation says "it talks about both of them."
- picks **findings-for-purpose** → results-are-the-point; explanation cites how interesting/specific the finding is.
- picks **background-omitted** on unfamiliar-audience items → background-is-filler; explanation calls the right answer "wordy."
- picks the **opposite-relation marker** → not reading the marker at all — a scan-discipline lapse rather than a concept gap; usually co-occurs with speed pressure.
- misses on *both* audience directions → audience-blindness; the student cannot say who the stated audience was after answering.

## Remediation pathways
- **mention-equals-relation**: minimal pair — two choices identical except one adds a likeness marker; ask which one *performs* the comparison. Then have the student circle the marker word in every comparison answer for a session.
- **results-are-the-point**: reframe drill — given a finding-framed sentence, rewrite it as intent-framed and vice versa; once the student can transform the framing, they can see it. 
- **background-is-filler / audience-blindness**: audience-swap exercise — same notes, same focus, goal written for a familiar then an unfamiliar audience; the correct answer flips, proving audience is load-bearing. Revisit [[goal-decomposition-checklist]] if the qualifier isn't even being extracted.
- If several signatures stay confusable, the upstream gap is marker-word recognition — revisit [[transition-relationship-families]].

## Mastery criteria
Given a goal sentence alone, the student can predict the fingerprint ("the answer will contain a difference word"; "the answer will introduce her with a full name and a role") before seeing choices, and their misses no longer land on the type-specific designed distractor. Proof: hard notes items answered at scan speed with elimination reasons stated in feature terms.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: fingerprint feature is blatant (an unmistakable likeness word) and only one choice carries it.
- **medium**: the designed distractor appears — right topic in the wrong uniform — so the feature scan must be paired with the checklist.
- **hard**: two features must be welded (background + focus; relation + specific category level), with distractors each carrying exactly one; or the fingerprint is subtler (intent vs results framing with overlapping vocabulary). At this end the item is simultaneously testing [[goal-decomposition-checklist]] category precision.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach two signatures only — comparison-needs-a-marker and new-audience-needs-background — as concrete rules with circled words. Breadth comes later.
- **Average**: the full catalog as a goal-type → fingerprint table they can reproduce; drill one goal type at a time, then mixed.
- **Advanced**: trap-first teaching — show the six distractor designs and have the student author the *wrong* answers for a given goal; building traps inoculates against them.
- **1500+**: the marginal gains are the subtle cues — framing reads (intent vs result), the last-name-only signal, and disciplined skepticism about the unverified length cue (usable as a tiebreaker instinct, never as primary evidence).
