# Support and weaken: judging new findings against a stated claim

## What it is
The textual Command of Evidence shape: a passage states a hypothesis, theory, or conclusion, and the choices offer four *new* findings; the student picks the one that most directly supports the claim — or, on the weaken variant, the one most inconsistent with it. This is the open-door task ([[premise-completion-vs-new-evidence]]): choices are granted as true and judged only for their bearing. It extends text completion by one move — instead of closing an argument, you evaluate an outside statement's force against it.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
This is the exam's most direct probe of scientific reasoning: can the student tell which observation actually bears on a specific causal story? Difficulty is manufactured almost entirely in the distractors, which are engineered against known student weaknesses: topic-matching, direction-blindness, condition-swapping, and partial reading. Support/weaken items skew toward science and social-science passages (unverified-claim, plausible — verify against bank composition).

## Mental model
**Claim → target → audit.** Three beats, never skipped, always in order: (1) isolate and plainly restate the claim; (2) independently characterize what would qualify — direction plus *mechanism*; (3) only then read choices, auditing each against the written target. The mechanism beat is what separates this node from generic direction-matching: **evidence must engage the claim's specific causal story, not its topic.** If the claim says a particular component causes the benefit, only a finding that *isolates that component* (the effect persists when everything else is stripped away, or vanishes when only that component is removed) supports it; praise of the whole product is bait.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Stem first: task verb (support/weaken) and claim-holder. Passages often carry two positions; the stem picks one.
2. Locate the claim (expect it late — unverified-claim as a rule; verify), restate plainly, compress to arrows if empirical ([[directional-evidence-prediction]]).
3. Write the target, including the mechanism qualifier: not just "X↑ → Y↑" but "*because of component C* / *under condition K*."
4. Audit choices into bins: right direction; wrong direction; no bearing (off-claim — see [[topical-vs-logical-relevance]]); right topic but wrong component/condition; right until a tail clause breaks it.
5. Read every choice to its final clause. A standard trap is the self-disqualifying tail: a mostly-qualifying choice whose last clause destroys the needed comparison or reverses its force — it punishes partial reading.
6. Check comparative structure: a claim about a *difference* needs evidence with a baseline. A striking number with no comparison point cannot support a comparative claim.
7. Anti-rationalization rule: if a choice requires you to build a story connecting it to the claim, that required effort is itself evidence the choice is wrong. Hold the target; don't meet distractors halfway.
8. Triage honestly: these items are effort-dense. If the claim won't pin down, defer and return rather than half-working it.

## Misconceptions
- **topic-match-equals-support** — the family's mask of the root error; treated fully in [[topical-vs-logical-relevance]]. Here it produces acceptance of any on-subject finding, especially generic findings about a phenomenon when the claim isolates a mechanism.
- **mechanism-agnosticism** — Students treat conditions the passage explicitly distinguishes as interchangeable (any stage of a process, any variety of a substance, any subgroup). They miss that a claim binding the effect to condition A is *contradicted*, not supported, by evidence binding it to condition B. Formed by gist-level reading that files distinctions as detail. Trap design: the condition-swap distractor — correct effect, wrong distinguished condition.
- **charitable-overreading** — Diligent students construct scenarios that make weak choices sensible, burning time and talking themselves into traps. Formed by classroom norms that reward finding merit in every interpretation. It is the misconception that most punishes *good* students.
- **partial-read-commitment** — Students commit on a choice's opening clause and skim its tail; the self-disqualifying-tail trap is built for exactly this. Formed by time pressure plus the (usually safe) habit of front-loaded reading.
- **baseline-neglect** — Students accept single-group or no-comparison findings for comparative claims because the headline number is impressive; they read the choice's claim but not its quantitative structure. Shares a root with magnitude errors in [[claim-anchored-data-reading]].

## Diagnostic indicators
- *mechanism-agnosticism*: wrong picks attach the right effect to a different passage-distinguished condition; student, asked to re-read the claim, is surprised the condition was specified.
- *charitable-overreading*: long dwell times; think-aloud contains "well, it COULD mean..."; final answer often a choice they constructed a bridge for.
- *partial-read-commitment*: wrong picks have disqualifying final clauses; fast per-choice reading times; student re-reads the tail on review and immediately self-corrects.
- *baseline-neglect*: picks the no-comparison finding for a comparative claim; explanation cites the number's size, not its structure.
- *topic-match-equals-support*: see [[topical-vs-logical-relevance]] indicators.

## Remediation pathways
- *mechanism-agnosticism*: contrast pair — same claim, two findings differing only in which condition carries the effect; make the student state why one supports and the other contradicts. Then a drill where every distractor is a condition swap. If they can't even list the passage's distinguished conditions, revisit [[argument-spine-decomposition]] (qualifier work).
- *charitable-overreading*: teach the effort heuristic explicitly as permission: "if it needs your help, it's wrong." Timed drills where rationalizing is made impossible (15-second per-choice budget) recalibrate the instinct.
- *partial-read-commitment*: tail-first drill — read each choice's final clause before its opening for one session; the traps become comically visible, which installs whole-choice reading permanently.
- *baseline-neglect*: teach the question "compared to what?" as a stamp applied to every finding offered for a comparative claim.
- *topic-match-equals-support*: route to [[topical-vs-logical-relevance]].

## Mastery criteria
The student runs claim → target → audit unprompted, states the mechanism qualifier in their target, and can name each rejected choice's bin. Proof: consistent accuracy on hard items featuring condition swaps and self-disqualifying tails, with per-choice justifications that reference the claim's specific causal story rather than its topic.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: clean directional claim; distractors mostly off-topic or wrong-direction; mechanism not at issue.
- **Medium**: mechanism-specific claims appear; condition-swap and generic-topic-support distractors; weaken variants with counterexample/null answers ([[directional-evidence-prediction]]).
- **Hard**: dense-language claims needing real decompression; two-position passages with wrong-claim-relevant distractors; self-disqualifying tails and baseline traps combined; composition with [[entailment-moves-and-boundaries]] (negations inside claims) and, in the quantitative variant, with [[claim-anchored-data-reading]].

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: don't start here — verify [[logical-text-completion]] first; this family assumes that machinery. Then teach only beat 1 and 2 (claim, target) on items with the choices hidden, exactly as with completions.
- **Average**: the mechanism idea is the unlock — most students at this band already direction-match but topic-match on mechanism claims. Teach it with the isolate-the-component frame: what result would you need if ONLY the claimed ingredient mattered?
- **Advanced**: charitable overreading and tails are the residual leaks. Give explicit permission to be uncharitable, and drill the tail-first exercise; also introduce two-claim passages to harden claim-holder discipline.
- **1500+**: full trap-taxonomy fluency — the student should classify all four choices by design intent (mirror, condition swap, generic support, broken tail, missing baseline) on review. Add the triage skill: recognizing within 20 seconds that a claim isn't pinning down and deferring the item deliberately.
