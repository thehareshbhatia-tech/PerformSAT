# Cross-text response prediction — answering "how would Author 2 respond?" before reading the choices

## What it is
The endgame protocol for paired-text questions: pin down exactly which element of the other text the stem targets, derive the response that the responding author's *stated commitments* license — nothing more — and predict it in your own words before the choices are read. The choices are then a lineup to match against, never a menu to reason from. A fixed low-working-memory pipeline: (1) T1 main point, (2) T2 main point, (3) relationship + hinge, (4) predicted response, (5) match.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The stem form "how would (the author of / a figure in) Text 2 respond to <specific element> of Text 1" is the family's workhorse (unverified-claim on dominance). It probes whether the student can *simulate a position* — reason inside another writer's commitments — rather than react with their own common sense. Dense unfamiliar texts overflow working memory, so the test also implicitly rewards students who did the argument work up front: reasoning from the choices forces a full re-read per choice, quadrupling the load, which is exactly the collapse mode the item's difficulty is built on.

## Mental model
**The author is a witness under oath; the prediction is their testimony.** The responding author may only say things their text commits them to — their claim, their evidence, their scope. Your prediction is what this witness *must* say about the targeted element: a disagreeing author responds negatively at the hinge; a both-factors author responds to a one-sided example by supplying the missing side; an author whose account centers a specific measurement responds to a rival account by pointing at what it omits. Any choice a reasonable person might say but this witness's text doesn't license is inadmissible — plausibility is not testimony.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Re-read the stem to find the target: WHICH element of the other text does the response address — a named researcher's belief, a quoted phrase, a specific example? If the stem quotes a phrase, locate it in the text first; it pins the referent and its local context.
2. Retrieve type + hinge from [[cross-text-relationship-taxonomy]]. The response happens *at the hinge*, aimed at the target.
3. Predict the response in one sentence, in your own words, before any choice is read. Force the discipline even when it feels slow — it is the load-bearing step.
4. Sweep choices by opening words: tone polarity kills wrong-direction choices cheaply ([[polarity-first-elimination]]); extreme quantifiers ("only," "never") are usually disqualifying (unverified-claim as a prior — verify in-text before relying on it).
5. For each surviving choice carrying a concrete detail: verify the detail's source text (misattribution check from [[two-voice-separation]]) and its scope against what the author actually committed to.
6. Reject choices about matters neither text addresses, however sophisticated they sound.
7. If two survive, return to the text for the narrow deciding fact. Never eliminate a merely-uncertain choice without that check — on this question type the correct answer often *looks* uncertain at first because it hinges on an overlooked detail.

## Misconceptions
- **plausible-real-world-response** — The student picks what a reasonable person would say (a common-sense critique, an obvious next question) rather than what THIS author's stated position licenses. Forms because simulating a constrained position is unnatural; one's own reaction arrives first and feels like insight. Produces scope-escape picks — choices raising adjacent issues (industry motives, alternative preferences) that neither text touches. The distractor is built to feel sophisticated.
- **choices-as-starting-point** — Overloaded students dive into the choices hoping recognition will substitute for comprehension; each choice triggers a full re-read, multiplying load and time. Forms as a coping response to dense texts. Produces slow, low-accuracy solves and vulnerability to every well-written distractor, since each is evaluated against a vague impression instead of a prediction.
- **premature-elimination-under-uncertainty** — Discarding choices the student merely feels unsure about. Usually rational elsewhere; on this type the pivotal detail is often overlooked on first read, so the credited answer is disproportionately likely to be the "unsure" one. Produces the signature of the right answer crossed out early.
- **prediction-as-own-opinion** — A subtler variant of position simulation failure: the student predicts before choices (good ritual) but the prediction contains their own judgment of who's right, not the author's licensed response. Produces predictions that match no choice, followed by a panicked fallback to choices-as-starting-point.
- **detail-trust-without-source-check** — Accepting a detail-bearing choice because the detail is familiar from the reading, skipping the which-text verification. The misattribution trap harvests this directly (full treatment in [[two-voice-separation]]).

## Diagnostic indicators
- **plausible-real-world-response**: picks scope-escape distractors; explanation begins "well, wouldn't the author think..." with no text anchor; can't point to the sentence licensing their pick.
- **choices-as-starting-point**: extreme time on paired items with multiple re-reads visible; no prediction in scratch work; accuracy collapses on paired sets specifically while single-text accuracy holds.
- **premature-elimination-under-uncertainty**: credited answer among their early eliminations; says "I wasn't sure what that one meant, so I cut it."
- **prediction-as-own-opinion**: scratch prediction expresses a verdict ("T2 is right because..."); reports "none of the choices matched my prediction" at high rates.
- **detail-trust-without-source-check**: picks misattribution choices confidently and quickly; explanation cites the detail's familiarity ("it said that in the passage").

## Remediation pathways
- **plausible-real-world-response**: the oath reframe — before predicting, ask "can I point to the sentence that licenses this response?" Drill with items whose scope-escape distractor is dissected: sophisticated, plausible, and anchored to neither text. If simulation itself is weak, prereq is [[cross-text-relationship-taxonomy]] (the type + hinge is what constrains the testimony).
- **choices-as-starting-point**: enforce the five-step pipeline mechanically — cover the choices (hand or card) until a written prediction exists. Show the time accounting: one prediction vs. four re-reads.
- **premature-elimination-under-uncertainty**: type-specific rule — on paired items, "unsure" choices get parked, not killed; a kill requires a named failed test (wrong polarity, wrong source, wrong scope). Pair with one worked example where the uncertain choice was credited.
- **prediction-as-own-opinion**: prediction template — "A2 would say: ___" in the author's voice, banning evaluative words about who is right. Read predictions aloud; opinion contamination is audible.
- **detail-trust-without-source-check**: remediate at [[two-voice-separation]]; here, add the checklist habit — every detail-bearing survivor gets a which-text tag before selection.

## Mastery criteria
On paired-text items the student produces a written or stated prediction before viewing choices, anchored to the responding author's commitments; eliminates by named tests (polarity, extremeness, source, scope) rather than feel; returns to the text to break ties; and no longer picks scope-escape or misattribution distractors — sustained across full-opposition, concession, and reinterpretation pairs at hard difficulty.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Medium (entry)**: full-opposition pair, stem targets T1's main claim, prediction is a straightforward negative response; one scope-escape distractor.
- **Hard**: concession relationships demanding yes-but predictions (hostile distractors planted for total-opposition readers); missing-measurement pivots where the response is "the rival account overlooks quantity X" and distractors mis-assign which author relies on which evidence or negate an affirmed fact; nuanced-author inversions where a both-factors author is offered their explicitly rejected one-factor reading; stems targeting a *figure cited within* a text rather than its author, adding an attribution layer; a statistic as the pivotal detail ([[claim-anchored-data-reading]] transfer). 
- Unverified-claim (structural prior, from test-construction observation): in theory-vs-evidence pairings the designed answer tends toward "appealing in principle, unsupported by the results" rather than the reverse — usable as a tiebreaker prior only, always verified in-text; gate through bank stats before the tutor asserts it.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: role-play before test items — "your friend loves this movie; the critic panned the acting; what does the critic say to your friend's favorite scene?" The witness-under-oath idea lands fast in conversation form. Then the pipeline on easy full-opposition pairs, steps written out.
- **Average**: the five-step pipeline as ritual, cover-the-choices drill, opening-word polarity sweeps, and the parked-not-killed rule.
- **Advanced**: concession-shaped predictions, missing-measurement pairs, cited-figure stems; postmortems that name which step failed (target-pinning? hinge? prediction? verification?) — the pipeline doubles as a diagnostic frame.
- **1500+**: construction-aware play: rank choices by polarity/extremeness/scope *before* reading the texts, then verify — demonstrating how much the choice set alone reveals — while stressing that priors are for ranking, never for final selection. Have them author a scope-escape distractor; the best students find this disturbingly easy, which is the lesson.
