# Second-sense vocabulary — familiar words wearing their formal meanings

## What it is
Everyday words carry formal second senses that academic prose uses constantly — the adjective sense of a word usually met as a noun, the "hedge" sense of a verb usually met as "meet requirements," and so on. The test builds items around these; the correct answer is often a word the student knows, operating in a sense they don't expect. Includes the practical detection cue: all four choices in a set share one part of speech, so a choice that *appears* to be the wrong part of speech is guaranteed to be functioning in a second sense — a signal to examine it more closely, never to eliminate it.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The exam samples the register of scholarly prose, where common words routinely operate in their formal senses. Second-sense items separate evidence-driven readers from recall-driven ones cleanly: the recall reader has strong (wrong) priors about a familiar word, while an unfamiliar word at least triggers caution. Unverified-claim: the plainest-looking words in a choice set skew correct while the most exotic skew distractor, and second-sense choices carry an elevated prior of being credited even beyond WiC items — treat both as priors to verify against bank stats, never as rules the tutor asserts.

## Mental model
**Familiarity is a disguise, not a datum.** A familiar word in a choice set is the one you know *least* about until the evidence speaks, because your prior for it is confidently wrong more often than your prior for a rare word (which is just absent). Three standing alerts: (1) the everyday sense of a word-probe target is presumed wrong until nothing else fits; (2) a "wrong part of speech" choice is a second sense in costume — inspect it; (3) the most impressive-looking word is bait as often as answer, so impressiveness is zero evidence. Morphology (prefixes, roots, cognates) is a bounded fallback for rare words: a plausibility check and tiebreaker, never the primary engine — some words wear negative-looking prefixes while being positive.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Run the standard evidence-forced pipeline first ([[evidence-forced-word-meaning]]); second-sense handling activates during the choice sweep.
2. When a choice looks like the wrong part of speech: apply the POS-uniformity fact — it must share the set's POS, so retrieve its sense *in that POS* before judging.
3. When a word-probe target is familiar: articulate the familiar sense explicitly, then check it against the evidence expecting it to fail; hunt for the sense the slot actually demands.
4. When drawn to an impressive rare word: demand the same definition-vs-evidence proof as for any choice; if its definition can't be stated, quarantine it (known-first discipline) rather than picking it for sophistication.
5. Morphology as last resort on rare survivors: decompose into parts, guess a meaning band, treat the result as weak evidence to break ties — and distrust it when a charge conflict appears.

## Misconceptions
- **pos-reflex-elimination** — The student instantly kills the choice that "isn't even the right kind of word." Forms from fast grammatical pattern-matching, usually a *good* instinct. Produces elimination of the credited second-sense answer — the wrong-POS illusion is a deliberately laid trap, and this misconception walks straight into it.
- **prestige-bias** — On hard items the student picks the most sophisticated-looking word, believing hard questions have exotic answers. Forms from the "SAT words" cultural script. The test exploits it inversely — planting obscure distractors beside plain correct answers — so choosing by impressiveness performs worse than chance on exactly the items where students trust it most.
- **prefix-literalism** — Trusting that a negative-looking prefix implies negative meaning. Forms by over-generalizing genuinely useful root/prefix instruction. Produces wrong-charge classifications of rare words during the polarity sweep, silently corrupting an otherwise sound elimination.
- **familiar-sense-anchoring** — The word-probe variant of single-meaning-belief: the student *can* recite the presumption rule but under time pressure still verifies the familiar sense first and accepts it if it merely "fits okay." Forms because the familiar sense usually does fit *grammatically*; only the evidence check rejects it. Produces familiar-sense decoy picks specifically under time pressure.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **pos-reflex-elimination**: the credited answer is among their first eliminations on second-sense items; explanation: "that's a noun/adjective, it can't go there." Fast elimination times on the choice they should have examined longest.
- **prestige-bias**: on hard items, picks the rarest word in the set at rates far above chance; accuracy on hard items *below* their medium-item baseline; explanation appeals to difficulty ("it's a hard question, so...").
- **prefix-literalism**: charge-tags rare words by their prefixes in scratch work; wrong-polarity errors only on morphologically deceptive words.
- **familiar-sense-anchoring**: timed-vs-untimed split — solves second-sense word-probe items untimed, misses them timed; picks the everyday-sense choice and cites a dictionary meaning.

## Remediation pathways
- **pos-reflex-elimination**: teach the POS-uniformity cue as a *reversal* of their instinct: "looks-wrong-POS = examine harder, not eliminate." Drill sets seeded with one costume choice each until the double-take is automatic.
- **prestige-bias**: show their own hit rate on rarest-word picks vs. plain-word picks; the data usually persuades. Reinforce with known-first discipline: an unprovable word can't be *chosen*, only reached by exhausting knowns.
- **prefix-literalism**: counterexample set — a handful of positive words in negative-looking morphology; reframe morphology as tiebreaker-grade evidence with a stated failure rate, not a rule.
- **familiar-sense-anchoring**: install the presumption rule as a timed habit: on word-probe items, the first action is writing the familiar sense and a strike-through, forcing the hunt for the demanded sense. Revisit [[evidence-forced-word-meaning]] if the student can't articulate WHY the familiar sense fails (they may be pattern-matching the remedy, too).

## Mastery criteria
The student examines rather than eliminates wrong-POS-looking choices, no longer over-picks rare words on hard items (verifiable from their pick distribution), correctly resolves word-probe items where the everyday sense fits grammatically but fails logically, and uses morphology only to break ties — all under normal time pressure.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Medium (entry)**: one second-sense choice in the set, evidence clearly rejecting the familiar sense; morphologically honest rare words.
- **Hard**: the second-sense word is the *answer* and also wears the wrong-POS costume; obscure prestige bait beside a plain correct answer; morphologically deceptive distractors corrupting the polarity sweep; period prose where even primary senses have drifted, making everything feel like a second sense. Composes with [[charge-composition-under-modifiers]] (sign arithmetic plus a costume answer) at the very top.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: mostly defer — this node presupposes the evidence-forced pipeline. One useful early piece: "words have more than one meaning, and the test loves the second one," shown with three everyday examples.
- **Average**: teach the three standing alerts as named tools; drill the POS double-take; introduce morphology WITH its counterexamples in the same lesson so the boundary is learned with the tool.
- **Advanced**: pick-distribution review (are they still prestige-biased under pressure?); second-sense hunting in real scholarly prose as a reading habit — a daily unknown-word log from serious nonfiction beats any word list.
- **1500+**: costume-answer items in period prose at full speed; have the student build a choice set containing a wrong-POS-illusion answer and a prestige decoy — authoring the trap inoculates against it.
