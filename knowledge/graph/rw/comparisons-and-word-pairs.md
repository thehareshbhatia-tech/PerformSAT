# Comparisons and word pairs: fixed templates and like-to-like balance

## What it is
Two related low-frequency families. (1) **Word pairs**: certain constructions
are fixed two-part templates whose second half is not negotiable — as…as,
not only…but also, more/-er…than, less…than, either…or, neither…nor, both…and,
between…and, from…to, so/such…that, no sooner…than. The test breaks a pair by
swapping the second element for a near-synonym (than for as, or after neither,
and after not only). (2) **Comparisons**: a comparison is valid only when both
sides are the same kind of entity — person↔person, work↔work,
attribute↔attribute. Comparing a composer's symphonies to another COMPOSER is
the classic break.

Frequency note: these are historically paper-test staples that appear only
occasionally on the digital exam [unverified-claim: low digital frequency per
source analysis as of late 2022 and our own review; verify against our bank and
newer official material]. Treat as high-scorer completeness content, not core
curriculum.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Word pairs probe whether idiomatic templates are stored as single vocabulary
items; comparisons probe logical symmetry — the sentence-level version of
parallel form, which is why [[parallel-structure]] is the prerequisite: a
comparison is a two-item coordination whose items must match in KIND as well as
form.

## Mental model
Two checks:
- **Template integrity**: on seeing the first half of a known pair, the second
  half is already determined — read the pairs as single words with a gap in the
  middle. Finger on the first marker, scan for its mandated partner.
- **Balance the scales**: put each side of a comparison on a scale pan and name
  what it IS (a person? a body of work? a measurement?). Mismatched kinds fail.
  Three legal repairs for an unbalanced side: repeat the noun with a possessive
  ("Prokofiev's"), restate it in an of-phrase ("the novels of…"), or use a
  stand-in — "that of" for a singular noun, "those of" for a plural. The
  stand-in must agree in number with the noun it replaces: "that of" pointing at
  a plural is itself a planted error. A fourth balancing device from our own
  taxonomy: the pro-verb ("runs faster than her brother DOES"), which turns a
  person-vs-attribute mismatch into clause-vs-clause.
- Quantity-word corollary: **fewer/many** pair with countable plurals,
  **less/much** with uncountable singulars. Decision procedure: find the noun
  being quantified and ask "can I count individual units of this?" Near-identical
  noun phrases flip the answer (many species / less biodiversity) — exactly the
  contrast the test builds.

## Completeness note: infinitive vs gerund after a verb
A neighboring idiom family, noted here for completeness only. Whether a given verb
takes an infinitive complement ("refused to participate"), a bare gerund ("avoided
participating"), or a gerund forced through a preposition ("insisted ON
participating") is pure idiom — there is no rule to derive, and the gerund
alternative usually drags a preposition with it, which is the surface cue that a
form has been swapped. The exam does not pit two idiomatic forms against each other
in one choice set [unverified-claim: per our review of released digital material;
verify against bank], so memorizing verb lists is negative-ROI. The whole play is:
recognize that the choices differ in complement form, trust the reading ear on the
one construction in front of you, and move on — minutes here are minutes taken from
rule-governed families that actually recur.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Triage ([[choice-set-diagnosis]]): choices varying in than/as/nor/and or in
   the second half of a recognizable template → word-pair item; choices varying
   in that-of/those-of/possessives around a comparison → faulty-comparison item.
2. Word pairs: locate the FIXED half in the passage (it's usually outside the
   blank), recall its mandated partner, eliminate everything else. Do not
   evaluate the near-synonym swaps for meaning — the template, not sense,
   decides.
3. Comparisons: name the kind of thing on the fixed side; demand the same kind
   on the choice side. Check the head noun, not the presence of the right name
   (a bare "Prokofiev" ≠ "Prokofiev's" — same head-noun discipline as the
   possessive trap in [[modifier-adjacency]]).
4. If the choice uses that-of/those-of, verify number against the replaced noun
   ([[grammatical-number-assignment]]).
5. Fewer/less: find the quantified noun, run the countability question, ignore
   register instincts.
6. Remember: if neither…nor governs a verb, the nearer noun sets its number
   (handled in [[grammatical-number-assignment]] — one decision, two nodes).

## Misconceptions
- **mix-and-match-pairs** — students treat correlative templates as
  compositional grammar ("not only…and," "neither…or") because each half feels
  independently fine. Forms because the halves ARE ordinary words elsewhere;
  only the template welds them. Produces near-synonym-swap picks — the exact
  distractor build.
- **name-presence-balance** — a comparison feels balanced because the right
  person/thing is MENTIONED on both sides, regardless of grammatical kind
  ("her sculptures … than Rodin"). Forms from meaning-first reading; the
  intended comparison is obvious, so the missing possessive is invisible. Same
  root as meaning-rescues-syntax in [[modifier-adjacency]].
- **that-of-is-fancier-so-safer** — students sprinkle "that of/those of" as a
  formality upgrade without checking necessity or number. Forms because the
  phrase appears in credited answers often enough to feel like a magic token.
  Produces number-mismatched stand-ins and stand-ins where the plain noun was
  fine.
- **less-fewer-style-choice** — both quantity words are believed acceptable
  everywhere. Forms because everyday usage (supermarket signage, speech) uses
  "less" for everything; students never learn the rule is deterministic.
- **attribute-object-flatten** — both sides are "things," so the comparison
  passes, even when one side is an object and the other a MEASUREMENT of an
  object (a bridge vs the length of a bridge). Forms because kind-checking
  stops at the person/thing level; the fix is lifting both sides to the same
  level of description.

## Diagnostic indicators
- Picks "not only…and" or "neither…or" completions → **mix-and-match-pairs**;
  test knowledge directly by asking them to complete the template cold — if
  they can't, it's vocabulary, not reasoning.
- Picks the bare-person choice in a works-comparison → **name-presence-balance**
  (ask "what two things is this sentence weighing?" — a meaning-level answer
  with no grammatical kinds confirms it).
- Picks "that of" pointing at a plural, or inserts a stand-in where the fixed
  side is already balanced → **that-of-is-fancier-so-safer**.
- Fewer/less picks tracking formality rather than countability →
  **less-fewer-style-choice**.
- Accepts object-vs-measurement comparisons but rejects person-vs-work ones →
  **attribute-object-flatten** (the coarse check works; the fine one doesn't).

## Remediation pathways
- **mix-and-match-pairs**: teach the pairs as vocabulary — a short fixed list
  memorized as welded units, then cold completion drills ("not only ___").
  There is no deeper rule to derive; honesty about that prevents rule-hunting.
- **name-presence-balance**: the scale-pans drill — for each side, name the
  kind aloud ("pan 1: sculptures; pan 2: a man") before judging. Then teach the
  three repairs + pro-verb as the repair menu.
- **that-of-is-fancier-so-safer**: two-question gate before accepting any
  stand-in: does the fixed side actually need replacing, and does the stand-in's
  number match? Show one item where the plain possessive beats the stand-in.
- **less-fewer-style-choice**: countability probe ("can I have three of it?")
  on minimal pairs; flag that the tested standard is deterministic even though
  everyday usage isn't.
- **attribute-object-flatten**: contrast pair with the measurement made
  explicit on both sides; teach "lift both sides to the same level."

## Mastery criteria
Completes the template list cold; names both scale pans before judging any
comparison; checks stand-in number automatically; and — the precision side —
leaves already-balanced comparisons alone. Proof: correct on a mixed set
containing one broken pair, one faulty comparison, one number-mismatched
stand-in, and one correct-as-given sentence.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: rarely tested easy; a broken high-frequency pair (either…or) with
  the fixed half adjacent to the blank.
- **medium**: like-to-like violations with the possessive repair; fewer/less
  with clearly countable/uncountable nouns.
- **hard**: that-of/those-of number agreement; attribute-vs-object mismatches;
  templates whose first half sits far from the blank (forcing the full-sentence
  read learned in [[parallel-structure]]); countability items where
  near-identical noun phrases flip the answer.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: skip this node until core conventions are solid — it's
  low-frequency content. If encountered, teach only either/or, neither/nor,
  not only/but also as memorized wholes.
- **Average**: template list + the scale-pans model; the three repairs
  presented as "ways to put the same KIND on both sides."
- **Advanced**: stand-in number agreement and the necessity gate; fewer/less
  probes; distant-anchor templates.
- **1500+**: this is precisely the "just in case" band this content exists
  for — cover attribute-vs-object lifts, pro-verb balancing, and the
  discipline of not over-repairing balanced comparisons. Frame frequency
  honestly: minutes invested here should come after every core node is at
  ceiling.
