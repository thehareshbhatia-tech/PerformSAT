# Sentence vs fragment — completeness is structural, never semantic

## What it is
The judgment of whether a word string is a complete sentence. A complete sentence
needs exactly two things: a subject and a working (conjugated) verb that matches
it. Everything else — length, detail, whether it "makes sense" alone — is
irrelevant. *It grows.* is a sentence; a forty-word string rich with names and
dates can be a fragment.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Every boundary mark is a claim about sentence-hood: a period asserts "a complete
sentence just ended." So the College Board tests whether students can verify that
claim structurally. The exam's leverage comes from the gap between structure and
feel: it builds complete clauses that FEEL incomplete (pronoun subjects whose
referent lives in the previous sentence) and fragments that FEEL complete (long,
informative, no working verb). Boundary items always sit inside short passages,
where surrounding information distracts from the structural judgment — the skill
must survive context noise. (unverified-claim as to how uniformly.)

## Mental model
**Two-slot check.** A sentence is a machine with two required slots: a subject
slot and a working-verb slot. To judge completeness, check the slots and ONLY the
slots — deliberately suppress the question "does this make sense by itself?"
That question is the enemy: written completeness is a property of structure, not
of information. The generative why: a sentence is the unit of assertion, and an
assertion needs a claimer-about (subject) and a claim-carrier (a verb bearing
time). Context can supply meaning; it can never supply a missing verb.

Slot-check refinements (each earns its place by blocking a trap):
- **Pronoun subjects fill the slot fully.** *They migrate in spring.* is complete
  even when you don't know who "they" are.
- **Quantity words fork.** Bare quantity subject → complete (*Most survive the
  winter.*). Quantity + *of which/of whom* → a relative clause that can never
  stand alone (*most of which survive...*). So *...the winter, most of them
  survive* is a splice; *...the winter. Most of them survive* and *...the winter,
  most of which survive* are both legal — a three-way contrast worth drilling.
- **-ing and to-forms never fill the verb slot.** Subject + -ing with no working
  verb = fragment (*The lakes rising every year* — no sentence). Choices built
  on *being* are usually fragment-makers (unverified-claim for the current exam;
  verify against bank before the tutor asserts it as a shortcut).
- **A relative word steals the verb after it.** In *the city, which houses two
  universities*, the verb *houses* belongs to *which*, not to *the city* — the
  outer subject is still verb-less.
- **Openers predict fragments.** A string opening with a preposition, an -ing
  form, a relative word (which/who/where), or a subordinator (because/although/
  while...) is a fragment until an independent clause shows up. (One rare
  exception: a which/who-opened string IS a sentence when the w-word itself is
  the subject of the main verb — the slot-check, not the opener, has the final
  word.) Subordinator-first
  sentences are legal ONLY when a comma + independent clause follows — the
  schoolroom "never start with because" rule is half-true and worth debunking
  explicitly.
- **A subordinate clause needs its own working verb too** if it has its own
  subject (*although the data suggesting...* is still broken). Exception: after
  time subordinators, a subject-less -ing is fine (*After moving twice, she...*).

## Expert reasoning process
1. Find every working verb in the string (verb-first parse from
   [[boundaries-word-roles]]).
2. Attach each verb to its subject; trace ownership when a relative word is
   present — who does this verb really belong to?
3. Anything left over (preposition chunks, -ing phrases, appositives, adverbs)
   is decoration; mentally gray it out.
4. Verdict: at least one subject + working-verb pair standing free of any
   subordinator or relative word → sentence. Otherwise → fragment.
5. Throughout: refuse the "does it make sense alone?" question. Structure only.

## Misconceptions
- **sentence-equals-complete-thought** — Elementary school defines a sentence as
  "a complete thought," so students judge by semantic self-sufficiency. Produces
  BOTH error directions: rejecting real sentences with pronoun or quantity-word
  subjects ("It doesn't say what 'they' is") and accepting long informative
  fragments. The test's favorite version: the clause after the blank is complete
  but context-dependent, so the student rejects the period option.
- **length-implies-completeness** — Long, detail-rich strings feel sentence-like.
  The exam builds elaborate fragments to exploit it: proper noun + relative
  clause + participial phrase, with the choices quietly toggling whether a
  working main verb ever appears.
- **ing-form-counts-as-verb** — -ing words look maximally verby; students accept
  *the committee having approved the plan* as complete. Answer choices swap a
  working verb for an -ing form to harvest exactly this.
- **never-start-with-because** — An over-taught childhood rule; students reject
  correct subordinator-first sentences and, worse, can't say when the pattern IS
  legal (comma + independent clause must follow).
- **relative-clause-can-finish-a-sentence** — The student hears a verb near the
  end (*...which spans nine time zones*) and counts it as the main verb, missing
  that the relative word owns it. Root: verbs are counted, not traced to owners.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **sentence-equals-complete-thought:** rejects the period/full-stop choice when
  clause 2 opens with a pronoun or quantity word; explanation includes "you can't
  start a sentence like that" or "it doesn't tell you what it refers to."
- **length-implies-completeness:** accepts the no-main-verb choice specifically
  on LONG stems; error rate rises with stem length; fast, confident wrong answers.
- **ing-form-counts-as-verb:** picks choices whose only candidate verb is an
  -ing/being form; when asked "what's the verb?", points at the -ing word.
- **never-start-with-because:** eliminates subordinator-first choices on sight,
  even when a comma + independent clause follows.
- **relative-clause-can-finish-a-sentence:** calls a fragment "fine" and cites
  the relative clause's verb as the main verb when probed.
Distinguish all of these from menu errors: here the student mis-COUNTS what's a
sentence; in [[three-legal-joins]] failures they count right but punctuate wrong.

## Remediation pathways
- **sentence-equals-complete-thought:** the two-word counterexample pair — *It
  grows.* (sentence) vs a 25-word verb-less fragment. Have the student rank both
  by "feels complete," then by slot-check, and confront the reversal. If it
  doesn't land, rebuild verb-finding in [[boundaries-word-roles]].
- **length-implies-completeness:** strip-to-skeleton drill: cross out every
  preposition chunk, -ing phrase, and relative clause; ask what's left. Fragments
  strip to nothing.
- **ing-form-counts-as-verb:** the time-stamp test — a working verb can change
  tense (*grows → grew*); an -ing form can't carry the sentence's time by itself.
  One-word repair drills (add or conjugate exactly one word) force the diagnosis.
- **never-start-with-because:** show the legal template *Subordinator clause,
  independent clause.* next to the illegal lone version; the rule isn't "never,"
  it's "not alone."
- **relative-clause-can-finish-a-sentence:** verb-ownership tracing: draw an
  arrow from each verb to its owner; the outer subject's arrow has nowhere to go.

## Mastery criteria
The student can classify 15 exam-register strings as sentence/fragment in under
90 seconds with ≥90% accuracy, INCLUDING pronoun-subject sentences and long
no-main-verb fragments; and can repair a fragment by changing exactly one word.
Proof: consistent correctness on boundary items where the trap is a
feels-wrong-but-complete clause 2, at medium+ difficulty.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy:** short strings, missing verb obvious; subject and verb adjacent.
- **Medium:** pronoun/quantity subjects (complete but feels incomplete); -ing
  swapped for the main verb; subordinator-first legal sentences.
- **Hard:** elaborate fragments — name + non-essential insert + participial
  tail, verb ownership hidden behind a relative word — composed with
  [[delete-and-reread]] (strip the insert to expose the missing verb) and
  [[window-widening]] (the decisive material sits far from the blank).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling:** one evolving example. Start from *Otters play.* and add one
  layer at a time (preposition chunk, adverb, insert, participial phrase),
  showing the verdict never changes; then delete the verb and show everything
  collapse. One variable per step.
- **Average:** trap-first — give the 15-item speed drill BEFORE instruction, let
  the "complete thought" instinct fail publicly, then teach the slot-check as
  the explanation of their own errors.
- **Advanced:** teach fragment triage by opener (preposition/-ing/relative/
  subordinator → suspect until an independent clause appears) as a speed layer
  on top of the slot-check.
- **1500+:** drill only the two residual leaks — verb-ownership under stacked
  relatives, and the quantity-word fork (them vs which/whom) — inside full
  passages with context noise.
