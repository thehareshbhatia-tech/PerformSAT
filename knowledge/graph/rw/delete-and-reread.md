# Delete and re-read — the interrupter test and its failure modes (HUB)

## What it is
The master move for every non-essential-element question: hypothesize the span
of the removable chunk, mentally excise it, and re-read what remains. A clean,
complete leftover sentence → the span is genuinely non-essential and needs a
full pair of enclosing marks. A garbled leftover → wrong span, or the material
is essential. Everything in the interrupter half of Boundaries — comma pairs,
dash pairs, parentheses, appositives, names, titles, transition words — is
this one test plus one extra discriminator per case. Crucially, this node also
teaches WHERE THE TEST FAILS, because the exam writes items against the naive
version of it.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The generative why: a bracketed span is material the host assertion doesn't
depend on — the brackets are a promise to the reader that the sentence's spine
runs straight through underneath. Deletion is simply checking whether the
promise is true. The SAT leans on this territory heavily (it is among the
highest-yield punctuation ground on the digital exam) because it composes: one
interrupter can hide a fragment, camouflage a splice, or bury a connector, so
a single test discriminates students at every level. Any part of the bracket
can be the tested edge — items often underline only the opening or only the
closing of a long interrupter, forcing whole-sentence reconstruction
(unverified-claim as to frequency).

## Mental model
**Brackets promise a straight spine.** The test verifies the promise:
1. **Hypothesize the span** — where does the removable chunk plausibly start
   and end?
2. **Delete it; re-read the remainder** — demand a complete, sensible sentence
   ([[sentence-vs-fragment]] is what "complete" means here).
3. **Iterate** — a garbled leftover often means a mis-drawn span, not
   essential material; move an endpoint and retry. Iterating is expected, not
   a sign of confusion.
And the two documented failure modes, taught as part of the tool:
- **Wrong-span mirage:** two commas in a sentence do NOT automatically fence a
  removable span — commas also end intro phrases, separate list items, and
  precede coordinators. Deleting between two unrelated commas yields nonsense
  or, worse, a plausible-but-wrong reading. The deletion test VALIDATES a
  hypothesis about a span; it never picks the span for you.
- **Bracketed-transition blind spot:** delete a comma-fenced *however* sitting
  between two independent clauses and the leftover reads fine — yet the
  original was a splice ([[comma-splice]]). The leftover being clean is
  necessary, not sufficient. Antidote: after deleting, also verify the
  original marks could LEGALLY bracket that span in that position (a comma
  pair cannot bridge two sentences).

## Expert reasoning process
1. **Scan left for an open bracket first.** Before judging the blank, look
   earlier in the sentence for an unpaired comma, dash, or open parenthesis.
   One found → the blank almost certainly closes it with the SAME mark
   ([[pair-completeness]]) — often instantly fatal to three choices.
2. Hypothesize the span; typical openers: relative words (which/who), noun
   restatements (appositives), -ing phrases, transition words.
3. Delete; re-read; demand completeness AND sense. Iterate endpoints if
   garbled.
4. **Verb-after-close check:** whatever follows the closing bracket must
   continue the host clause correctly — usually the main verb. A choice
   following the close with an -ing form or a fresh pronoun subject quietly
   manufactures a fragment or a splice once the bracket is stripped.
5. Run the sufficiency check from the failure modes: could these marks legally
   fence this span here? Is the leftover clean because the span was extra —
   or because deletion hid a splice?
6. For the digital format: train with literal cross-outs on paper early, then
   wean to purely visual span-masking — physical strike-through isn't
   practical on screen.

## Misconceptions
- **two-commas-equals-removable** — Over-generalizing the bracket schema to
  ANY comma pair. Root: the deletion test is usually taught without its
  failure cases. The exam salts sentences with an intro-phrase comma plus a
  list comma and lets students "delete" between them.
- **deletion-verdict-is-final** — Believing a clean leftover certifies the
  original. Root: the test taught as an oracle instead of a one-way check.
  Harvested by the bracketed-transition splice, the exam's most elegant trap
  against well-taught students.
- **garbled-means-essential** — Concluding "essential, no commas" the first
  time a deletion reads badly, when the real problem was a mis-drawn span.
  Root: nobody said iterating is normal. Produces missing-bracket answers on
  long appositives whose true span started earlier than the student guessed.
- **delete-only-what-is-underlined** — Anchoring the span to the underline
  instead of to the sentence's structure; the tested edge may be only one end
  of a span that opened twenty words back. Cousin of local-window judgment
  ([[window-widening]]).

## Diagnostic indicators
- **two-commas-equals-removable:** on wrong-span items, the student reports
  the sentence "works without it" while quoting a deletion across unrelated
  commas; their span endpoints don't align with any constituent.
- **deletion-verdict-is-final:** accepts comma-pair transitions between
  independent clauses; when probed, cites the clean deletion as proof —
  the signature of a student taught the naive test.
- **garbled-means-essential:** answers "no commas" on true interrupters whose
  span they mis-drew; when asked to re-try with shifted endpoints, they get
  it — the knowledge is there, the iteration habit isn't.
- **delete-only-what-is-underlined:** misses cluster on items underlining one
  bracket edge of a long interrupter; their deletions start exactly at the
  underline.

## Remediation pathways
- **two-commas-equals-removable:** teach the comma-jobs inventory
  ([[essential-vs-nonessential]]): before bracketing, name each comma's job; only
  a matched PAIR of doing-nothing-else commas can be a bracket. Drill on
  salted sentences.
- **deletion-verdict-is-final:** show the trap directly — one clean deletion
  that hides a splice — then install the second question: "could a comma pair
  legally sit between two sentences? Never." Route through [[comma-splice]]
  if the splice recognition itself is weak.
- **garbled-means-essential:** endpoint-iteration drills: give a garbled first
  deletion on purpose, require two more span attempts before any verdict.
- **delete-only-what-is-underlined:** whole-sentence reconstruction reps —
  cover the choices, find the full span from the sentence alone, THEN look
  ([[window-widening]] shares this remediation).

## Mastery criteria
The student runs delete-and-reread unprompted on interrupter items, iterates
spans without stalling, and — the real bar — catches both failure modes: they
reject the bracketed-transition splice AND refuse to bracket unrelated comma
pairs. Proof: consistent accuracy on hard items where the naive deletion test
endorses the wrong answer.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy:** short which-clause or appositive mid-sentence, both edges visible,
  clean deletion.
- **Medium:** longer spans; the verb-after-close check needed; dash and
  parenthesis pairs; one bracket edge underlined.
- **Hard:** the failure-mode items — wrong-span decoys and bracketed-
  transition splices; interrupters stacked so one deletion exposes a second
  problem (a missing main verb, a double connector); composition with
  [[essential-vs-nonessential]] meaning stakes and [[transition-backward-anchoring]]
  three-sentence logic.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling:** physical first — pencil cross-outs, big spans, obvious
  appositives. One skill: delete, read aloud, verdict. No failure modes yet;
  build trust in the tool before complicating it.
- **Average:** add span iteration and the left-scan for open brackets; then
  introduce ONE failure mode (wrong-span) with salted sentences.
- **Advanced:** teach both failure modes explicitly and the sufficiency
  question ("could these marks legally fence this span?"). This is the
  difference between owning the tool and being owned by it.
- **1500+:** adversarial reps where the deletion test alone endorses a wrong
  answer and only the legality cross-check saves them; plus digital-format
  weaning — full visual masking at speed, no finger-tracking.
