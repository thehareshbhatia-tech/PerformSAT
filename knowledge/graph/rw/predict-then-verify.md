# Predict, then verify: answer in your own words before the choices can talk

## What it is
The core answering discipline: formulate a rough answer — a few words, possibly jotted — BEFORE reading the choices, then look for the choice that matches your prediction. Follow with disciplined elimination (kill only on identified wrongness) and, when down to two, an ACTIVE verification pass back in the text — checking a specific concrete claim of one finalist, not staring at the choices until one "feels right."

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Distractors are engineered to sound plausible to a mind with no prior commitment; a student who reads choices cold lets the test frame the possibilities. Prediction inverts the power dynamic — choices are graded against your standard instead of seducing you. The final-two verification exists because the hardest distractors survive gist filtering by design; only a concrete, falsifiable check discriminates. unverified-claim: most final-two standoffs pit a half-right hybrid against a plausible-but-unsupported choice, and the winner is decided by checking one concrete detail — validate against our bank's response data.

## Mental model
**Order the meal before the waiter recites the specials.** Three phases with different logic:
1. **Predict**: cheap, rough, in your own words. Not a polished answer — a direction ("something like: the new method found errors the old one missed").
2. **Eliminate**: kill fast on clear wrongness ONLY (named species — see [[distractor-species-taxonomy]]). Never eliminate a choice because you don't understand it; confusion is not wrongness, and abstract diction on correct answers is a deliberate test-writer move.
3. **Verify**: on the final two, return to the text and check one specific, concrete claim of one choice. This is an act, not a mood — you are trying to falsify.
Prediction intensity scales with question type: skippable on easy literal lookups; highest value on completion, support/weaken, and paired-text items where distractor plausibility is engineered hardest.

## Expert reasoning process
1. After the targeted read, state your own answer in ≤10 words (jot it if the item is hard — [[shorthand-note-discipline]]).
2. Scan choices for a meaning-match to the prediction (expect paraphrase, one abstraction rung up — [[abstraction-ladder-paraphrase]]).
3. If one choice matches, verify it forward (every-word audit) and commit. Don't shop the other choices for something prettier.
4. If nothing matches, distrust the prediction before distrusting the test: re-read the stem, widen the text window, revise. "Not what I expected" ≠ wrong — the credited answer may come from an unexpected direction.
5. If two survive: identify what each claims that the other doesn't, and check THAT differential claim in the text.
6. If still unresolved after honest work: flag, pick a consistent fallback, move on ([[writing-module-economics]]). Return only if time remains.

## Misconceptions
- **choices-first-shopping** — Reading the choices as a menu of possibilities and picking the most attractive. Formed because it feels faster and because prediction feels like extra work. Produces trap picks at exactly the engineered plausibility points; the student's accuracy tracks distractor quality, not their own skill.
- **confusion-elimination** — Cutting choices they don't understand. Comfort heuristic; kills abstractly-worded correct answers (see [[supported-not-stated]]).
- **stare-down-tiebreak** — Resolving final-two by re-reading the two choices repeatedly, waiting for a feeling. Formed because returning to the passage feels like admitting failure or wasting time. Produces coin-flip outcomes on the hardest items — and reliably picking the WRONG one, since the surviving trap was built to out-feel the answer.
- **fifty-fifty-is-bad-luck** — Believing chronic final-two losses are tie-breaking bad luck. The deep diagnosis: habitually reaching two finalists and losing usually means the passage's POINT was never grasped — a comprehension deficit upstream, not an elimination-technique deficit. Remediation lives in vocabulary, complex-syntax exposure, and point-reading ([[topic-point-attitude-reading]]), not in more tricks.
- **prediction-perfectionism** — Refusing to look at choices until the prediction is polished. Formed by over-applying the rule. Costs time; the prediction is a compass, not a thesis.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **choices-first-shopping**: telemetry shows choice-area engagement almost immediately after passage read; miss species vary with distractor design rather than clustering.
- **confusion-elimination**: eliminated-first choices are disproportionately the credited abstract ones; student explanations include "that one made no sense."
- **stare-down-tiebreak**: long final-two dwell with no passage re-visit (no scroll/re-highlight events); near-50% conversion on flagged final-two items.
- **fifty-fifty-is-bad-luck**: frequent final-two situations ACROSS skills with sub-60% conversion; drill accuracy fine on easy items; the pattern spans question families (that cross-family spread is the signature).
- **prediction-perfectionism**: very long pre-choice dwell on items ultimately answered correctly; pacing pressure at module end.

## Remediation pathways
- choices-first-shopping: cover-the-choices drill — choices physically hidden until a prediction is spoken/typed; compare accuracy with and without to make the payoff visible.
- confusion-elimination: asymmetric-rule teaching plus plain-language translation of abstract correct answers ([[abstraction-ladder-paraphrase]]).
- stare-down-tiebreak: differential-claim ritual — before choosing between finalists, the student must state "A says X that B doesn't; the text says ___." No statement, no answer.
- fifty-fifty-is-bad-luck: route upstream — point-reading drills ([[topic-point-attitude-reading]]), academic vocabulary ([[second-sense-vocabulary]]), complex-syntax exposure. Do NOT respond with more elimination technique; that treats the symptom.
- prediction-perfectionism: 10-word cap and a 15-second prediction budget; prediction quality is graded on direction, not completeness.

## Mastery criteria
Student predicts before choices on hard items by habit (observable in think-alouds or jot logs), converts final-two situations at well above chance with a stated differential check, and treats "no match" as a signal to revise rather than to force. Proof: final-two conversion rate and per-item process evidence, not raw accuracy alone.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: prediction matches a choice nearly verbatim; no real finalists.
- **Medium**: match requires one abstraction rung; one engineered finalist survives to verification.
- **Hard**: prediction is directionally right but the credited answer approaches from an unexpected angle (forcing step 4's revision loop); finalists are hybrid-vs-unsupported requiring a precise differential check; composes with [[belief-revision-template]] (the prediction must carry old-vs-new polarity) and [[reference-compression-tracking]] (verification requires unpacking a compressed referent).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach predict as "guess the answer in your head first" — no jotting requirement yet, no verification protocol. Win: they experience recognizing their guess among the choices, which is intrinsically motivating.
- **Average**: full three-phase loop with the asymmetric elimination rule; introduce jotting on the three high-value types (completion, evidence, paired).
- **Advanced**: final-two forensics — differential-claim ritual, species-naming of the surviving trap, and the revision loop for no-match situations.
- **1500+**: calibration and honesty — knowing which items genuinely warrant the full loop vs. a fast literal read; and treating any 50/50 feeling as diagnostic data about comprehension to log and fix, not a tactical problem to out-trick. Post-mortems ask "what did I misread or overextend," never "why did my answer deserve credit."
