# Polarity-first elimination — spend the cheapest reliable feature before the expensive ones

## What it is
A work-ordering discipline: when the exact required answer is uncertain, extract the cheapest feature you can still compute reliably — its charge (positive / negative / neutral) — and use it to eliminate wrong-charge choices before doing any fine-grained meaning comparison. A second cheap pass screens *degree*: choices that overstate certainty or intensity (proves, demolishes, only, never) die before precision work begins.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Not a question type but the expert's universal cost-management tool. It resolves words-in-context items where the student can't predict the exact word, purpose items where four rhetorical verbs must be compared, and cross-text response items where choices open with tone-revealing verbs. The test implicitly rewards it: wrong answers are routinely planted on the wrong side of the polarity line or at the wrong intensity, so a student who screens polarity first converts many 4-way decisions into 2-way decisions at near-zero cost.

## Mental model
**Cheap-and-certain before expensive-and-uncertain.** Features of an answer have prices: charge is almost free, degree is cheap, exact meaning is expensive. An expert never pays for precision on a choice that already failed a cheap test. Physically mark the required charge (+ / − / 0) before sweeping choices; sweep strictly in order; kill wrong-charge and over-degree choices; only survivors get full-definition scrutiny.

## Expert reasoning process
1. From the evidence (connector family, tone words, relationship between texts), compute the required answer's charge. Write it down — offloading it protects working memory.
2. If a modifier sits between evidence and answer slot (less, hardly, fails to), hand off to [[charge-composition-under-modifiers]] before fixing the charge.
3. Sweep choices A→D in order. Eliminate every choice with the wrong or clearly neutral charge. Do not linger; do not evaluate meaning yet.
4. Second cheap pass: eliminate choices whose *degree* exceeds what measured academic prose does — verbs of proof/ridicule/demolition, quantifiers like only/never/all — unless the passage explicitly earns that strength.
5. Spend precision only on survivors: full definition vs. evidence, word-by-word verification.
6. Calibration rule: when unsure whether a choice passes a cheap test, **retain it**. Cheap tests are for confident kills only; forced kills are how right answers die early.

## Misconceptions
- **precision-first-grinding** — The student compares full meanings of all four choices immediately, overloading working memory and running out of time. Forms because school never teaches feature ordering; "read all the options carefully" is the only strategy ever given. Produces slow solves, second-guessing, and abandonment on hard items.
- **polarity-as-verdict** — The student picks the first right-charge choice without the precision pass. Forms from over-trusting a newly learned tool. Produces intensity-mismatch and near-synonym errors: right direction, wrong meaning — exactly the distractors the test builds for this.
- **neutral-slot-forcing** — The student assigns a charge to a slot that is genuinely neutral (descriptive, technical), then eliminates the correct neutral choice. Forms because the tool works so often it gets applied unconditionally. The fallback for neutral slots is substitution testing, not charge.
- **charge-of-topic-not-slot** — The student tags the passage's *subject* as negative (disease, extinction) and demands a negative answer, when the author's stance — the thing that matters — is neutral or admiring. This is a [[tone-attitude-reading]] failure surfacing here.
- **uncertain-kill** — Eliminating a choice merely because it *feels* unsure rather than because it failed a test. Produces the signature pattern of the right answer being crossed out early.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **precision-first-grinding**: long time-per-item across all difficulties; scratch work shows definitions of all four choices; accuracy fine untimed, poor timed.
- **polarity-as-verdict**: picks intensity-mismatch distractors (mild word where evidence demands strong, or vice versa); explanation stops at "it's negative, and the answer needed to be negative."
- **neutral-slot-forcing**: wrong on technical/descriptive slots specifically; explanation includes a charge assignment the passage doesn't support.
- **charge-of-topic-not-slot**: wrong-polarity picks concentrated in passages with grim subject matter but neutral authorial stance.
- **uncertain-kill**: the credited answer appears among the student's first eliminations; says "I didn't know that word / wasn't sure, so I crossed it out."

## Remediation pathways
- **precision-first-grinding**: impose the two-pass protocol explicitly — first pass may ONLY mark +/−/0 per choice, no meaning allowed. Time both passes to show the speed gain.
- **polarity-as-verdict**: contrast pair of two same-charge choices differing only in intensity; show the evidence fixing degree, not just direction.
- **neutral-slot-forcing**: teach the escape hatch — if charge cannot be confidently assigned in ~5 seconds, skip to substitution testing. Neutral is a legitimate output.
- **charge-of-topic-not-slot**: revisit [[tone-attitude-reading]]; drill separating "what is discussed" from "how the author regards it."
- **uncertain-kill**: rule: a kill requires naming the failed test aloud. "Unsure" is a park, not a kill — quarantine unknowns and prove the knowns first.

## Mastery criteria
On items where the student cannot pre-predict the exact answer, they reliably (a) commit a written charge before reading choices, (b) eliminate 2–3 choices on cheap features, (c) still run the precision pass on survivors, and (d) never eliminate solely on unfamiliarity — evidenced by intensity-mismatch and wrong-polarity distractors no longer appearing among their picks on medium/hard items.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: slot charge is obvious; one choice per polarity; charge alone nearly decides it.
- **Medium**: two same-charge survivors force a real precision pass; one negation between evidence and slot; degree screening needed against one extreme choice.
- **Hard**: stacked modifiers make the required charge itself a computation ([[charge-composition-under-modifiers]]); genuinely neutral slots punish charge-forcing; all four choices share a polarity so the tool must be recognized as inapplicable. Composes with [[second-sense-vocabulary]] when a familiar-looking survivor is operating in a second sense.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: this is the highest-leverage tool to teach first — it produces visible progress without vocabulary growth. Teach +/− tagging with physical marks; celebrate 4→2 reductions even when the final pick is wrong.
- **Average**: add degree screening and the retain-when-unsure calibration rule; drill the two-pass ritual until pass one contains zero meaning-talk.
- **Advanced**: focus on the failure modes — neutral slots, all-same-polarity sets — so the tool's *boundaries* are as automatic as the tool.
- **1500+**: frame as feature economics: which feature is cheapest *for this item*? Sometimes structure ([[connector-logic-families]]) is cheaper than charge. Have them narrate feature-ordering decisions on hard items.
