# The emphasis map: authors mark their own treasure

## What it is
Writers flag what they consider important with a small, learnable inventory of emphasis machinery — and the tested idea usually sits next to one of these flags. The inventory: contrast and consequence transitions; importance vocabulary ("crucial," "notably," and their synonyms); intensifying adverbs ("especially"-type words); explicit causal and explanatory verbs; colons and dashes (whose payload explains or delivers the preceding claim); italics; claim-of-purpose language ("the goal is," "what this shows"); and position itself — first sentence, final sentence, sentence directly before a colon. Reading with the map means knowing where value concentrates before you look.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Question writers need short passages to support non-trivial questions, so they build items around the passage's load-bearing sentences — which are exactly the ones the author marked. unverified-claim: tested content sits disproportionately adjacent to emphasis machinery, and key claims are disproportionately signaled by dashes, colons, italics, and strong evaluative vocabulary — a strong regularity worth quantifying against our bank before the tutor asserts it numerically. The deeper ability probed: sensitivity to how prose signals its own hierarchy, which is most of what "reading well" means under time pressure.

## Mental model
**The author has already highlighted the passage for you.** An expert's eye is trained to snag on the marks: a colon promises "here's the payoff"; a dash pair cradles a definition or a punchline; "especially" says "the exam question lives near me"; a pivot word says "the point is about to arrive" ([[belief-revision-template]]); the final sentence of a short passage is a privileged position. The map also works in negative: long unmarked stretches of neutral description are usually scenery — safe to read fast, unlikely to be tested alone. Importance-ranking replaces uniform-effort reading.

## Expert reasoning process
1. During the targeted read, let the marks set reading speed: slow at flags, fast through unmarked description.
2. When a global question needs the point: check sentence one, the final sentence, then any colon/dash payloads and importance vocabulary — in that order (unverified-claim on the ordering's hit-rate; derive from bank data).
3. When a local question's span seems answerless: look for the nearest emphasis mark outside the span — the deciding material is often its neighbor.
4. Treat quotation-marked skepticism (scare quotes) and evaluative adjectives as attitude flags feeding [[topic-point-attitude-reading]].
5. In choices, expect the credited answer to restate a FLAGGED sentence, not an unflagged one ([[abstraction-ladder-paraphrase]]).

## Misconceptions
- **uniform-effort-reading** — Giving every sentence equal attention because "careful means thorough." Formed by close-reading schooling and fear of missing something. Costs time and, worse, flattens the hierarchy so the student can't tell claims from scenery; produces detail-promotion picks ([[distractor-species-taxonomy]] species 11).
- **punctuation-blindness** — Treating colons, dashes, and italics as typographic noise rather than semantic signals. Formed because school grammar teaches punctuation as rules for writing, not as signals for reading. The student re-derives from content what the punctuation already announced, wasting the cheapest cue on the page.
- **signal-word-literalism** — Knowing "however" matters but only scanning for that exact word, missing the synonym family (yet, still, in fact, recent work suggests) and non-lexical pivots. Formed by memorized keyword lists. Produces missed pivots on passages that vary the signal.
- **decorative-flag-assumption** — At the other extreme: treating EVERY flag as pointing to the answer. Some marked material is a concession or a scene-setter; flags mark importance to the AUTHOR's argument, which the question may probe from an angle. The map narrows the search; it doesn't finish it.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **uniform-effort-reading**: flat per-sentence dwell in reading telemetry; slow overall pace; detail-promotion misses.
- **punctuation-blindness**: misses on items whose credited answer restates a colon/dash payload; when asked "where would you look?", never mentions punctuation.
- **signal-word-literalism**: catches "however" pivots but misses "in fact" or hedge-then-assert pivots; template recognition inconsistent across surface wordings.
- **decorative-flag-assumption**: overconfident picks of choices restating a flagged concession; answers "it was emphasized" as sole justification.

## Remediation pathways
- uniform-effort-reading: two-speed drill — student marks each sentence of a passage "slow" or "fast" BEFORE answering, then compares with where the answer actually lived; repeated until speed-setting is automatic.
- punctuation-blindness: payload drills — passages with colons/dashes where the task is only "what does the mark promise, and what delivers it?"; then show items whose answers are the payloads.
- signal-word-literalism: build the family inventory by FUNCTION (contrast, cause, assertion-after-hedge) rather than by word list ([[transition-relationship-families]]); drill recognition across varied surface forms.
- decorative-flag-assumption: contrast pair — one item where the flagged sentence is the answer's source, one where the flag marks a concession and the answer lives across the pivot; teaches flags as priors.

## Mastery criteria
Given a passage, the student can mark its two or three load-bearing sentences in seconds and state which mark identified each; their reading-speed profile shows differentiated dwell. Proof: maintained accuracy at reduced per-item time — the map's whole payoff is accuracy AND pace together.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: one loud flag (a colon before the key claim; a bolded "however"), question keyed directly to it.
- **Medium**: two competing flags (a concession also marked); synonym-family pivots; answer restates the flagged material one abstraction rung up.
- **Hard**: quiet flags only (a single intensifier, a positional cue); flags mark the setup while the tested idea is the unmarked implication; composes with [[belief-revision-template]] (hedge language as the flag) and [[reference-compression-tracking]] (the flagged sentence compresses an earlier idea).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach three flags only — first sentence, last sentence, colon — as "the three places to look." Concrete, positional, immediately usable.
- **Average**: full inventory organized by function; two-speed reading drills; connect flags to question types (global → edges; function → pivots).
- **Advanced**: negative-space reading (what unmarked stretches mean), competing-flag adjudication, attitude flags (scare quotes, evaluative adjectives).
- **1500+**: the map as prior, not oracle — items built to punish flag-chasing; quantified self-calibration (when their flag-guided first look misses, where was the answer actually?). Speed goal: the map runs preattentively during the first read.
