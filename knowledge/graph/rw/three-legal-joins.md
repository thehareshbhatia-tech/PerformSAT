# The three legal joins — the complete menu for two independent clauses

## What it is
When the clause count comes back "independent + independent," exactly three
families of marks may sit at the seam:
1. **Full stop family:** period or semicolon — interchangeable for boundary
   purposes (capitalization aside).
2. **Comma + coordinator:** a comma followed by one of the seven coordinating
   conjunctions (and, but, or, so, yet, for, nor).
3. **Colon or dash — conditional:** legal only when the second clause explains,
   defines, or gives the result of the first.
A comma alone is never on the menu. Nothing else is on the menu. The menu is
closed — that closure is the power.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Each join encodes a different reader instruction, which is why all three exist
and why they're not interchangeable with a bare comma. A full stop says "unit
complete; reset." Comma + coordinator says "unit complete, AND here is its
logical relation to the next one" — the coordinator carries the linking work
the comma alone is too weak to do. A colon says "unit complete; what follows
unpacks it" — a forward-pointing promise. The SAT tests whether students hold
the menu as a closed system: most distractors are either off-menu marks (comma
alone, semicolon + fragment) or on-menu marks used against their condition
(colon before a clause that merely continues the topic).

## Mental model
**A closed three-row lookup table, plus one semantic gate.** Two independents →
scan the choices against the three rows. Only the colon/dash row requires
thought beyond structure: ask "does clause 2 unpack clause 1 (explain, define,
give the result)?" Unpacks → colon/dash eligible. Merely adds more topic →
colon dies; full-stop family or comma+coordinator required. Everything else is
mechanical:
- period = semicolon (collapse them into one mental mark: "full stop");
- after a full stop, a coordinator is redundant — any choice shaped
  "period/semicolon + and/but" can be killed on sight;
- comma + coordinator needs BOTH pieces: coordinator without the comma is a
  run-on shaped join; comma without the coordinator is a splice
  ([[comma-splice]]).
- colon vs dash at a clause seam is pure style; the exam will not force that
  choice (unverified-claim — consistent across sources, verify against bank).

## Expert reasoning process
1. Confirm the count is two independents ([[clause-counting]]) — this menu is
   meaningless on any other count.
2. Collapse the choice set: mark period and semicolon options as the same mark;
   if both appear, BOTH are usually wrong
   ([[boundary-equivalence-cancellation]]).
3. Strike off-menu shapes: comma alone; full stop + coordinator; comma +
   connective adverb (*however* has no gluing power —
   [[boundaries-word-roles]]).
4. If a colon/dash survives, run the unpacking gate: restate clause 2's job in
   three words — "explains it" / "names it" / "results from it" → keep;
   "more info" → kill.
5. Install the survivor and re-read the whole sentence.

## Misconceptions
- **comma-can-join-sentences** — The splice belief; canonical treatment in
  [[comma-splice]], root in [[punctuating-by-ear]].
- **semicolon-as-fancy-comma** — Semicolons taught late and vaguely, so students
  deploy them as upscale commas before fragments and dependent clauses
  (*...; all of which failed*). Fix: the semicolon carries a period's full
  weight — both sides must pass the sentence test. Produces "counts-but-
  illegal-mark" misses even in students who parse perfectly.
- **colon-only-for-lists** — Students never learned colons can join clauses, so
  they eliminate correct colon answers; then, once told colons can join, they
  over-accept colons with no unpacking relation. Both directions come from
  learning the colon by example instead of by its promise ("what follows
  unpacks what precedes"). One source claims colons are disproportionately
  common in CORRECT answers on the digital exam (unverified-claim — verify
  against our bank before the tutor uses it as a prior).
- **full-stop-plus-conjunction-feels-thorough** — Students accept *...ended.
  But the data...* as suspect and *...ended; but the data...* as careful,
  because more connective tissue reads as more grammatical. In answer-choice
  form (semicolon + coordinator joining two clauses where the comma version is
  offered), the doubled join is the trap. Note the nuance: a sentence may
  legitimately BEGIN with a coordinator in real prose; the killable shape is
  the redundant join offered against its minimal twin.
- **stronger-break-for-longer-clauses** — The belief that long clauses "need"
  a semicolon and short ones a comma. Mark strength tracks structure, not
  length — pure [[punctuating-by-ear]] residue.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **semicolon-as-fancy-comma:** wrong picks cluster on semicolon choices where
  one side is a fragment; the student states the sides correctly when asked
  (this is the signature "counts-but-illegal-mark" profile from
  [[clause-counting]]).
- **colon-only-for-lists:** eliminates colon choices instantly on non-list
  items ("colons are for lists" verbatim in explanations); after partial
  instruction, accepts colons where clause 2 just continues the topic.
- **full-stop-plus-conjunction-feels-thorough:** picks the doubled-join
  distractor when a minimal legal join is available; justifies with "clearer."
- **stronger-break-for-longer-clauses:** mark choice correlates with clause
  length across their history rather than with structure.

## Remediation pathways
- **semicolon-as-fancy-comma:** identity drill — rewrite ten semicolon
  sentences with periods; any rewrite that fails exposes the illegal use. Chant
  the identity: semicolon = period with a lowercase letter. If misses persist,
  the sentence test itself is weak → [[sentence-vs-fragment]].
- **colon-only-for-lists:** matched pair — *She had one rule: never explain.*
  vs *She had one rule: she also kept a journal.* Same shape, opposite
  verdicts; the unpacking gate is the only difference. Teach the colon as a
  promise the second clause must keep.
- **full-stop-plus-conjunction-feels-thorough:** show the three minimal legal
  joins side by side for one sentence pair, then the doubled version; have the
  student articulate what job the extra word does (none).
- **stronger-break-for-longer-clauses:** betrayal set from
  [[punctuating-by-ear]] with clause lengths manipulated.

## Mastery criteria
Given "two independents" for free, the student can enumerate the full legal
menu from memory, kill all off-menu choices in under 15 seconds, and
articulate the colon gate in their own words. Proof: near-zero error rate on
medium boundary items whose count is unambiguous, plus correct colon
accept/reject decisions in both directions at hard difficulty.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy:** clean seam, obvious count, one legal choice among three off-menu
  shapes.
- **Medium:** the count requires demoting a dependent clause first; the
  connective-adverb decoy appears; period and semicolon both offered
  (equivalence cancellation available).
- **Hard:** the colon gate under a subtle unpacking relation; the seam hidden
  behind an interrupter ([[delete-and-reread]]) or a hinge phrase
  ([[window-widening]]); transition-word logic stacked on top of the join
  choice ([[transition-backward-anchoring]]).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling:** teach only two rows first (full stop; comma + coordinator)
  until automatic; add the colon row later. The closed menu is the message:
  "there are three ways. There is no fourth way. You never have to wonder."
- **Average:** drill the identities as chants (period = semicolon; comma+and =
  period; comma alone joins nothing), then the colon gate as the one place
  meaning enters.
- **Advanced:** teach the menu through the reader-instruction lens (reset /
  link-with-logic / promise-of-unpacking) so each rule is re-derivable, and
  introduce choice-set forensics ([[boundary-equivalence-cancellation]]).
- **1500+:** hard-mode colon items (result relations, definition relations that
  don't look like definitions) and adversarial doubled-join items. At this
  level, also flag the actuarial colon-frequency claim as a tiebreaker they
  should NOT rely on until we verify it (unverified-claim).
