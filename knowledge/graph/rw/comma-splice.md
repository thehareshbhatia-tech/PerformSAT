# Comma splice and run-on — the illegal joins and their trap signatures

## What it is
The two ways to join independent clauses illegally: a comma alone between them
(splice) and nothing at all (fused run-on). Both are categorically wrong on the
exam — no context rescues them. This node also catalogs the two constructions
that generate almost all splice bait, because recognizing the bait shape is
faster than re-deriving the verdict every time.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
A comma's structural meaning is "sub-unit boundary inside a still-open
assertion." Putting one between two complete assertions gives the reader a
contradictory instruction — "keep going" across a boundary where the grammar
says "done" — which is exactly why the convention forbids it, and why the rule
is re-derivable rather than arbitrary. The SAT tests it because the splice is
the single most natural-feeling error in student writing: speech runs
assertions together with a small dip in pitch, and the comma FEELS like that
dip. It is the flagship harvest of [[punctuating-by-ear]].

## Mental model
**A comma is never a bridge.** Two independent clauses, comma between them →
wrong, always, regardless of how long, smooth, or closely related the clauses
are. The two bait shapes to know on sight:
- **Comma + pronoun subject:** *...the reef recovered within a decade, it now
  supports hundreds of species.* The substring ", it/he/she/they/this/one" near
  an underline is a high-prior splice flag — treat it as an alarm to go count
  clauses (unverified-claim as to exam frequency; use as a search cue, never
  as a verdict).
- **Comma + connective adverb:** *...the reef recovered, however the survey
  found new damage.* *However/therefore/moreover* and time-openers
  (*today, currently, later*) have no gluing power — grammatically the adverb
  is invisible, so the boundary still needs a full stop. The adverb changes
  the MEANING relation, not the punctuation requirement.
And the run-on's signature disguise: a **hinge phrase** at the seam (*...the
reef recovered within a decade near the northern atolls the survey found new
damage* — where does clause 1 end?). No mark at a two-clause seam is fused no
matter how the phrases distract; finding the seam is [[window-widening]] work.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Alarm shapes first: comma + pronoun, comma + connective adverb near the
   blank → immediately run the count ([[clause-counting]]).
2. Confirm both sides are independent (demote subordinated/relative clauses —
   a comma before *which* or before a *because*-clause is NOT a splice).
3. Splice confirmed → the answer must come from the legal menu
   ([[three-legal-joins]]); prefer whichever legal join the choices actually
   offer (usually only one survives).
4. For a candidate connective adverb, delete it mentally and re-check: the
   punctuation verdict must be identical with and without it.
5. Beware the reverse trap: a choice that INSERTS a pronoun subject converts a
   legal fragment tail into a second independent clause — manufacturing a
   splice that wasn't there ([[sentence-fragment-connectors]]).

## Misconceptions
- **comma-as-soft-period** — Commas are "small pauses," so a long breath
  between clauses feels comma-worthy; the test writes long first clauses to
  make the splice feel natural. Face of [[punctuating-by-ear]]; this is its
  highest-volume harvest.
- **however-behaves-like-but** — Meaning-equivalence masks the word-class
  difference (adverb vs conjunction); produces confident ", however," splices.
  Deep cause: school teaches connector MEANING but never connector CLASS
  ([[boundaries-word-roles]]). The trap is cheap to write and appears at every
  difficulty band.
- **related-clauses-may-share-a-comma** — Students believe closely-related
  sentences are exempt ("they're about the same thing, so a comma keeps them
  together"). Semantic closeness has zero structural force; the exam picks
  maximally related clause pairs precisely to trigger this.
- **splice-fixable-only-by-period** — A repair-side gap: the student knows the
  splice is wrong but believes the period is the only cure, rejecting legal
  comma+coordinator or colon repairs. Produces misses when the choice set
  omits the full stop on purpose.
- **comma-before-subordinate-is-a-splice** — The overcorrection: after
  learning splices, students start flagging every comma+clause as one,
  including legal *", which..."* and *", although..."* attachments. Root: they
  learned the surface (comma + more words with a verb) instead of the
  count (comma + INDEPENDENT clause).

## Diagnostic indicators
- **comma-as-soft-period:** accepts splice choices specifically after long
  first clauses; sound vocabulary in explanations; bidirectional comma errors
  elsewhere ([[punctuating-by-ear]] profile).
- **however-behaves-like-but:** picks comma+*however* variants; when probed,
  says "however means but"; accuracy on the same items jumps when *but* is
  substituted — a clean differential test the tutor can run.
- **related-clauses-may-share-a-comma:** justifies splices with topical
  closeness ("it's continuing the same idea"); misses cluster on same-subject
  clause pairs.
- **splice-fixable-only-by-period:** correctly rejects the splice but then
  picks an off-menu "fix" or stalls when no period is offered; wrong answers
  cluster on items keying comma+coordinator or colon.
- **comma-before-subordinate-is-a-splice:** false-alarms on legal dependent
  attachments; their "splice" calls fail the independence check when asked to
  verify both sides.

## Remediation pathways
- **comma-as-soft-period:** the betrayal confrontation in
  [[punctuating-by-ear]], then re-anchor: a comma is never a bridge between
  two things that could each end with a period.
- **however-behaves-like-but:** minimal pair with only the connector swapped;
  then the invisibility test (delete the adverb — does the boundary still need
  a full stop?). Revisit [[boundaries-word-roles]] if the three-bin connector
  sort isn't automatic.
- **related-clauses-may-share-a-comma:** show two maximally-related clauses and
  two unrelated ones, same punctuation verdicts; structure is blind to topic.
- **splice-fixable-only-by-period:** repair drills where the period is banned —
  force one splice through all three legal joins ([[three-legal-joins]]).
- **comma-before-subordinate-is-a-splice:** independence-verification ritual:
  before calling "splice," read the post-comma material alone and demand it
  pass the sentence test ([[sentence-vs-fragment]]).

## Mastery criteria
The student catches 100% of planted splices in a mixed set WITHOUT
false-alarming on legal comma+dependent attachments, and can repair one splice
three legal ways. Proof: hard-band items where the splice is camouflaged by a
long first clause or a connective adverb, answered with structural
justification.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy:** short clauses, comma + pronoun subject at an obvious seam.
- **Medium:** comma + connective adverb; long first clause raising the
  acoustic pull; legal repair other than a period keyed.
- **Hard:** the manufactured splice (pronoun inserted by the answer choice);
  splice hidden behind an interrupter so the clause count needs
  [[delete-and-reread]]; fused seam behind a hinge phrase needing
  [[window-widening]]; bracketed-transition splice (comma pair around
  *however* between two independents — see [[transition-backward-anchoring]]).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling:** teach ONLY "comma is never a bridge" plus the comma+pronoun
  alarm. One rule, one alarm shape, drilled to reflex before any nuance.
- **Average:** add the connective-adverb family and the invisibility test;
  drill the *but/however* differential until the word-class distinction is
  articulate, not just pattern-matched.
- **Advanced:** teach the reverse traps — the manufactured splice and the
  false-alarm on dependent clauses — so the rule fires on the COUNT, not on
  the surface shape.
- **1500+:** all remaining value is in camouflage: interrupter-masked splices
  and the bracketed-transition splice, where the deletion test itself misleads
  (its documented failure mode — see [[delete-and-reread]]). Drill those two
  compositions specifically.
