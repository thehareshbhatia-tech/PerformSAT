# Reference tracking: pronouns and compression-nouns carry the argument

## What it is
Academic prose never repeats an idea in full; it compresses. Two compression devices carry most of the load: **pronouns** (it, they, this, that) and **compression-nouns** — nouns that name ideas rather than objects (claim, assumption, phenomenon, discrepancy, conclusion), where one word stands in for an entire prior sentence or multi-sentence chunk. Tracking what each device points to is the skill of keeping hold of the argument's thread. The exam almost never asks about it directly, yet every hard passage requires it.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Writers compress because uncompressed prose is unbearably repetitive — compression is a courtesy to fluent readers and a wall for everyone else. The test exploits the wall: key findings routinely hinge on one compression-noun phrase, and questions target the compressed idea rather than the surface detail (unverified-claim on frequency; verify against official items and our bank). A student who can't unpack "this discrepancy" cannot answer a question about what the discrepancy is, no matter how good their elimination technique.

## Mental model
**Every pronoun and compression-noun is a baggage-claim ticket; the luggage is behind you.** Three rules govern redemption:
1. **The referent is (almost always) earlier.** To resolve a ticket, back up to the START of the sentence containing the earlier idea and read forward — never stare at the ticket itself, never read onward from it. (Rare cases run forward; unverified-claim: passages occasionally place a pronoun before its noun within a two-sentence span.)
2. **The luggage may be a proposition, not an object.** A compression-noun's referent is often a full clause or a stated relationship. Unpacking it means RESTATING the earlier idea, not pointing at a nearby noun.
3. **Tickets are single-use.** The same pronoun can switch referents sentence to sentence. Resolve each occurrence independently — no referent inertia.
Cheap filters: grammatical number (plural pronoun needs plural referent) shortlists candidates; sense-check ("would this noun logically do what the sentence says?") confirms the survivor. For "the former / the latter": index by order in the ORIGINAL two-item list, never by order of mention in the follow-up sentence.

## Expert reasoning process
1. On meeting a pronoun or compression-noun that matters to the question: stop. Do not reread the phrase; jump back to the previous sentence boundary and read forward.
2. Use number agreement to shortlist candidate referents; confirm by sense.
3. For compression-nouns, expect a proposition: restate the prior sentence's claim and check the noun is a fair one-word summary of it.
4. For former/latter, physically locate the original two-item list and index into it by original order.
5. Treat every new occurrence as unresolved until checked.
6. Carry the unpacked idea (not the pronoun) into your prediction ([[predict-then-verify]]).

## Misconceptions
- **noun-means-object** — The elementary-school "person, place, or thing" schema makes students parse compression-nouns as naming physical things nearby in the text. They hunt for an object antecedent, fail, and conclude the passage is unreadable. Produces misses on any item whose stem or choices hinge on the compressed idea; feeds proposition-trap distractors where every single-noun "referent" a student lands on is wrong.
- **reread-in-place-loop** — When confused, rereading the confusing phrase itself (or reading forward from it) because backing up feels like wasted time. The referent is BEHIND them, so the loop never terminates. The signature failure behavior on dense passages: costs both accuracy and pacing.
- **referent-inertia** — Assuming one pronoun keeps one referent for the whole passage. Formed because easy texts behave that way. The exam switches a pronoun's referent mid-description precisely to exploit it.
- **nearest-noun-grab** — Resolving to the closest preceding noun without number or sense checks. A heuristic that usually works in casual reading; fails exactly where the test inserts distance or a decoy noun of matching position between anaphor and referent.
- **follow-up-order-confusion** — Reading "the latter" as "whichever was mentioned most recently," which breaks when the writer discusses the latter first.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **noun-means-object**: when asked "what does 'this phenomenon' refer to?", answers with a single noun instead of a restated clause; picks right-fact-wrong-referent distractors ([[distractor-species-taxonomy]] species 10).
- **reread-in-place-loop**: telemetry shows repeated dwell on one region with no backward scroll/eye movement; extreme per-item time on dense passages with eventual wrong answer.
- **referent-inertia**: misses cluster on passages where a pronoun shifts referents; explanation reveals they carried the first referent through.
- **nearest-noun-grab**: picks the choice naming the decoy noun; fast, confident, wrong on distance-trap items.
- **follow-up-order-confusion**: systematic misses on former/latter items — a narrow, highly specific signal worth an automatic tag.

## Remediation pathways
- noun-means-object: contrast demo — show the same paragraph with every compression spelled out in full (bloated, unreadable) vs. compressed; then tap-the-referent exercises where the correct "answer" is a whole highlighted clause. If it doesn't land, drill single-pronoun resolution first.
- reread-in-place-loop: teach the physical move — "the answer is behind you; back up one sentence boundary" — and drill it as a reflex on flagged phrases. Budget guidance: one backward jump costs seconds; the loop costs the item.
- referent-inertia: shift-trap drills — passages where one pronoun changes referents; student marks each occurrence's referent independently.
- nearest-noun-grab: number-and-sense ritual before accepting any referent; decoy-noun contrast pairs.
- follow-up-order-confusion: re-derive the convention from "former = first in the list, latter = later in the list," then one inverted-order example.

## Mastery criteria
Given a marked-up passage, the student can identify the referent of every flagged pronoun and compression-noun — including proposition-referents, restated in their own words — without multiple choice scaffolding. Proof: near-perfect tap-the-referent performance on shift-trap and proposition-trap passages, plus disappearance of species-10 picks in scored items.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: single obvious pronoun, referent in the same sentence.
- **Medium**: referent in a different sentence; plural-agreement filtering needed; a decoy noun sits closer than the true referent.
- **Hard**: same pronoun shifting referents; compression-nouns pointing at whole propositions; former/latter with inverted follow-up order; poetic or 19th-century syntax placing the referent at stylistic distance (composes with [[fiction-poetry-literal-reading]]). At the top end, the QUESTION quietly depends on a compressed referent the stem never mentions.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: pronouns only, modern prose only, referent always one sentence back. The baggage-ticket metaphor. Tap-the-referent widgets with instant feedback; no MCQ until the decoding is reliable.
- **Average**: introduce compression-nouns and the proposition rule ("the answer might be a whole sentence"); mixed drills with decoy nouns and number filtering.
- **Advanced**: shift traps, former/latter inversions, and bridging to scored items — show an Inferences item where each distractor encodes a differently mis-resolved referent, so the subskill's cash value is explicit.
- **1500+**: archaic and poetic syntax, and speed: resolution should be a sub-second reflex on modern prose, with deliberate backing-up reserved for genuinely gnarly cases. Teach them to notice when a stem's innocent-looking noun ("the author's claim") is itself a compression ticket needing redemption.
