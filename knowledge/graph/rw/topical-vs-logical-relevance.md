# The root misconception: mistaking topical relevance for logical relevance

## What it is
This node names the single deepest error underlying the whole evidence-and-logic cluster: treating "is about the same subject" as if it meant "bears on the argument." A statement is *topically* relevant when it mentions the passage's entities, findings, or theme; it is *logically* relevant only when it changes the status of the specific claim at issue — completes it, supports it, or damages it. The SAT's most reliable distractor class across three question families is a statement with full topical relevance and zero logical relevance.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
For most of a student's school career, topical relevance *was* the criterion: find a quote about the topic, cite a fact about the subject, and credit followed. The SAT deliberately breaks that contract to test genuine argument evaluation. The same root error wears different masks per family: on text completions it appears as the **entity-mention halo** (choices naming passage people/things feel safe) and vivid-detail absorption; on textual support items it appears as **topic-match-equals-support** (any finding about the subject feels like support); on quantitative items it appears as **graph-accurate-but-claim-irrelevant** choices (a true description of the chart that addresses the wrong aspect of the claim). One disease, three rashes.

## Mental model
**Relevance is a relation to the claim, not to the topic.** Picture the claim as a door and every candidate statement as a key: topical relevance means the key is in the right building; logical relevance means it turns *this* lock. The test fills the building with keys. The operational check is one question asked of every tempting choice: *"If this statement is true, what happens to the claim?"* If the honest answer is "nothing," the choice is garnish — no matter how accurate, vivid, or on-topic it is.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Fix the claim first ([[argument-spine-decomposition]]); relevance cannot be assessed against a topic, only against a claim.
2. For each candidate statement (an answer choice, a data reading), ask the one question: does its truth move the claim — complete, strengthen, or weaken it?
3. Treat surface signals of relevance — shared entities, recycled vocabulary, factual accuracy about the passage or chart — as *zero evidence* of logical relevance. They are cheap for item writers to plant.
4. Expect the correct answer to sometimes look topically *drab* (a plain statement, a small unglamorous data point) while a distractor looks topically rich. Richness is bait.
5. When two choices both seem to "move the claim," check *which* claim — passages often carry a neighboring claim, and a statement can be logically relevant to the wrong one.

## Misconceptions
This node IS the misconception, decomposed into its per-family masks:
- **entity-mention-halo** (completions) — choices naming passage entities feel on-topic and safe; under time pressure, mention becomes a proxy for relevance. Formed by skim-and-match test-taking habits. Produces picks of vivid but argumentatively idle details.
- **topic-match-equals-support** (textual CoE) — any finding about the claim's subject registers as support. Formed by school evidence tasks that only ever required topicality. The test exploits it with on-topic findings that never touch the claimed relation — e.g., generic praise of a product when the claim isolates one ingredient.
- **graph-match-equals-correct** (quantitative CoE) — "this choice is true of the graph" is treated as sufficient. Formed by years of single-source comprehension checks where factual accuracy WAS the criterion. The test plants multiple graph-accurate distractors with the wrong focus (unverified-claim on "sometimes multiple per item" — verify against official items and our bank); students verifying choice-by-choice against the chart pick the first accurate one they meet.
- **richness-bias** — the meta-error: the more detailed/specific/vivid a statement, the more probative it feels. In fact detail level and probative force are independent, and item writers exploit the felt correlation.

## Diagnostic indicators
- Cross-family error signature: the student's wrong picks across Inferences AND both CoE skills share the property "accurate/on-topic but claim-inert." This cross-skill pattern is the tell that the root, not the family-specific technique, needs treatment.
- *entity-mention-halo*: wrong picks name passage entities; justification is "the passage talks about that."
- *topic-match-equals-support*: on support items, justification cites subject overlap ("it's about the same study") without stating a direction or mechanism.
- *graph-match-equals-correct*: student verifies each choice against the chart and stops at the first true one; justification is "I checked, and that's accurate."
- All masks: the student cannot answer "what happens to the claim if this is true?" without re-reading, or answers "nothing" and still resists eliminating.

## Remediation pathways
- The universal move: install the one-question test — *"If true, what happens to the claim?"* — as a mandatory gate on the final two choices of every evidence item. It is short enough to survive time pressure and directly measures logical relevance.
- *entity-mention-halo*: spine-vs-garnish sort ([[argument-spine-decomposition]] remediation), then a drill where every distractor names passage entities and the correct answer names none.
- *topic-match-equals-support*: contrast pair — a claim about a specific mechanism plus two findings, one about the general topic, one isolating the mechanism; have the student explain why only the second moves the claim. Then route to [[directional-evidence-prediction]] so they predict the needed evidence *before* seeing bait.
- *graph-match-equals-correct*: demonstrate an item where chart-first verification provably yields the wrong answer, then teach claim-first order ([[claim-anchored-data-reading]]).
- If no fix lands, the student likely can't isolate the claim at all — revisit [[argument-spine-decomposition]] before continuing.

## Mastery criteria
The student spontaneously distinguishes "true/on-topic" from "bears on the claim" in their own explanations, and eliminates accuracy-bait distractors *by naming what the claim needed*. Proof: across a mixed set spanning all three families, near-zero picks of claim-inert distractors, including graph-accurate ones on quantitative items — the cross-family consistency is the point.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: claim-inert distractors are also slightly off-topic, so topicality still filters them; the misconception survives uncorrected.
- **Medium**: distractors become fully on-topic and factually accurate; only the claim-relation test separates them. This is where scores drop for topicality-reliant students.
- **Hard**: distractors are on-topic, accurate, AND logically relevant *to a neighboring claim* in the passage; separating them requires precise claim-holder discipline plus the relation test. Composes with [[support-and-weaken-textual]] mechanism-specificity and [[claim-anchored-data-reading]] accuracy-vs-relevance auditing.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: use non-SAT material first. "Claim: this restaurant's desserts are overpriced. Statement: the restaurant is very popular." On-topic? Yes. Does it move the claim? No. Ten of these builds the distinction faster than any passage work.
- **Average**: name the school-contract break explicitly — "everything that earned you credit in English class is now the trap." Then install the one-question gate and drill it on the family they're currently studying.
- **Advanced**: work the neighboring-claim variant and quantitative accuracy-bait; their topicality reflex is gone, but wrong-claim relevance still catches them.
- **1500+**: have them author claim-inert distractors for given claims (the item-writer's exercise). When a student can manufacture the bait, the halo effect is permanently dead; residual work is speed — applying the gate in under five seconds.
