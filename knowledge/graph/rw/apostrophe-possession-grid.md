# Apostrophes: the number × possession grid and the pronoun inversion

## What it is
Every noun has four written shapes — plain plural (-s, no apostrophe), singular
possessive ('s), plural possessive (s'), and the irregular-plural possessive
(children's: 's because the plural has no s). An apostrophe item is just choosing
a cell in this 2×2 grid (number × possession). Pronouns INVERT the noun logic:
possessive pronouns take no apostrophe at all (its, their, whose, your), and a
pronoun + apostrophe always spells a contraction (it's = it is/has). Sound
carries zero information here — every competing form is a homophone — so only
structure decides.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Apostrophes are the purest test of writing-by-analysis vs writing-by-ear: the
student must encode number and ownership into marks the ear can't hear. The exam
leans on two noun shapes at once ("the researchers' findings") and on the
its/their homophone families [unverified-claim: it- and they- forms dominate;
you- and who- forms are rare — verify vs our bank]. Items span all bands; fused
number + apostrophe sets are the hard end.

## Mental model
Two mechanical tests cover everything:
- **Nouns — the next-word test.** Look at the word immediately after the noun in
  question. Another noun follows → possession is happening → an apostrophe
  belongs (position set by the owner's number). Anything else follows (verb,
  preposition, end of sentence) → no apostrophe. Why it works: only a noun can
  be owned.
- **Pronouns — the expansion test.** Any pronoun spelled with an apostrophe is a
  contraction: expand it to its full phrase (it is, they are, who is) and reread
  the sentence. Parses → contraction is right; doesn't → you need the
  no-apostrophe possessive. Why the inversion exists: possessive pronouns are
  complete words in their own right — its is to it what his is to him, and
  nobody writes "hi's."
Plus two instant-kill facts: pluralization alone NEVER uses an apostrophe; and
some spellings don't exist at all (any apostrophized possessive of it), so they
die on sight with no context needed. In a two-noun stack ("the owner's
records"), the second noun never takes an apostrophe unless a third noun follows
it. And there/their/they're adds a third member that is neither possessive nor
contraction — a place/existential word; ask whether the sentence points at a
location.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Triage ([[choice-set-diagnosis]]): choices permuting apostrophe placement or
   homophone spellings → this node.
2. Kill impossible forms and apostrophized second-nouns immediately — form-based
   eliminations are free.
3. If number also varies across choices, settle number FIRST (from the referent,
   via [[grammatical-number-assignment]] / [[pronoun-antecedent-agreement]]) —
   it eliminates half the grid before any punctuation thinking.
4. Nouns: run the next-word test; if possession, set apostrophe position from
   the owner's number (one owner → 's; many owners → s'; no-s plural → 's).
   Work two-noun stacks left to right: owner gets number + apostrophe, owned
   noun stays bare.
5. Pronouns: expand every apostrophized choice and reread; check the there-form
   against "is this a place/existence statement?"
6. Never consult the ear — remind yourself all candidates sound identical.

## Misconceptions
- **apostrophe-marks-plural** — an apostrophe gets inserted when pluralizing,
  especially on long, unfamiliar, or s-ending words. Forms because plural -s and
  possessive -s are phonetically identical and public signage models the error
  constantly; the apostrophe becomes a general "this noun was modified" flag.
  Signature wrong answer: plural-with-apostrophe in any noun item.
- **possessive-pronouns-need-apostrophes** — school drilled "apostrophe-s shows
  ownership" on nouns, and students generalize it to pronouns, where the rule
  inverts. Genuinely counterintuitive; produces it's-before-a-noun picks.
- **homophone-collapse** — their/there/they're (and your/you're) are stored as
  one fuzzy sound with spellings chosen by habit. Forms from writing by ear,
  reinforced by texting. The expansion test is the direct antidote.
- **possessive-zone-spreads** — in two-noun stacks the student apostrophizes
  both nouns or the second one, believing possession "covers" the phrase. Forms
  from surface pattern-matching rather than seeing ownership as a directed
  relation (owner → owned).
- **apostrophe-position-is-style** — s' is read as a fancy variant of 's rather
  than as encoding plural ownership. Forms because no one ever told the student
  the POSITION carries meaning; produces random position picks that are right
  half the time and mask the gap.

## Diagnostic indicators
- Chooses plural + apostrophe when no noun follows → **apostrophe-marks-plural**
  (the next-word test was never run).
- Chooses the contraction form directly before a noun → 
  **possessive-pronouns-need-apostrophes**.
- their/they're/there picks that track sentence rhythm rather than expansion;
  inconsistent across items → **homophone-collapse**.
- Double-apostrophe or second-noun-apostrophe picks in stacks → 
  **possessive-zone-spreads** (this distractor is a standard plant and is always
  wrong).
- Correct possession detection but ~50% accuracy on 's vs s' position, with
  number stated wrong when asked "how many owners?" → 
  **apostrophe-position-is-style**, or an upstream number gap — probe
  [[grammatical-number-assignment]] before drilling punctuation.

## Remediation pathways
- **apostrophe-marks-plural**: teach the next-word test with its why ("only
  nouns can be owned"); drill classify-only (apostrophe needed: yes/no) on
  twenty noun+next-word pairs before any full items.
- **possessive-pronouns-need-apostrophes**: the his-anchor — its:it :: his:him;
  "you'd never write hi's." One vivid contrast beats repetition; then expansion
  drills.
- **homophone-collapse**: make expansion mandatory and mechanical — every
  apostrophized pronoun choice gets spoken in full before judgment. Add the
  place-check for the there-form.
- **possessive-zone-spreads**: draw the arrow — ownership points FROM owner TO
  owned; the mark sits on the tail, never the head. Then two-noun stack drills
  with "who owns what?" verbalized.
- **apostrophe-position-is-style**: teach position-as-code ("apostrophe before
  the s = one owner; after = many"), then owner-counting drills; revisit plural
  morphology (incl. no-s irregulars and unchanged Latin plurals like species,
  where fake singular back-formations are the planted distractor).

## Mastery criteria
Runs the next-word and expansion tests unprompted and can state WHY each works;
solves fused number × apostrophe sets in the correct order (number first);
never picks an impossible form or an apostrophized second noun. Proof:
consistent hard-band accuracy on two-noun stacks and its-family sets, with
owner-count verbalized correctly.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: single noun, four grid cells, next-word test decides instantly;
  its/it's with an obvious expansion.
- **medium**: two-noun stacks; they-triple with the there-form live; owner
  number requiring passage logic (one brain per person, many birds per flock).
- **hard**: number × apostrophe fusion where the referent's number must be
  hunted first (composes [[pronoun-antecedent-agreement]] and
  [[grammatical-number-assignment]]); irregular and unchanged plurals; a noun's
  's that is actually an is/has contraction and must be disambiguated.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach the two tests as physical rituals (point at the next
  word; say the expansion aloud). Don't present the full grid until the tests
  are habitual — the grid without the tests reads as four things to memorize.
- **Average**: full grid + position-as-code + the pronoun inversion with the
  his-anchor. Introduce the number-first ordering on mixed sets.
- **Advanced**: two-noun stacks, irregular plurals, the noun-'s-as-contraction
  reading, and speed (form-based eliminations should be instant).
- **1500+**: the only misses left are fused sets under time pressure and exotic
  plurals. Drill the discipline of never ear-checking — high scorers regress to
  the ear precisely when confident — and the fixed order: kill impossible
  forms → settle number → place the mark.
