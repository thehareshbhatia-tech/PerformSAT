# Window-widening: read past the blank before you decide

## What it is
The single meta-skill underneath nearly every Standard English Conventions item: the evidence that settles a punctuation, agreement, or modifier choice almost never sits at the blank — it sits somewhere the eye hasn't traveled yet. Widening the window means deliberately reading from the start of the current sentence to well past the option, and sometimes into the neighboring sentence, before committing. Most conventions traps are not knowledge failures; they are attention failures, engineered so that a decision made from a three-word neighborhood looks correct and a decision made from the whole structure is correct.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The digital SAT conventions items are built by writers who know exactly how far a rushed reader looks. The subject that governs a verb is placed several words upstream behind an interrupter; the noun a modifier must touch is placed just past where the reader stopped; the second half of a punctuation pair is placed a full clause away. Every one of these is a bet that the student judged locally. A student who has internalized window-widening defuses the entire category with one habit rather than topic-by-topic rules.

## Mental model
**The answer lives outside the blast radius of the blank.** Before choosing, expand your reading window in both directions until you can see the full grammatical unit the option belongs to — the whole sentence for boundaries and agreement, the clause on the other side for pair punctuation and transitions. If you decided without moving your eyes, you almost certainly fell for the local read.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Freeze the reflex. The instant an option "sounds right" from the immediate words, treat that as a warning, not a signal.
2. Read from the sentence's true start — past any opening interrupter — to locate the grammatical spine (subject + verb, or the two things being joined).
3. Read past the option to the sentence's end (or the next clause) to find the other half of whatever the option is part of: the verb for a subject, the noun for a modifier, the closing mark for an opening one.
4. Only now classify the item ([[choice-set-diagnosis]]) and apply the specific rule.
5. Verify by re-reading the full widened span, not the neighborhood.

## Misconceptions
- **local-sufficiency** — Believing the words immediately around the blank contain enough to decide. Forms because in easy items they often do, and because reading is effortful under time pressure, so the mind economizes to the smallest span that yields an answer. Produces the keyed-wrong choice on exactly the items writers designed to punish local reading.
- **stop-at-the-blank** — Reading up to the option and choosing, never continuing past it. Forms from left-to-right reading habit: the option feels like a destination, not a midpoint. Fatal for modifier-target and pair-punctuation items whose evidence is downstream.
- **first-noun-is-the-subject** — Grabbing the nearest noun before the verb as its subject. Forms because proximity usually equals grammatical relationship in simple sentences, so the shortcut is right often enough to feel reliable — until an interrupter is inserted precisely to break it (see [[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]]).

## Diagnostic indicators
- **local-sufficiency / stop-at-the-blank**: accuracy is high on short-sentence items and collapses specifically on long sentences with interrupters or clause-spanning structure — a length-correlated error pattern, not a topic-correlated one.
- **first-noun-is-the-subject**: agreement errors cluster on items with a prepositional phrase or clause between subject and verb; the student can state the rule but misapplies it under disguise.
- In teach-back, the student justifies a choice by quoting only the words adjacent to the blank.

## Remediation pathways
- Drill the habit, not the topics: mixed conventions items where the student must underline the full governing span (subject-to-verb, modifier-to-noun, open-mark-to-close-mark) BEFORE choosing. The physical marking externalizes the window.
- For first-noun errors, hand the student the interrupter-deletion move from [[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]] and [[essential-vs-nonessential]] so the true spine becomes visible.
- If errors persist even with the window widened, the deficit is downstream rule knowledge, not attention — route to the specific topic node.

## Mastery criteria
The student's accuracy no longer degrades with sentence length or interrupter presence — the discriminating evidence. They can, on request, point to the exact upstream/downstream word that settles the item, proving they read the whole unit rather than the neighborhood.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: short sentences where local and global reads agree — the habit is invisible because it costs nothing.
- **Medium**: one interrupter between subject and verb, or a modifier whose target sits just past the option.
- **Hard**: multiple nested interrupters, pair punctuation spanning a full clause, or evidence that lives in the previous sentence (transitions, pronoun reference) — the window must widen across the sentence boundary.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach this FIRST, before any specific punctuation rule — a student who reads locally cannot benefit from rules whose evidence they never see. Frame it as one habit that quietly fixes many item types.
- **Average**: pair with [[choice-set-diagnosis]] so widening feeds directly into classifying what the item tests.
- **Advanced**: cross-sentence widening for transitions and pronoun reference; recognizing that interrupter length is itself a difficulty dial writers turn.
- **1500+**: the habit is automatic; the value is meta — recognizing when an item is ONLY hard because it bets on a local read, which lowers guessing risk and speeds confident selection.
