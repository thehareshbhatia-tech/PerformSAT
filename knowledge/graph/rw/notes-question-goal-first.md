# Notes questions: the goal sentence is the whole question

## What it is
Rhetorical Synthesis items present a bulleted set of research notes, a stated goal ("The student wants to …"), and four choices that "use relevant information from the notes." The governing insight: the **goal sentence is the only evaluation criterion**. The correct answer is essentially the goal's key phrase restated in new words — the question contains its own answer key. The bullet list is largely a decoy: every choice is faithfully drawn from the notes, so verifying choices against the bullets is not the tested skill.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
This type is native to the digital SAT and tests whether a writer can select content *for a rhetorical purpose* — the same judgment as deciding what belongs in a sentence given an aim. On the real exam these items close each R&W module (the module runs vocabulary-first through the Expression block and puts notes/synthesis **last**), and at least one appears in effectively every module — this ordering and near-guaranteed presence is verified against our own bank and data, not just claimed. Because they sit last, they meet students at maximum fatigue and minimum clock, which is exactly when the goal-first shortcut matters most.

## Mental model
**Invert the reading habit: interrogate the question, not the passage.** Every other reading item rewards careful passage reading; this one rewards careful *question* reading. Picture the goal sentence as a lock and the four choices as keys — the bullets are the box the keys came in. You inspect the lock (goal verb + focus phrase), then try keys against it. You do not study the box. The bullets earn a look only as a tiebreaker if two keys appear to fit ([[goal-decomposition-checklist]] handles why usually only one truly does).

## Expert reasoning process
1. Skip the bullets. Read the goal sentence first.
2. Extract the goal's verb (present / introduce / emphasize / specify / compare …) and its focus phrase, plus any qualifier (audience, relation). Full parsing is [[goal-decomposition-checklist]].
3. Predict the observable features the right answer must display (a comparison word, a background clause, purpose framing, plural examples — see [[goal-answer-signatures]]).
4. Scan the choices for those features — keyword-level first, then verify the survivor in full.
5. Reject on-topic choices doing the wrong rhetorical job; truth is not a discriminator here.
6. Commit at the first full match. Glance at the bullets only if two choices genuinely survive.

## Misconceptions
- **bullets-first-processing** — the student reads the note list top-to-bottom before the question, because layout order and the school rule "read everything, then answer" dictate it. This is the notes-question expression of the domain's root misconception (choice/stimulus-first processing). The cost is mostly time — often the difference between finishing the module and not — but it also floods working memory with facts that all reappear in the choices, making *every* option feel supported.
- **truth-equals-correctness** — the student believes a factually accurate, notes-supported statement must be right. Forms because nearly all school assessment rewards true statements. Here all four choices are true, so truth has zero discriminating power; the student ends up choosing the most interesting or detailed true statement, which is frequently a designed distractor.
- **complexity-freeze** — the student reads the dense bulleted format as "hard question" and either over-invests time or skips it. Forms from the surface heuristic that more text means more difficulty. Costly precisely because these are among the most mechanical, fastest items on the exam once the workflow is known — and they arrive when time is shortest.
- **support-verification-compulsion** — the student cross-checks the chosen answer against the bullets "to be safe," re-reading the notes after already finding the match. Forms as residue of evidence-based reading training. Pure time loss: fabricated-content distractors are claimed not to be part of this type's design (**unverified-claim** — all four choices restating genuine note content should be re-confirmed against official items before the tutor asserts it as absolute).

## Diagnostic indicators
- **bullets-first-processing**: long time-to-first-choice-interaction on notes items despite decent accuracy; the student, asked to walk through their process, starts by summarizing the bullets. Module-tail timeouts with unanswered notes questions are the downstream signature.
- **truth-equals-correctness**: wrong answers are the informative-but-wrong-job distractors; the student defends a miss with "but it's right there in the notes."
- **complexity-freeze**: notes items skipped or answered last with panic-length dwell times, while the same student handles transitions briskly.
- **support-verification-compulsion**: correct answers with anomalously long tail-times; visible re-reading of bullets after selection.

## Remediation pathways
- **bullets-first-processing**: the bullets-hidden demonstration — present a notes item with the bullet list physically removed, goal + choices only, and let the student discover it's fully solvable. Then reintroduce the bullets as scenery. One or two rounds of this scaffold usually rewires the entry point. Our drill UI can enforce it by revealing bullets only on demand.
- **truth-equals-correctness**: show all four choices verified true against the notes, then ask "so what actually differs?" — forcing the discovery that the rhetorical job is the only live variable. Follow with [[goal-decomposition-checklist]].
- **complexity-freeze**: timing proof — have the student solve three notes items goal-first with a stopwatch; the measured speed dissolves the threat appraisal better than reassurance does.
- **support-verification-compulsion**: name the behavior, state the design fact (with its caveat), and cap the item with a hard time budget; permission to skip verification is the cure.

## Mastery criteria
The student's first fixation on a notes item is the goal sentence, they can state the goal's requirements before reading any choice, and they resolve typical items comfortably under a minute without touching the bullets. Proof: sustained accuracy with notes-item times at or below their transition-item times — the inversion of the naive pattern.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: single-component goal ("emphasize X") with one choice on-focus and three off-focus; goal-first solves it on keywords alone.
- **medium**: multi-component goals and right-topic-wrong-job distractors; requires the full checklist discipline of [[goal-decomposition-checklist]].
- **hard**: hard versions sharpen category precision (adjacent-category and superset distractors) and stack components (focus + audience + relation) — the escalation lives in the decomposition and signatures nodes, not in this workflow, which is constant across difficulty. Bullet sets stay short (a claimed 3–5 notes — **unverified-claim** on the exact range).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach the inversion as a slogan they can hold ("read the ask, not the notes") and run bullets-hidden items until the entry habit flips. Defer multi-component goals.
- **Average**: full workflow with feature prediction; drill the truth-vs-job distinction explicitly with all-true choice sets.
- **Advanced**: position these as the module's free time bank ([[writing-module-economics]]) — train them to be the fastest items on the page, banking clock for the reading-heavy front of the module (answered earlier or via flagged navigation).
- **1500+**: the residual risk is overconfidence on multi-component goals — a fast reader who match-commits on the first satisfied component. Route them to [[goal-decomposition-checklist]] hard sets; the workflow itself needs no further work.
