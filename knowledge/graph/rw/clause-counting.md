# Clause counting — the master decision procedure (HUB)

## What it is
The single routine that decides every Boundaries question: count the independent
clauses touching the punctuation slot, then look up the (small, closed) menu of
legal marks for that count. Every punctuation "rule" in this cluster is a row in
that lookup table; this node is the table's index. A Boundaries item is never
really asking "which mark sounds right?" — it is asking "one sentence here, or
two?" The mark is downstream of the count.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The College Board is probing whether a student can parse sentence architecture
under time pressure and context noise — a compressed proxy for reading and
writing competence. Boundaries is among the highest-frequency SEC territory, and
nearly every distractor in it is generated by one of exactly two failure points:
the student miscounts the clauses, or counts correctly and then picks an illegal
mark. That two-failure structure is why this node is the diagnostic hub for the
whole cluster (see Diagnostic indicators).

## Mental model
**Count first, punctuate second.** The procedure, run the same way every time:

1. **Widen the window.** Read from the true start of the sentence to its final
   period — never judge from the words near the blank
   (see [[window-widening]] for why the items punish local reads).
2. **Find every working verb; attach each to its subject** (the parse from
   [[sentence-vs-fragment]]).
3. **Demote captured clauses.** A subject-verb pair opened by a subordinator or
   a relative word is dependent — it doesn't count.
4. **Count the free (independent) clauses** on each side of the slot.
5. **Menu lookup:**
   - two independent clauses → the legal-join menu ([[three-legal-joins]]);
   - sentence + fragment → the connector menu ([[sentence-fragment-connectors]]);
   - one clause, no seam → NO boundary-strength mark may appear at all
     ([[essential-vs-nonessential]]).
6. **Cross-check the choice set** for equivalent options that cancel each other
   ([[boundary-equivalence-cancellation]]).

The generative why: punctuation is a structural notation system. Writing lost
speech's intonation, so marks encode where units of assertion begin and end. A
mark's "strength" is exactly the size of the boundary it is licensed to close —
which is why the count, and nothing else, determines the mark.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Blank spotted at a seam → immediately back up to the sentence's true start.
2. Verb-first parse; subjects attached; subordinated/relative clauses demoted.
3. State the count out loud (internally): "independent + independent" or
   "independent + fragment" or "one clause, decorated."
4. Only now read the choices — and read them as a SET: strike options that are
   grammatically identical, strike marks illegal for the count.
5. If two independents and a colon/dash is offered, ask the one semantic
   question the menu allows: does clause 2 unpack clause 1?
6. Verify the survivor by re-reading the whole sentence with it installed.
Experts never decide by pause-feel; every choice reduces to count + lookup.

## Misconceptions
This hub's misconceptions are mostly inherited — the diagnostic value is in
SPLITTING them correctly:

- **cant-count-clauses** — The upstream failure class: the student's parse is
  broken (misses a verb, counts an -ing form as a verb, doesn't demote a
  subordinated clause, loses the subject in a preposition chunk). Any menu
  knowledge is useless on top of a wrong count. Root causes live in
  [[sentence-vs-fragment]] and [[boundaries-word-roles]].
- **counts-but-illegal-mark** — The downstream failure class: the parse is
  right, but the student's mark-to-count table is corrupt (semicolon before a
  fragment, comma between two sentences, colon after an incomplete launch).
  Root causes live in [[three-legal-joins]], [[comma-splice]],
  [[sentence-fragment-connectors]].
- **punctuate-by-sound** — The root misconception beneath both classes when the
  student doesn't run the procedure at all: mark placement by pause-feel.
  Treated canonically in [[punctuating-by-ear]]; every remediation here begins
  by replacing ear with count.
- **local-window-judgment** — Judging the blank from the few words around it,
  because reading the whole sentence feels slow. The hinge-phrase items are
  engineered so the local read endorses the wrong option. Treated in depth in
  [[window-widening]].

## Diagnostic indicators
The tutor's first job on any Boundaries miss is to classify it into one of the
two failure classes — they need different remediation:

- **cant-count-clauses signals:** the student misclassifies a SIDE when asked
  ("is what follows the blank a complete sentence?" → wrong answer); accepts
  fragments in one-word-repair drills; misses items whose trap is a
  feels-wrong-but-complete clause (pronoun subject after the blank); their
  wrong picks scatter across mark types with no pattern. When explaining, they
  describe meaning, not structure.
- **counts-but-illegal-mark signals:** the student states the sides correctly
  when asked, then still picks a semicolon before a fragment or a comma between
  two sentences; wrong picks CLUSTER on a specific mark (all semicolon misuse,
  or all splices); repairs fragments fine but fixes splices with the wrong mark.
- **punctuate-by-sound signals:** justifications mention pause, breath, flow,
  or "it reads better"; see [[punctuating-by-ear]].
- **local-window-judgment signals:** fast answers on long sentences; misses
  concentrate on items where the decisive connector or clause boundary sits far
  from the blank.

## Remediation pathways
- **cant-count-clauses:** drop back to [[sentence-vs-fragment]] slot-checking;
  do NOT reteach punctuation rules — they aren't the problem. Rebuild the parse,
  then re-run the same missed items.
- **counts-but-illegal-mark:** keep the student at this node's step 5; drill the
  menu as a lookup table with the count given for free ("two independents —
  list every legal mark"), then reintroduce counting. Target the specific
  corrupt row ([[three-legal-joins]] or [[sentence-fragment-connectors]]).
- **punctuate-by-sound:** run the ear-vs-count confrontation in
  [[punctuating-by-ear]] before anything else.
- **local-window-judgment:** enforced full-sentence reads with the blank masked
  until the count is stated; see [[window-widening]].

## Mastery criteria
Given any boundary item, the student can (a) state the clause count on each side
of the slot BEFORE looking at choices, (b) name the legal mark family for that
count, and (c) justify the answer in structural language ("independent +
fragment, so period dies") — consistently at medium difficulty and ≥75% at hard.
The count-stated-first behavior is the mastery tell, not just the right answer.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy:** two short clauses, seam at the blank, obvious verbs; the count is
  nearly given away.
- **Medium:** one side's completeness is disguised (pronoun subject, quantity
  word); a dependent clause is dressed as independent; connective adverb bait.
- **Hard:** the count itself is hidden — an interrupter buried between subject
  and blank ([[delete-and-reread]] needed to strip it), a connector placed far
  from the blank ([[window-widening]]), or a hinge phrase whose ownership
  decides the seam location. Hard items compose three or more nodes; the
  procedure is what keeps the composition tractable.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling:** teach the procedure as a physical checklist they literally
  tick through, with the count spoken aloud. One skill per session: sessions on
  counting only (no choices shown), then menu only (count given), then both.
- **Average:** the key upgrade is making the routine EXPLICIT — most students
  who "sort of get it" run a vibes version. Have them verbalize count-then-menu
  on every item for a week; speed comes from routine, not shortcuts.
- **Advanced:** teach the choice-set cross-check (step 6) as a first-class move;
  introduce items where equivalence cancellation solves the item faster than
  parsing ([[boundary-equivalence-cancellation]]).
- **1500+:** train the procedure to survive maximum camouflage: long
  interrupters, three-sentence transition logic, hinge phrases. The residual
  errors at this level are almost never menu errors — they're window errors, so
  pair with [[window-widening]] drills under time pressure.
