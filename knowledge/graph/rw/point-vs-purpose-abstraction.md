# Point vs. purpose — the claim being made vs. the act being performed

## What it is
Two distinct layers that whole-passage questions can target. The **point** is the argument — a claim you could dispute ("urban beekeeping harms wild pollinators"). The **purpose** is the rhetorical act performed — an infinitive verb plus a generalized object ("to challenge a common assumption about a conservation practice"). Purpose answers re-express the point one abstraction level up: specific nouns map to general categories, and the passage's dominant move becomes a verb. Includes the working vocabulary this mapping requires — the rhetorical verbs answer choices are built from (qualify, concede, bolster, undermine, evoke, characterize, minimize) — since a student who can't parse the choices can't match anything to them.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Primary-purpose stems are a fixture of Text Structure & Purpose. They probe two abilities at once: compressing a passage to its point (shared with main-idea work) and *re-describing that point as an act at the right altitude*. No prior schooling separates argument from rhetorical act, so the layer split itself is the tested skill. Useful structural prior: the opening sentence usually telegraphs whole-passage purpose, especially in short passages — trust it, then verify with a skim rather than a reread (unverified-claim on reliability; gate through bank stats before the tutor asserts it).

## Mental model
**Zoom out one click, then verb it.** Take the point (a claim), replace its specific nouns with their categories (a beekeeping study → "recent research"; Dickinson's dashes → "a poet's stylistic choice"), and name what the passage *does* with that claim (explain, challenge, illustrate, reconcile). The credited purpose answer will be recognizably your point wearing general clothes and led by an infinitive verb. If a choice's altitude is wrong — a subordinate detail's job dressed as the whole passage's purpose, or vice versa — it is wrong no matter how accurate its content.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Derive the main point first, as a disputable claim in your own words ([[topic-point-attitude-reading]]). Purpose cannot be computed before point.
2. Check the first sentence against your point — it usually frames the purpose; confirm with a skim of paragraph heads rather than a reread.
3. Lift one abstraction level: generalize the specific nouns; select the verb that names the passage's dominant move. Match the verb's *direction and strength* to the passage's earned tone ([[tone-attitude-reading]]).
4. Screen choices: eliminate extreme verbs the prose doesn't earn (prove/demolish/ridicule class), then wrong-tone verbs ([[polarity-first-elimination]]).
5. Altitude-check the survivors: does this choice describe the WHOLE passage's act, or one paragraph's? A choice can be true and still be at the wrong grain.
6. Verify the object, not just the verb: "to challenge *a widely held view about X*" fails if the passage challenges a *new proposal* — one wrong noun kills the choice.

## Misconceptions
- **purpose-equals-restated-point** — The student hunts for their main-point sentence verbatim among the choices and panics when nothing matches, because purpose choices are cast as rhetorical verbs plus generalized objects. Root cause: no schooling distinguishes argument from act. Produces either random picks (nothing matched) or attraction to the choice sharing the most surface words with the passage.
- **wrong-altitude** — Describing a real but subordinate detail's job as the passage's purpose, or inflating the whole purpose onto a local question. Forms because salience (the most memorable paragraph) masquerades as centrality. Produces picks that are accurate about the wrong grain — the hardest distractor species here because nothing in the choice is false.
- **extreme-verb-attraction** — Picking "proves/demolishes/mocks" choices because the passage argues *for* something, so strong verbs feel loyal to it. Forms from equating conviction with strength. Fails because evidence-weighing prose qualifies rather than proves; the earned verb is usually a describe/suggest/question-class verb.
- **verb-lexicon-gap** — The student cannot parse the choices at all: "qualify" read as "be eligible," "concede" unknown, "undermine" vaguely negative. Not a reasoning failure — a vocabulary failure that masquerades as one, producing seemingly random errors on otherwise easy purpose items.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **purpose-equals-restated-point**: reports "none of the choices said what the passage said"; picks the choice with maximum word overlap; strong on point questions, weak on purpose questions — the split is the signature.
- **wrong-altitude**: picks choices describing one paragraph's job on whole-passage stems (or the reverse); explanation defends the content's accuracy without addressing scope.
- **extreme-verb-attraction**: systematic preference for the strongest verb among choices; misses concede/qualify answers as "too weak."
- **verb-lexicon-gap**: asks what choice-words mean; errors uniform across difficulty; hesitation localized to reading the choices rather than the passage.

## Remediation pathways
- **purpose-equals-restated-point**: teach the two-step transform explicitly — write the point, then rewrite it as "to [verb] [generalized object]." Drill the noun-generalization move alone (specific → category) until automatic. If the point itself is wrong, drop back to [[topic-point-attitude-reading]].
- **wrong-altitude**: the coverage test — "does this choice account for EVERY paragraph, or just the vivid one?" Have the student label each paragraph's job, then check the candidate purpose against all labels.
- **extreme-verb-attraction**: verb-ladder calibration (notes < suggests < argues < proves); rule: the verb must be *earned by the hedging level of the prose*, not by the student's sympathy with the argument. Revisit [[tone-attitude-reading]] if degree-reading itself is the gap.
- **verb-lexicon-gap**: direct instruction — the rhetorical verb set is finite and teachable as vocabulary with polarity and strength tags (qualify = limit a claim, mildly negative-toward-scope; bolster = strengthen, positive). Fastest fix in this node; do it first when detected.

## Mastery criteria
Given a passage, the student can produce point AND purpose as separate sentences (claim vs. infinitive act), generalize nouns to the right altitude, select verbs matching earned tone and strength, and reject true-but-wrong-grain choices — proven by parallel accuracy on point-style and purpose-style stems over the same passages at hard difficulty.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Medium (entry)**: short passage, purpose telegraphed by the opening sentence; choices differ in verb direction (challenge vs. explain) so tone screening nearly decides it.
- **Hard**: mixed-move passages (describe then question) where the dominant act must be weighed; concession-heavy prose where the earned verb is "qualify"; wrong-altitude distractors accurate about a subordinate section; abstract objects two levels up requiring careful noun-mapping; literary passages where the "argument" is an effect, not a claim. Composes with [[cross-text-relationship-taxonomy]] (each text's purpose feeds the relationship call) and [[rhetorical-role-identification]] (local jobs must sum consistently with the global act).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach the rhetorical verb lexicon FIRST — it unblocks everything and feels like winnable vocabulary work. Then the point/purpose split with everyday acts ("what is a complaint DOING vs. SAYING?").
- **Average**: the two-step transform (point → generalized infinitive) as a written ritual; verb ladders for degree; first-sentence-then-skim verification posture.
- **Advanced**: altitude discrimination drills (paragraph-job labeling vs. whole-passage act); mixed-move passages; strict one-wrong-noun verification.
- **1500+**: literary and period passages where purpose is aesthetic; adversarial sets where every choice passes tone screening and only altitude or object separates them; have the student write a wrong-altitude distractor for a passage they just solved.
