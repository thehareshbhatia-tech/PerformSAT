# Reading the choices first: diagnosing what a conventions item actually tests

## What it is
Every Standard English Conventions item uses the same stem prompt ("Which choice
completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?"),
so the prompt never tells you which rule is on trial. The four answer choices do.
The way the choices DIFFER from one another is a fingerprint of the tested
convention, and reading that fingerprint before reading the passage is the
top-level expert skill that every other conventions skill hangs off of.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The exam deliberately dresses one convention as another: choices that vary tense
when the real split is singular/plural, choices that look like style variants when
the real split is where a modifier's subject sits. A student who applies the wrong
rule can still land on a grammatical-sounding sentence — that's the trap design.
The College Board is effectively probing whether you can classify a problem before
solving it. Diagnosis failures explain a huge share of wrong answers by students
who "know all the rules": they solved the wrong problem correctly.

## Mental model
**The choices are the question.** Hold a small lookup table: *how the four choices
differ → what is being tested.*

| Choices differ in… | Tested convention |
|---|---|
| verb number (some -s forms, some bare) — with or without tense noise | subject-verb agreement ([[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]]) |
| verb time only, all same number | tense ([[verb-tense-timeline]]) |
| singular vs plural pronouns / pronoun vs named noun | pronoun agreement or clarity ([[pronoun-antecedent-agreement]], [[pronoun-clarity-and-specificity]]) |
| apostrophe placement / homophone spellings | possession grid ([[apostrophe-possession-grid]]) |
| the OPENING words of each choice, after an intro phrase | modifier adjacency ([[modifier-adjacency]]) |
| the grammatical shape of an item in a series or pair | parallel structure ([[parallel-structure]]) |
| who/whom/which/where/when | relative pronoun and case ([[relative-pronoun-and-case]]) |
| identical words, shuffled punctuation marks | boundaries (see [[sentence-vs-fragment]] cluster) |
| than/as/nor and second halves of paired words | word pairs and comparisons ([[comparisons-and-word-pairs]]) |

The second half of the model: **the axis the choices vary on that does NOT decide
the answer is decoration.** If choices split on number, tense variation is noise.

One calibration worth carrying for active-vs-passive splits: **passive voice is
not an error.** Never auto-eliminate a choice for being passive — a clean
passive that satisfies the tested rule can win. But between two otherwise-clean
choices, the active form is the expected key, and a wordy passive construction
is a distractor marker (unverified-claim on the skew — verify against bank
before the tutor uses it as more than a tiebreaker prior). Voice is almost
always a decoration axis; treat it as the deciding axis only when nothing else
separates the survivors.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Read the four choices before reading the passage sentence. Ask: what is the
   minimal difference between them? (Same words, different marks? Same marks,
   different verb forms? Different opening nouns?)
2. Classify the item using the fingerprint table. If two axes vary at once (e.g.,
   number AND tense), assume the harder-to-hear axis (number) is the real one and
   treat the audible axis as bait — confirm from the passage.
3. Only now read the passage — and read the amount the diagnosis demands: modifier
   and apostrophe items are sentence-local; tense and generic-pronoun items need
   the neighboring sentences; parallelism needs the fixed anchor outside the blank.
4. Apply the one rule the diagnosis selected. Deliberately refuse to evaluate the
   choices on any other axis ("this one sounds more formal" is never the rule).
5. Plug the survivor back in and reread the whole sentence from its start as the
   final check.

## Misconceptions
- **solve-by-sound** — the student reads each choice into the sentence and picks
  what sounds smoothest. Forms because spoken English tolerates agreement slips and
  loose reference, so the ear is calibrated to the wrong standard; also because
  no one ever taught them the choice set carries information. Produces picks on the
  decoration axis (a tense change that "fixes" a number error). The test builds at
  least one euphonious wrong choice per item to feed this.
- **prompt-tells-me-the-topic** — the student expects the question stem to say
  what's tested (as school quizzes do) and, finding it generic, starts solving with
  no hypothesis at all. Forms from worksheet-style practice where the rule was
  named in the page header. Produces slow, unfocused solving and time pressure
  errors late in the module.
- **one-axis-blindness** — having spotted one difference among choices (say,
  apostrophes), the student stops scanning and misses that number also varies.
  Forms because scanning feels done once a pattern is found. Combined items
  (number × apostrophe, form × separator) punish it: the student gets the
  apostrophe right and the number wrong.
- **style-preference-solving** — the student treats grammatically-clean choices as
  a style menu and picks the most sophisticated one. Forms from essay feedback that
  rewards elevated diction. Produces "longest/fanciest" picks; conventions answers
  are usually the plainest form that satisfies the one tested rule.

## Diagnostic indicators
- Picks a wrong-number verb but explains their choice with tense reasoning →
  **solve-by-sound** / fell for the number-flip-with-tense-noise design (route to
  [[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]]).
- Long time-on-task with no elimination pattern; explanation starts "I read the
  passage and…" rather than "the choices differ in…" → **prompt-tells-me-the-topic**.
- Gets single-rule items right but combined sets wrong, with the miss always on the
  second axis → **one-axis-blindness**.
- Consistently picks the longest or most formal grammatical choice → 
  **style-preference-solving**.
- Explains a correct answer with an incorrect rule ("comma because you pause") →
  diagnosis skill absent even when the answer was lucky; treat as not-mastered.

## Remediation pathways
- **solve-by-sound**: contrast pair — one item where the smooth-sounding choice is
  wrong on number; have the student find the axis in the choices FIRST, out loud.
  If it doesn't land, drill the fingerprint table alone (choices shown, passage
  hidden, task = name the convention).
- **prompt-tells-me-the-topic**: teach the two-step ritual explicitly ("choices
  first, name the test, then read"). Timed micro-drills of classification-only.
- **one-axis-blindness**: give choice sets and ask "list EVERY way these four
  differ" before allowing an answer. Revisit [[apostrophe-possession-grid]]'s
  decide-number-first ordering as the model for two-axis items.
- **style-preference-solving**: show three past picks where the plain choice won;
  name the rule "the SAT rewards the minimal correct form, never the fancy one."

## Mastery criteria
Given only a choice set (passage hidden), names the tested convention correctly on
~9 of 10 mixed items within seconds each, including two-axis sets where they name
both axes and say which one will decide. On full items, their first verbalized move
is a classification, and accuracy on mixed conventions sets matches their accuracy
on same-skill blocked drills (the gap between blocked and mixed performance is the
direct measure of this skill).

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: one axis varies, category-mismatch distractors; classification is
  nearly automatic.
- **medium**: one real axis plus one noise axis (number + tense; form + voice);
  student must pick the deciding axis.
- **hard**: two live axes that must BOTH be satisfied (number × apostrophe form,
  parallel form × separator consistency), or a convention disguised as a different
  one (agreement posing as tense, modifier item posing as a style rewrite).
  Hard items compose this node with [[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]],
  [[apostrophe-possession-grid]], or [[parallel-structure]].

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: don't teach the table abstractly. Run many rounds of "here are
  four choices, what's different?" with no passage. The insight "the differences
  are a message" is the whole lesson; rules come later.
- **Average**: teach the fingerprint table plus the decoration principle (the
  varying axis that doesn't decide). Practice on number-plus-tense sets where they
  must say "tense is noise here" before answering.
- **Advanced**: focus on two-axis items and disguises. Have them author a
  distractor set for a given rule — designing the noise axis teaches them to see it.
- **1500+**: the marginal errors here are speed and the rare mis-classification on
  disguised items. Drill classification under time pressure and the specific
  high-scorer trap: clean-but-dangling choices that reward whole-sentence
  re-attachment habits (see [[modifier-adjacency]]).
