# Licensed inference moves and where inference must stop

## What it is
A short list of logical moves that a text *actually licenses* — and, just as important, the boundary line beyond which no conclusion is permitted no matter how reasonable it sounds. The licensed moves are mostly complements and negation handling: what a superlative, a restriction, an emphasis, or a percentage says about everything it *excludes*, and how negations flip or cancel. The boundaries mark what stated facts can never yield: motives, recommendations, superiority claims, or anything outside the stated population and time window.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
SAT "inference" is entailment, not interpretation. The test manufactures wrong answers precisely at the boundary line — statements one comfortable step *past* what the text establishes — because students trained on open-ended classroom inference habitually take that step. The licensed-moves list exists because correct answers are routinely disguised as the logical complement of a stated fact rather than a restatement of it.

## Mental model
**Inference is arithmetic on statements, with a hard fence.** Inside the fence, a few operations are always valid: "the most effective X" *entails* every other X is less effective; "only when A" *entails* not when not-A; "focuses largely on A" *entails* less focus elsewhere; "14% are X" *entails* 86% are not; "not impossible" *resolves to* possible; "older than" can reappear as "not younger than." Outside the fence live all the things a text never proves by describing: that a used method is the best or only method (usage ≠ effectiveness), why anyone did anything, what *should* be done, and anything about other times, places, or groups.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Identify the operative statement (usually the sentence at or feeding the blank/claim) and check it for operators: superlatives, *only/largely/mostly*, percentages, comparatives, negations.
2. Apply at most one or two licensed moves to generate the entailed statement. If the chain needs three or more creative steps, you've left the fence.
3. On any negation — especially doubles — slow down deliberately and resolve to the positive form before comparing to choices ("not unlikely" → likely).
4. Run the boundary checklist against tempting choices: Does it claim best/only from mere usage? Does it assign a motive? Does it say *should*? Does it exit the stated time window, place, or population? Any yes → eliminate.
5. Expect the correct answer to feel small, even boring. A conclusion that feels insightful is usually outside the fence.

## Misconceptions
- **inference-means-leap** — Students believe a good inference adds something the author didn't say, because English classes reward reading between the lines. They reject the small entailed answer as "too obvious" and pick the imaginative one. Trap design: a speculative, could-be-true extension (motives, future behavior, funding, applications) placed next to the boring correct complement.
- **usage-proves-merit** — From "researchers used method M," students conclude M is the best or only way. Formed by a charitable-reading habit: if the author chose to mention it, it must be special. The test writes distractors asserting superiority or exclusivity of a merely-described method.
- **descriptive-to-normative-slide** — Students accept a *should*-flavored conclusion after a purely descriptive passage, importing an implied recommendation. Formed because school reading often asks "what is the author suggesting we do?" even of neutral texts.
- **negation-fumbling** — Under time pressure, students process "not un-X," "fails to reject," or a negated comparative as its opposite, or drop one negation in a stack of two. This is a working-memory failure, not a knowledge gap; it produces exactly-backwards answers. Trap design at the hard end reportedly leans on double negatives (unverified-claim — verify frequency against our bank before the tutor asserts it).
- **scope-drift** — Students let a claim about one population/period silently generalize ("teens in the 1990s study" becomes "people"), then accept choices at the wider scope. Formed by summary habits that strip qualifiers (shares a root with *qualifier-dropping* in [[argument-spine-decomposition]]).

## Diagnostic indicators
- *inference-means-leap*: student rejects the correct answer as "too obvious"; picks the speculation distractor; explanation includes "you can kind of assume that..."
- *usage-proves-merit*: picks distractors containing *best, only, most effective* about a described method; when asked, says "well, they used it, so it must work best."
- *descriptive-to-normative-slide*: picks the *should/ought/needs to* distractor after a descriptive passage.
- *negation-fumbling*: wrong answers are direction-inverted versions of the right one; error rate spikes specifically on items with stacked or embedded negations; fast response times on those same items.
- *scope-drift*: picks the widened-scope distractor; restates the passage without its population/time qualifiers.

## Remediation pathways
- *inference-means-leap*: reframe the task — "state what the text forces, not what it inspires." Contrast pair: one classroom-style interpretive question vs. one SAT completion on the same paragraph, showing the different licensed answers. If it doesn't land, revisit [[premise-completion-vs-new-evidence]] (closed-room rule).
- *usage-proves-merit*: single counterexample kills it: "I wrote this note with a pen" — does that make pens the best writing tool? Then have the student generate the fenced version ("a pen *can* be used").
- *descriptive-to-normative-slide*: teach the is/ought flag: any *should* in a choice demands a *should* (or explicit evaluation) in the passage. Quick scan drill on 5 items.
- *negation-fumbling*: externalize — rewrite every negated statement in positive form on scratch paper before comparing. This is a procedure fix, not a concept fix; enforce the slowdown ritual.
- *scope-drift*: qualifier-underlining drill; then the tutor offers widened-scope choices and the student must name the exact qualifier each one violates.

## Mastery criteria
The student can (a) generate the entailed complement of superlative/restriction/emphasis/percentage statements on demand, (b) resolve double negatives correctly at speed, and (c) veto boundary-violating choices *by naming the violated rule* (motive, normative, scope, usage≠merit). Proof: hard-band completion items with speculation and inverted-direction distractors answered consistently, with correct verbal justifications.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: rarely tested in isolation at the easy band; single explicit statements, answer is near-restatement.
- **Medium**: one complement move required (percentage, *only*, superlative); one speculation distractor.
- **Hard**: stacked operators (a negated comparative inside a restriction), double negatives, boundary-violating distractors that echo the passage's vocabulary, and composition with [[logical-text-completion]] reference-chain backtracking or dense diction requiring [[evidence-forced-word-meaning]] decoding first.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: skip the abstract rules; teach through everyday statements ("only seniors may park here — so can juniors?"). Build the complement reflex on non-academic sentences before touching SAT passages.
- **Average**: give them the move list explicitly as a small toolkit and the fence as a four-item veto checklist. Their problem is usually not capacity but never having been told classroom inference is disallowed — say that directly.
- **Advanced**: drill negation stacks and scope precision under time; these are the residual errors. Introduce positive-form rewriting as a targeted ritual only for items flagged with negations.
- **1500+**: adversarial practice — have the student *write* boundary-violating distractors for a given passage. Authoring traps is the fastest route to never falling for them, and it exposes any remaining softness in the fence.
