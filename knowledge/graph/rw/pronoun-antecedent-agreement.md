# Pronoun-antecedent agreement: the referent must be on the page and set the number

## What it is
A pronoun inherits its number (and person/thing type) from the noun it stands
for — and that noun must literally appear in the text. A noun that is merely
implied by the passage's logic (the plural of a stated singular, the owner behind
a possessive) does not count as an antecedent. Choosing between it/its and
they/their is therefore a two-step act: locate the stated antecedent, then read
its number morphologically.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Conversational English resolves reference by meaning; edited prose resolves it by
text. The exam probes whether a reader tracks literal referents through dense
prose. Thing-antecedents (it/its vs they/their) dominate over person-antecedents
[unverified-claim: frequency skew from prep-source analysis; verify vs our bank]
because thing-number is less salient to the ear — "the data… it" and "the
data… they" both sound fine until you check the noun.

### Singular "they" — current-standard guidance (modernized)
Older prep materials taught that generic singular antecedents ("each student")
require "his or her," treating "their" as an error. That guidance is dated.
Singular "they" for a generic or unspecified person is accepted by every major
current style authority, and the digital SAT does not stage "their vs his-or-her"
showdowns: official pronoun-number items pivot on antecedents whose number is
structurally unambiguous — usually THINGS (a phenomenon vs phenomena, a species'
members) or quantifier heads whose number is fixed ("each of the…" → singular).
Teach students: never eliminate "they/their" on the theory that "they can't be
singular"; DO eliminate it when the stated antecedent is a grammatically singular
noun and a singular pronoun choice is offered, because number agreement — not
gender inclusivity — is what's being tested. [unverified-claim: "CB never makes
singular-they itself the decision point" — consistent with released digital items
we've reviewed, but keep verifying as new official material appears; the tutor
should present this as how the test behaves, not as a grammar verdict.]

## Mental model
**Point at the noun, read its ending.** Three questions in fixed order:
(1) What stated noun does this pronoun stand for? (2) Is that noun singular or
plural — checked morphologically (is there a plural marker?), never by feel?
(3) Does the candidate pronoun match in number and person/thing type? Any
mismatch eliminates. The referent usually sits earlier in the same sentence or in
the previous sentence — when a pronoun opens a sentence, back up one sentence to
find it. (Rarely, the referent follows the pronoun.)

A second, quieter axis: **generic-person consistency**. The generalized-person
pronouns (formal "one," collective "we," direct-address "you") are each fine in
isolation but cannot be swapped mid-passage; the correct choice is whichever the
surrounding sentences already committed to. This is a cross-sentence consistency
check, not a single-sentence grammar check.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Choice-set triage: choices mixing singular and plural pronouns (or pronouns vs
   a named noun) → stop and hunt the antecedent before evaluating anything.
   Grammatical plausibility of each choice in isolation is the trap.
2. Find the stated antecedent — same sentence first, then one sentence back.
   Reject implied antecedents: if the needed noun isn't printed, it can't govern.
3. Read number off the noun's form, applying [[grammatical-number-assignment]]
   for quantifier heads (each/every → singular), collectives, and gerunds.
4. Strip interrupters and accompaniment phrases before judging — the same
   camouflage used against verbs ([[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]]) is used
   against pronouns.
5. Check person/thing type (a thing-pronoun for a person or vice versa is its own
   error class), then check the passage's generic-person register if the choices
   are one/we/you forms.
6. If choices pair pronouns with possessive homophones (its/it's/their/they're),
   settle number FIRST — it kills half the set — then apostrophe form
   ([[apostrophe-possession-grid]]).

## Misconceptions
- **implied-antecedent-ok** — the student accepts a plural pronoun because the
  plural concept is obviously intended, though only a singular noun appears (or
  only a possessive form of it). Forms because speech resolves reference by
  meaning; the written standard feels pedantic. The trap: a perfectly grammatical
  plural pronoun sitting next to the correct singular one.
- **proximity-referent** — the pronoun is matched to the NEAREST noun rather than
  the logical referent. The same "trust the local surface" root as
  nearest-noun-is-subject in [[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]] and the
  boundaries cluster's comma-equals-pause habit. The test parks a wrong-number
  noun right before the blank to feed it.
- **semantic-number** — number is judged by feel ("the team… they") instead of by
  the noun's form. Overlaps [[grammatical-number-assignment]]'s
  feels-plural-is-plural; here it surfaces as pronoun picks.
- **register-blindness** — on generic-person items the student judges the
  sentence locally ("your results" sounds fine) without checking which generic
  pronoun the passage already uses. Forms because school grammar rarely tests
  cross-sentence consistency.
- **they-cant-be-singular** — the over-corrected inverse of dated instruction:
  the student reflexively eliminates "they/their" whenever the antecedent is one
  person, even when the antecedent is generic and "they" is the credited or
  uncontested choice. Forms from older prep books and strict schooling. See the
  modernized guidance above: eliminate on stated-noun number, never on the
  pronoun's theoretical capacity.

## Diagnostic indicators
- Picks the plural pronoun when only a singular noun (or its possessive) is on
  the page, and defends it with "but it obviously means all of them" →
  **implied-antecedent-ok**.
- Picks the pronoun matching the noun nearest the blank; miss rate rises when a
  wrong-number noun is adjacent → **proximity-referent** (the assessment-design
  proximity-referent distractor is built for this; log it as a referent-logic
  gap, not pronoun knowledge).
- Misses on collective/each/data-type antecedents while ordinary ones are fine →
  **semantic-number**; remediate in [[grammatical-number-assignment]].
- On one/we/you items, answers without looking at neighboring sentences →
  **register-blindness** (watch for fast wrong answers).
- Eliminates "their" first on generic-person items and explains "they is
  plural" → **they-cant-be-singular**.

## Remediation pathways
- **implied-antecedent-ok**: the point-at-it drill — the student must physically
  point at (or quote) the antecedent noun before answering; if they point at a
  word that isn't a noun on the page, the rule teaches itself. Contrast pair:
  same sentence with the antecedent pluralized, flipping the answer.
- **proximity-referent**: ask "who/what actually does or owns this?" before
  looking at nouns; then show the adjacent-noun bait explicitly so they recognize
  the plant. If it persists, drill subject-hunting in
  [[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]] — it's the same muscle.
- **semantic-number**: route to [[grammatical-number-assignment]] (bounded-unit
  model), then return with mixed pronoun items.
- **register-blindness**: rule of thumb — a one/we/you choice set is ALWAYS a
  read-the-neighbors item; drill with the deciding sentence highlighted, then
  unhighlighted.
- **they-cant-be-singular**: show the current-standard note plus two items where
  "their" is right; reframe the elimination rule as "match the stated noun's
  number," which handles every case without any belief about "they."

## Mastery criteria
Quotes the antecedent before choosing, every time; correctly rejects
implied-antecedent choices; handles thing-antecedents at the same accuracy as
person-antecedents (the gap between those is the telltale of ear-reliance); and
solves number-plus-homophone sets in the number-first order. Proof: hard-band
consistency on items where the antecedent is a quantifier head or sits one
sentence back.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: antecedent in the same sentence, ordinary nouns, choices split
  person/thing or singular/plural crudely.
- **medium**: antecedent one sentence back; collective or each/every heads;
  interrupters between antecedent and pronoun.
- **hard**: implied-antecedent traps (only the possessive or the singular is
  stated); generic-person consistency across paragraphs; pronoun number fused
  with apostrophe form in one choice set (composes
  [[apostrophe-possession-grid]]); intensive -self forms that must match in both
  number and type.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach only the three questions, in order, with pointing. Use
  person antecedents first (easier to hear), then reveal that thing-antecedents
  are the tested case and the same three questions still work.
- **Average**: emphasize the on-the-page rule and morphological number-checking;
  introduce the number-first ordering for homophone sets.
- **Advanced**: implied-antecedent traps and generic-person register; teach the
  back-up-one-sentence habit until it's automatic on sentence-opening pronouns.
- **1500+**: the residual errors are proximity plants in very dense prose and
  fused number × apostrophe sets under time pressure. Also brief them on the
  singular-they landscape explicitly — high scorers are the ones most likely to
  have internalized the dated rule and over-eliminate.
