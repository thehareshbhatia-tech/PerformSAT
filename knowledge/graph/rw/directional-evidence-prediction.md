# Predicting the direction of qualifying evidence before reading choices

## What it is
The technique of converting a claim into a directional relation between variables ("more X → more Y") and then specifying, *before reading any choices*, what direction of finding would support it and what direction would weaken it. Support = a finding pointing the same way as the claim. Weaken = a finding pointing the opposite way — **or showing no relationship at all**, or supplying a counterexample; undermining never requires proving the claim false, only being most inconsistent with it. You predict the *shape* of the evidence, not its content.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Support/weaken items live or die on polarity, and polarity is exactly what degrades under time pressure and negation. The College Board builds mirror-image distractors — the wrong-direction twin of the correct answer — into both support and weaken stems, so a student who verifies relevance but not direction fails symmetrically. Directional prediction is also the honest test of scientific reasoning: knowing what data *would* falsify a hypothesis is the skill, and the test asks it directly.

## Mental model
**Set the target before the arrows fly.** Write the claim as an arrow relation on scratch paper (shade↑ → beetles↓), then write the target next to it: "support = same arrows; weaken = flipped arrows OR flat line." Every choice is then a five-second comparison against the written target instead of a fresh act of reasoning. The physical act of writing the arrows is load-bearing: polarity errors are working-memory failures, and paper doesn't fumble negations.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Isolate the claim and check it's empirical (relates variables/conditions to outcomes). If it is, compress to arrow notation.
2. Read the stem's task verb and write the explicit target: same-direction finding (support) or opposite/null/counterexample (weaken).
3. Note the claim's *mechanism qualifiers* — which specific component, condition, or subgroup must carry the effect — and add them to the target line ([[support-and-weaken-textual]] treats this fully).
4. Sweep choices against the target, sorting fast: right direction / wrong direction / no bearing / right topic but wrong condition / right idea but a tail clause that breaks it.
5. Double-handle negations: a choice stating "did NOT decline" flips its apparent direction; resolve to positive form before comparing ([[entailment-moves-and-boundaries]]).
6. Remember the null: on weaken items, "no difference was found between groups" is a fully qualified answer even though it points nowhere.

## Misconceptions
- **direction-blindness** — Students verify a choice is relevant but never check polarity, picking a strengthener on a weaken stem or vice versa — especially when the claim or choice contains negation. Root cause: holding the relation in the head instead of on paper. Trap design: the mirror-image distractor, present on most support/weaken items (unverified-claim on frequency — verify against bank).
- **weaken-means-disprove** — Students believe undermining requires proof of falsity, so they reject counterexamples ("the protective trait was present, yet the harm occurred anyway") and null results as "not strong enough," hunting instead for an impossible knockout. Formed by everyday usage of "disprove." Produces picks of dramatic-sounding but claim-inert choices.
- **null-is-neutral** — The specific belief that a no-effect finding is irrelevant rather than damaging. A claim asserting a relationship is contradicted by evidence of no relationship; students miss this because "nothing happened" feels like an absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence.
- **stem-polarity-drift** — Midway through the choices, students forget whether the stem asked for support or weaken (especially after several same-direction distractors) and answer the opposite task. A pure attention failure amplified by not writing the target down.

## Diagnostic indicators
- *direction-blindness*: wrong answers are exact mirror images of correct ones; error rate roughly doubles on items containing negations; student's explanation establishes relevance and stops ("it's about the beetles and the shade, so...").
- *weaken-means-disprove*: on weaken items, student passes over the counterexample/null choice and picks something rhetorically aggressive; says "that doesn't prove it wrong."
- *null-is-neutral*: eliminates "no significant difference" choices early and specifically; can articulate support logic but not weaken logic when asked.
- *stem-polarity-drift*: misses cluster at the end of long items; student re-reads the stem after answering and self-corrects immediately when prompted — the knowledge was there, the anchor wasn't.

## Remediation pathways
- *direction-blindness*: mandate arrow notation for two weeks — claim arrows plus a written target line on every support/weaken item. The error is procedural; the fix is externalization. If arrows themselves confuse, revisit [[argument-spine-decomposition]] compression.
- *weaken-means-disprove*: reframe with the actual criterion — "most inconsistent with," not "fatal to." Contrast pair: a knockout refutation (never on the test) vs. a counterexample (the real answer shape); show both damage the claim, only one exists among the choices.
- *null-is-neutral*: one counterexample drill — claim "X causes Y," finding "X present, Y unchanged." Ask what a believer in the claim would predict, then what actually happened. The gap IS the weakening.
- *stem-polarity-drift*: written target line (same fix as direction-blindness) plus a re-read-the-stem checkpoint before final commitment.

## Mastery criteria
Given any empirical claim, the student produces the arrow form and both targets (supporting shape, weakening shape) in under 15 seconds, unprompted. Proof: consistent accuracy on hard weaken items whose correct answers are nulls or counterexamples, and near-zero mirror-image picks across a mixed support/weaken set with negation-bearing items.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: claim already stated as a clean relation; choices differ blatantly in direction; no negations.
- **Medium**: claim needs compression first; mirror-image distractor appears; one negation in claim or choices; weaken items introduce null-result answers.
- **Hard**: double negations across claim and choice; mechanism qualifiers make two same-direction choices differ only in *which component* carries the effect (composes with [[support-and-weaken-textual]]); quantitative variants require extracting direction from a chart before comparing ([[claim-anchored-data-reading]], [[chart-decoding-fundamentals]]).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach direction with physical intuition first — "the claim says more homework, better grades; which of these findings agrees, which argues back?" Verbal, no notation, everyday claims. Introduce arrows only after the agree/argue-back instinct exists.
- **Average**: install the written target ritual and the weaken-includes-null rule (the highest-yield single fact in this node — most have never heard it). Drill mixed stems so polarity stays active.
- **Advanced**: negation-loaded items and counterexample recognition; have them state, for each claim, exactly what a falsifying data point would look like before seeing choices.
- **1500+**: compress the ritual to mental notation, but keep the written target for negation-flagged items only — selective externalization is the expert behavior. Add speed pressure and quantitative hybrids where the direction must be read off a trend ([[chart-decoding-fundamentals]]).
