# Functional word roles — the five labels boundaries questions actually use

## What it is
The minimal grammar vocabulary a student needs before any punctuation work: verb,
noun, pronoun, preposition, and connector — each defined by a hands-on check the
student can run, not by a definition to recite. The goal is functional literacy
("can you find the working verb in this sentence?"), not grammar taxonomy. Every
label here exists only because a later boundaries decision consumes it.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The SAT never asks "identify the preposition." But every Boundaries item silently
assumes the student can (a) find the conjugated verb, (b) find its subject, (c)
recognize that a word like *however* is a different kind of connector from *but*.
Students who miss easy boundary items usually aren't missing a punctuation rule —
they're mis-tagging a word, which corrupts every rule downstream. This node is
pure infrastructure: fast to teach, and it makes every other node in the cluster
learnable.

## Mental model
**Words have jobs, not identities.** The same surface word can be a noun in one
sentence and a verb in the next; you classify by asking "what job is this word
doing HERE?" Five job checks cover the exam:

- **Verb (working form):** carries the sentence's time and matches a doer.
  *decides / decided / has decided* are working forms; *to decide* and *deciding*
  are not — they're verb-flavored, but they can't anchor a sentence.
- **Noun:** can follow *a/an/the*. Idea-words in academic prose (*erosion*,
  *investment*, *tendency*) count fully — abstract nouns are real nouns.
- **Pronoun:** a stand-in for a noun already in play (*it, they, this, many,
  most, some*). A pronoun subject is a full-strength subject.
- **Preposition:** a relation word (*of, in, with, despite, during*) always
  completed by a noun. A preposition+noun chunk is packaging — it can never
  contain the subject or the working verb.
- **Connector — the load-bearing three-way split:**
  1. *Coordinators* (and, but, or, so, yet, for, nor) can glue two clauses with
     a comma's help.
  2. *Subordinators* (because, although, while, since, unless, when, if...) demote
     the clause they open to dependent status.
  3. *Connective adverbs* (however, therefore, moreover, consequently, instead...)
     MEAN what coordinators mean but are grammatically just adverbs — they have
     zero gluing power. Meaning and grammar are different axes; this one split
     powers an entire comma-splice trap family.

Two verb-morphology facts round out the kit: third-person verbs mark number
backwards from nouns (-s on a verb = SINGULAR: *it runs / they run*), and the
irregulars *be/have* (is/are, was/were, has/have) hide the number cue, so their
forms must be automatic.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Enter every sentence verb-first: find each working (conjugated) verb.
2. For each verb, ask "who or what does this?" — that's its subject.
3. Mentally gray out preposition+noun chunks; the subject is never inside one.
4. When a connector appears, tag its class (coordinator / subordinator /
   connective adverb) before letting its meaning influence anything.
5. When unsure of a word's job, run a substitution check (*to ___* for verbs,
   *a/the ___* for nouns) instead of reaching for terminology.

## Misconceptions
- **verb-s-means-plural** — Students generalize noun morphology (-s = plural) to
  verbs, where it is inverted. Forms because school drills plurals on nouns only.
  Produces agreement misses on it/they contrasts and makes students mis-identify
  which word is the verb at all.
- **abstract-nouns-arent-nouns** — School examples of nouns are concrete (dog,
  city), so *formation* or *scarcity* doesn't register as a noun; the student then
  can't locate subjects in academic prose and concludes the sentence "has no
  subject" — a false fragment call.
- **transition-equals-conjunction** — Because *however* means *but*, students
  assume it behaves like *but*. Meaning-similarity masks the word-class
  difference. This is the seed misconception behind the ", however," splice
  family treated in [[comma-splice]] and [[transition-backward-anchoring]].
- **verby-look-equals-verb** — -ing forms and to-forms look maximally verb-like,
  so students count them as working verbs. Fully treated in
  [[sentence-vs-fragment]]; planted here as a word-role error.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **verb-s-means-plural:** student pairs *they* with *runs* or calls *runs*
  "plural" when explaining; misses cluster on it/they and be/have items.
- **abstract-nouns-arent-nouns:** on subject-finding, the student skips the
  abstract head noun and grabs a concrete noun inside a preposition chunk
  ("the rate of the glaciers" → picks *glaciers*); wrong answers follow the
  chunk-noun's number.
- **transition-equals-conjunction:** student picks comma + *however* where
  comma + *but* would be right, and defends it as "they mean the same thing."
  The tell is a meaning-based explanation of a structure question.

## Remediation pathways
- **verb-s-means-plural:** contrast pair drill — *the engine runs / the engines
  run* — then state the inversion explicitly: "on verbs, -s is the singular
  uniform." Two minutes, high yield.
- **abstract-nouns-arent-nouns:** the *a/the* test on ten abstract nouns from
  science prose; then subject-finding with preposition chunks physically crossed
  out. If it doesn't land, the student needs the chunk-graying habit before the
  noun work.
- **transition-equals-conjunction:** side-by-side minimal pair — *The trial
  failed, but the team persisted* (legal) vs *The trial failed, however the team
  persisted* (splice) — and name the principle: meaning is what a word says;
  class is what a word is allowed to do. Reinforce at [[comma-splice]].

## Mastery criteria
Given any exam-register sentence, the student can underline every working verb,
name each verb's subject, and tag any connector's class — in under 20 seconds,
without terminology beyond the five labels. Proof: consistent verb/subject
identification embedded in easy-medium boundary items, not on a labeling quiz.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy:** short sentences, concrete subjects, verb adjacent to subject.
- **Medium:** abstract subjects, preposition chunks between subject and verb,
  a connective adverb present as bait.
- **Hard:** this node stops being tested alone; it composes into
  [[clause-counting]] (multi-verb sentences) and [[essential-vs-nonessential]]
  (tagging the word a comma touches). Hard items assume this skill is free.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling:** teach only verb + subject first; add connectors later. Use the
  substitution checks as physical rituals. Never introduce the word "conjugated" —
  say "working verb" (a form that could carry a time stamp).
- **Average:** the three-way connector split is the highest-value ten minutes.
  Drill sorting 15 connectors into the three bins until instant.
- **Advanced:** frame word roles as slots ("this sentence has a verb slot, filled
  or empty?") — sets up fragment detection as slot-checking.
- **1500+:** only the connective-adverb/conjunction split and the be/have
  irregulars remain error-prone; verify those and move on. Show them how a
  hard item hides the connector class inside an interrupted sentence.
