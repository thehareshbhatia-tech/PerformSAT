# Decompose the goal into a required-ingredients checklist

## What it is
The parsing discipline that makes goal-first solving reliable: split the goal sentence into its **verb** (present / introduce / emphasize / specify / compare …), its **focus phrase** (the exact thing to be presented/emphasized), and any **qualifiers** (an audience — "for readers unfamiliar with X"; a relation — "similarity between A and B"). A multi-part goal becomes a checklist, and the correct answer must satisfy **every** component. Satisfying one component while missing another is the anatomy of the classic near-miss distractor.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Difficulty in notes questions is manufactured almost entirely here: easy items have one-component goals; hard items stack components and then supply distractors that each satisfy a proper subset. The type is testing precise reading of a task specification — the same competence as parsing what a word problem actually asks for ([[stem-family-recognition]] is the cross-section sibling).

## Mental model
The goal is a **recipe with mandatory ingredients**. Verb = the cooking method, focus = the main ingredient, qualifiers = dietary constraints. A dish that nails the main ingredient but ignores the constraint is rejected the same as one with the wrong ingredient. Two sub-rules govern the matching:
- **Category precision**: the focus must be matched at the *correct category level*, not by topical association. A goal about physical signalling is not satisfied by a choice about sound-based signalling; "signals in general" is weaker than "the specific body part used." Adjacent categories and supersets are wrong.
- **First-match stop**: the design guarantees exactly one choice satisfies the full checklist, so once a choice checks every box, commit — at most a quick sweep to confirm no second candidate.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Read the goal sentence; mark the verb, the focus phrase, and every qualifier.
2. Count the components. Two or more → write (mentally) an explicit checklist.
3. Fix the category level of the focus: what would count as *exactly* this, what would be a neighbor, what would be a superset?
4. Scan choices; eliminate any that fail a component outright (fastest kills first — a missing comparison word, an absent audience accommodation).
5. On the surviving candidate(s), verify component-by-component. Reject partial satisfiers no matter how complete they feel.
6. Commit at first full match.

## Misconceptions
- **topic-match-equals-goal-match** — the student accepts a choice because it discusses the right subject, never checking that it performs the right rhetorical job. Rooted in keyword-matching strategies learned for reading comprehension, where topical overlap usually does indicate the answer. This is the misconception **the majority of notes-question distractors are built on**: right-topic-wrong-function choices exist precisely to reward it.
- **adjacent-category-substitution** — the student lets a semantically related category satisfy the focus (sound for movement, a work's awards for its materials). Forms from loose paraphrase habits — school rewards recognizing "close enough" restatements, so nearness reads as match. Produces misses on category-precision items, where the test plants a near-category choice that feels like a paraphrase.
- **partial-checklist-commit** — on multi-component goals, the student stops verifying after the first satisfied component (usually the focus) and commits. Forms because single-component goals dominate easy practice, training a one-check habit; also a time-pressure adaptation. Produces the classic near-miss: right focus, missing audience/relation component.
- **superset-acceptance** — the student treats a broader statement as covering the specific ask ("it mentions signals, and body-part signalling is a signal"). Forms from the logic of school summarizing, where general statements subsume specifics. The test exploits it with dignified-sounding general choices; specific goal → specific answer.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **topic-match-equals-goal-match**: misses land on the informative-but-wrong-job distractor; the student's defense is subject-based ("it's about the sculpture") with no mention of the goal verb. Fast wrong answers.
- **adjacent-category-substitution**: misses land on the near-category choice; asked to point at the goal's focus phrase, the student paraphrases it a level too loosely.
- **partial-checklist-commit**: accuracy is fine on single-component goals and drops sharply on two-plus-component goals — the cleanest difficulty-stratified signature in this skill. The picked distractor satisfies the focus but not the qualifier.
- **superset-acceptance**: misses land on the most general choice; the student argues inclusion ("that covers it").

## Remediation pathways
- **topic-match-equals-goal-match**: contrast pair — two choices on the identical subject, one informing and one comparing, under a comparison goal. Ask "which one *does what the goal says*?" The verb-as-criterion insight usually lands in one exposure; reinforce by requiring the student to read the goal verb aloud before choices.
- **adjacent-category-substitution**: category-ladder drill — given a focus phrase, have the student generate the exact match, a neighbor, and a superset, then classify choices into those bins. Builds the precision reflex directly.
- **partial-checklist-commit**: force externalization — the student must state the checklist (all components) before scanning, and tick each on the chosen answer. If it doesn't stick, cap practice to multi-component items until the one-check habit is extinct.
- **superset-acceptance**: counterexample — a goal asking for a specific mechanism next to a lofty general choice and a plain specific one; show the general choice fails the "could this sentence only have been written for this goal?" test. Revisit [[notes-question-goal-first]] if the student is still choice-anchored.

## Mastery criteria
The student can recite a goal's full component list from one read, classify each choice as full-match / partial-match / off-focus with the failing component named, and their accuracy on multi-component goals equals their single-component accuracy. That parity is the mastery signal; the gap is the diagnostic.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: one component, choices differ by focus; checklist is trivial.
- **medium**: two components (focus + relation, or focus + audience); one distractor per missing component.
- **hard**: stacked qualifiers plus category-precision pressure — the near-category and superset choices appear alongside partial satisfiers, so the item punishes both loose matching and incomplete verification. Composes with [[goal-answer-signatures]]: at the hard end, signature prediction is what keeps the checklist fast enough.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: teach verb + focus only; have them underline both on every item before anything else. Multi-component goals wait.
- **Average**: full checklist discipline with explicit component counting; introduce the category ladder (exact / neighbor / superset) as a named tool.
- **Advanced**: hard sets where every distractor is a designed partial match; require the failing component to be named on every elimination — elimination-by-reason, not by vibe.
- **1500+**: speed-harden the checklist: component extraction in one read, kills ordered by cheapest check first. The residual miss at this level is almost always a rushed category call on an adjacent-category distractor — drill precisely that.
