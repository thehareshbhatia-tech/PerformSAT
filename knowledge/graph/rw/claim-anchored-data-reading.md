# Claim-anchored data reading: the graphic serves the claim, not the reverse

## What it is
The strategy layer for quantitative Command of Evidence: a passage plus a table or graph, with a stem asking which choice completes the text, supports a claim, or weakens a conclusion using the data. The governing principle: **anchor on the claim first and read the display last.** Convert the claim into a checklist of 2–3 explicit testable conditions ("recovered after a dip" = goes down then up; "far lower" = a large gap; "the same species" = one entity, internal contrast), then audit choices against ALL conditions — visiting the graphic only for what the checklist demands. Correctness requires accuracy AND alignment: a choice can describe the display perfectly and still be wrong because it addresses the wrong aspect of the claim.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
These items test whether a student can use data *as evidence for something* rather than merely read it. The design inverts the natural attack order: students want to study the graphic first, but the display is deliberately overbuilt — extra series, inert jargon, irrelevant regions — around a claim that needs only a slice. Distractors that are graph-accurate but claim-irrelevant are a standard design, sometimes several per item (unverified-claim — verify against official items and our bank), which makes graphic-first verification a strategy that reliably selects wrong answers. These items reuse the completion and support/weaken skeletons with a display as the evidence source (unverified-claim on how uniformly true this is).

## Mental model
**The claim writes a shopping list; the graphic is the store.** You never wander the store — you walk in with the list (the claim's 2–3 conditions), pick up exactly those items, and leave. Choices are judged against the list, not against the store: "available in the store" (true of the graph) is worthless if it isn't "on the list" (required by the claim). And if exactly one choice satisfies the whole list, it's correct even before graph verification — re-checking is fine; re-litigating eliminated choices because they "also match the graph" is the designed trap.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Read the passage's final sentence(s) and the stem first; extract the claim with its qualifiers — magnitude words, time windows, the grammatical subject, emphasis verbs ([[argument-spine-decomposition]]).
2. Decompose the claim into a written condition checklist BEFORE looking at any numbers.
3. Triage by choice shape: long, information-dense verbal choices → try to resolve on wording alone (eliminate wrong-entity-count, wrong-magnitude, wrong-direction, off-focus options); short label-only choices (a bare region, category, or year) → the graphic is genuinely required (unverified-claim that verbosity inversely predicts graphic-necessity — treat as a heuristic with exceptions, not a law; some verbose-choice items still need the display to split two survivors).
4. In the graphic, run the [[chart-decoding-fundamentals]] TALK scan, then look ONLY for checklist features; deliberately ignore other series and opaque jargon — technical terms are inert tokens you never need to understand.
5. Audit each surviving choice against EVERY condition: magnitude ("far lower" needs a genuinely large gap; "roughly equal" needs near-identical values), entity count (a claim about ONE subject's internal contrast disqualifies any two-subject comparison structurally, regardless of numbers), window (right comparison in the time slice the transition demands), and direction.
6. For completion hybrids: an illustration transition before the blank means the governing idea is the sentence BEFORE the blank; a contrast transition means the blank states the opposite pole ([[transition-relationship-families]]).
7. For weaken variants: find the inference gap in the passage's conclusion — usually a category-level claim drawn from its most prominent member — and hunt the display for the ignored counterexample (a second member behaving oppositely).
8. One survivor → commit. Do not reopen.

## Misconceptions
- **graph-match-equals-correct** — the quantitative mask of the root error; treated fully in [[topical-vs-logical-relevance]]. Here: "true of the display" taken as sufficient, producing first-accurate-choice picks.
- **must-understand-everything-first** — Students believe the display must be fully digested before answering; they decode every series and term, wasting minutes and — worse — unmooring attention from the claim so that all accurate-looking choices feel equally viable. Formed by school science norms that reward exhaustive decoding. Also feeds **complexity-equals-difficulty** panic: visual busyness misread as item difficulty, causing skips of easy items.
- **any-gap-counts** — When the claim needs a *dramatic* difference, students accept any nonzero gap in the right direction. The test plants a distractor citing a genuine but modest gap beside the correct large-gap choice; students who check direction but not magnitude take the bait.
- **entity-count-blindness** — Students skim the claim's grammatical subject and accept choices comparing two different subjects when the claim describes one subject's internal contrast (same drug at two doses, same month in two conditions). The two-entity distractor carries plausible numbers precisely so only the structure disqualifies it.
- **residual-second-guessing** — After a clean elimination, students reopen the item because the graphic "has more in it," distrusting a verbal solution to a visual-looking question. It converts right answers into wrong ones at the last moment.

## Diagnostic indicators
- *graph-match-equals-correct*: student verifies choices against the display in order and stops at the first accurate one; justification = "I checked it, it's true."
- *must-understand-everything-first*: long time-to-first-elimination; think-aloud starts with the display, not the stem; visible distress on jargon-heavy items but fine performance on plain ones.
- *any-gap-counts*: wrong picks cite small true gaps on magnitude-worded claims; student never mentions the size requirement when explaining.
- *entity-count-blindness*: wrong picks compare across entities on internal-contrast claims; asked to restate the claim, student drops "the same X."
- *residual-second-guessing*: answer changes on review from correct to incorrect; hesitation after a complete elimination; "but the graph also shows..." phrasing.

## Remediation pathways
- *graph-match-equals-correct*: demonstrate one item where display-first verification provably selects a wrong answer, then rebuild with claim-first order. Route to [[topical-vs-logical-relevance]] for the root treatment.
- *must-understand-everything-first*: the inert-jargon demonstration — an item solved fully while treating every technical term as "blah": the student watches all needed information turn out to be on the checklist. Pair with the deflation message: busy display ≠ hard item.
- *any-gap-counts*: contrast pair — two true gaps, one modest, one dramatic, against a "far greater" claim; have the student state the numeric gap aloud for each. Install "magnitude words are conditions, not decoration."
- *entity-count-blindness*: subject-underlining drill — circle the claim's grammatical subject and stamp choices ONE-ENTITY or TWO-ENTITY before any number work.
- *residual-second-guessing*: calibration rule taught as permission — one full-checklist survivor is proof, and verification may confirm but never reopen. Show their own review-change statistics if available; the data usually ends the habit.
- If checklist decomposition itself fails, revisit [[argument-spine-decomposition]]; if extraction fails, [[chart-decoding-fundamentals]].

## Mastery criteria
The student writes (or states) the condition checklist before touching the display, visits the graphic only for checklist features, and audits magnitude/entity/window explicitly. Proof: consistent accuracy on hard items containing multiple graph-accurate distractors, plus correct weaken-by-counterexample picks, with no correct-to-incorrect answer changes on review.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: single-series display; claim with one condition; distractors inaccurate or blatantly off-claim; direct value reads.
- **Medium**: multi-series displays with irrelevant series; magnitude conditions appear; first graph-accurate-but-irrelevant distractors; completion hybrids with illustration transitions.
- **Hard**: 2–3 stacked conditions (entity + magnitude + window); two-entity and small-true-gap distractors together; uniformity distractors on variance claims ("each"/"all" as tells); weaken items requiring the ignored-counterexample hunt; busy displays where slice-discipline is the whole game — composes with [[support-and-weaken-textual]] logic and [[entailment-moves-and-boundaries]] scope limits. Stronger module-1 performance routes 1–2 of the hardest of these into module 2 (unverified-claim — adaptive-composition specifics need verification).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: first verify [[chart-decoding-fundamentals]] — strategy teaching is wasted on extraction failures. Then teach ONLY the order inversion (claim → checklist → display) with one-condition claims; the deflation message ("busy is not hard") matters as much as the method.
- **Average**: the checklist is the unlock. Drill claim → 2–3 written conditions as its own exercise, then full items where they must name which condition each rejected choice fails. Introduce the choice-shape triage as a time tool.
- **Advanced**: magnitude and entity-count auditing — the conditions this band still skips; add weaken-by-counterexample items and two-claim passages. Teach the triage heuristic's failure mode: verbose choices that still need the display to split two survivors.
- **1500+**: full trap taxonomy on review (small-true-gap, two-entity, same-value, uniformity, wrong-window, off-text plausibility) and second-guessing hygiene. At this band the differentiator is committing on one clean survivor in a busy display without a confidence wobble.
