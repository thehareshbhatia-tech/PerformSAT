# Cross-text relationship taxonomy — the full menu of ways two texts can relate

## What it is
A working vocabulary for the relationship between paired texts that goes beyond "they agree" / "they disagree." Naming the relationship precisely — including *where* the overlap ends and what the hinge is — is the step that pre-answers the question. The taxonomy:

1. **Full opposition** — the claims are incompatible; accepting one means rejecting the other.
2. **Partial concession** — agreement on part of the ground, divergence on the rest ("grants the benefit, warns about the excess"). The correct answer may live in the agreement zone, the dispute zone, or straddle both.
3. **Scope adjustment** — T2 doesn't reject T1's claim; it narrows it ("true, but only for coastal species") or broadens it ("this holds far beyond the studied case").
4. **Methodological critique** — T2 accepts the question but disputes how T1's evidence was gathered or measured (sample, instrument, proxy, missing variable).
5. **Reinterpretation of the same evidence** — both texts accept the same data; T2 offers a different explanation or mechanism for it (dueling-explanations pairs).
6. **Complementary reinforcement** — T2 supports T1 from a different angle: new evidence for the same claim, or the mechanism behind T1's observed effect.
7. **Application / extension** — T2 takes T1's framework or finding and applies it to a new case, sometimes revealing strain in the original.
8. **Problem–solution / question–answer** — T1 raises a difficulty or open question; T2 addresses it.

Relationships 3–8 are where students trained only on agree/disagree fail: same-side and orthogonal pairs exist on the digital SAT alongside adversarial ones.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The dominant stem — some form of "how would Author 2 respond to X in Text 1" (unverified-claim on dominance) — is answerable in advance *only if* the relationship has been named at the right resolution. "They disagree" predicts a negative response but not its content; "T2 issues a methodological critique of T1's proxy measure" predicts the response almost verbatim. Unverified-claim: disagreement is the modal relationship on the exam — but partial agreement occurs, and treating disagreement as the only possibility is a trained failure mode; verify the distribution against our bank before the tutor asserts any prior.

## Mental model
**Name the hinge, not just the direction.** A relationship label has two parts: the *type* (from the menu above) and the *hinge* — the specific sub-question the texts actually split or meet on. "T1 and T2 disagree" is a direction without a hinge; "T1 attributes the decline to predation, T2 to a food-supply measure T1 never took" is type 5 with its hinge, and it already contains the answer to any response question. If you cannot state the hinge in one phrase, you have not yet found the relationship — you've found the topic.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Take the two ledger slots from [[two-voice-separation]].
2. Map the agreement zone vs. the dispute zone: what would both authors sign? Where exactly does the overlap end? Mark the boundary, not just "they differ."
3. Classify against the menu: incompatible claims? shared claim with a caveat? same data, different story? same conclusion, different route? Note that several types can nest (a partial concession *containing* a methodological critique) — name the load-bearing one.
4. State the hinge in one phrase.
5. Sanity-check with tone ([[tone-attitude-reading]]): the named type predicts the response tone — opposition predicts critical, concession predicts yes-but phrasing, reinforcement predicts approving. A mismatch between named type and felt tone means one of them is wrong; recheck before proceeding.
6. Hand the type + hinge to [[cross-text-response-prediction]].

## Misconceptions
- **disagreement-means-total-opposition** — Students assume disagreeing authors reject everything the other says, then eliminate concession-shaped correct answers ("accepts X but cautions Y") for not being negative enough. Formed by debate-style framing of disagreement as team-vs-team. The test plants purely hostile distractors beside the correct grants-then-cautions choice for exactly this reader.
- **agree-disagree-binary** — The student's relationship vocabulary has two values, so scope adjustments, reinterpretations, and complementary pairs get forced into one of them. Formed because agree/disagree is the only vocabulary school discussion ever demanded. Produces category errors on same-side pairs — often reading complementary texts as opposed merely because they emphasize different things.
- **topic-level-relationship** — Naming the relationship at the topic's altitude ("both discuss coral bleaching") instead of the claims'. Produces an empty relationship that predicts nothing; the student then reasons from the choices instead ([[cross-text-response-prediction]]'s choices-as-starting-point failure begins here).
- **hinge-skipping** — Naming a correct type but no hinge ("they partially agree") and stopping. The prediction step then underdetermines the answer, and half-right-nuance distractors — right type, wrong hinge — survive elimination.
- **relationship-from-tone-alone** — Inferring "disagreement" purely because T2 sounds critical, when the criticism targets a method or scope, not the claim. Produces full-opposition calls on type-3/4 pairs and subsequent wrong-response predictions.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **disagreement-means-total-opposition**: picks the most hostile response choice; eliminates grants-then-cautions answers, saying they're "too positive"; relationship statement uses absolute language the texts don't.
- **agree-disagree-binary**: relationship statements are always one of two words; systematic errors concentrated on same-side and reinterpretation pairs while adversarial pairs are fine — a clean split in the error log.
- **topic-level-relationship**: relationship statement names the topic, no claims ("both are about..."); solve path shows choice-by-choice rereading.
- **hinge-skipping**: correct type in their statement but picks half-right-nuance distractors; cannot answer "on what specific question do they split?" without rereading.
- **relationship-from-tone-alone**: calls opposition on pairs where T2 accepts T1's conclusion but attacks its method; explanation cites tone words rather than claims.

## Remediation pathways
- **disagreement-means-total-opposition**: contrast pair — full-opposition pair vs. partial-concession pair on similar topics; show the correct response choice differing in shape (hostile vs. yes-but). Teach "agreement zone mapping": list what BOTH authors would sign before naming the split. Prereq if stuck: [[cross-text-relationship-taxonomy]].
- **agree-disagree-binary**: teach the eight-type menu directly with one miniature pair per type; then classification drills (menu lookup, no question attached). Vocabulary precedes discrimination.
- **topic-level-relationship**: enforce the claim-form test — each slot must be disputable ("A1 thinks ___ is true"). Topics aren't disputable; claims are. Drop back to [[two-voice-separation]] if slots won't come out as claims.
- **hinge-skipping**: require the one-phrase hinge as a written step; drill "state the sub-question they split on" as its own exercise with feedback.
- **relationship-from-tone-alone**: separate the two readings explicitly: WHAT does T2 dispute (claim? method? scope?) vs. HOW does it sound. Show one methodological-critique pair where the tone is sharp but the conclusion is shared.

## Mastery criteria
Given any paired set, the student names the relationship type from the full menu (including same-side and orthogonal types), states the hinge in one phrase, and marks where agreement ends — before reading choices — with classification accuracy holding on reinterpretation, scope-adjustment, and complementary pairs, not just oppositional ones.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Medium (entry)**: overt full opposition or clean partial concession; hinge stated nearly verbatim in both texts.
- **Hard**: reinterpretation-of-same-evidence pairs where both texts affirm identical data; methodological critiques whose sharp tone masks a shared conclusion; scope adjustments hinging on one quantifier; nested types (concession wrapping a critique); complementary pairs that novices read as opposed. Composes with [[argument-spine-decomposition]] (what exactly is disputed — claim or support?) and feeds every hard case of [[cross-text-response-prediction]].

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: two types only — full opposition and partial concession — with everyday scenarios (two reviews of one restaurant: one pans it; one likes the food but not the service). Add types gradually.
- **Average**: full menu with miniature pairs; agreement-zone mapping as a drawn diagram (overlapping regions); hinge-statement drills.
- **Advanced**: nested and disguised types; tone-vs-target separation; timed classification (type + hinge in ~40 seconds).
- **1500+**: humanities and aesthetic pairs, where positions are stances rather than empirical claims and the menu must be applied by analogy; have the student construct a pair instantiating an assigned type — authoring a reinterpretation pair proves they see the claim/evidence joint.
