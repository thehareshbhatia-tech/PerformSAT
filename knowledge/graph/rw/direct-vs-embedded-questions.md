# Direct vs embedded questions — does the sentence ask, or assert?

## What it is
The rule deciding whether a sentence ends with a question mark or a period
when question-flavored content is present. A **direct question** stands alone
as an asking sentence and ends with "?". An **embedded question** is a
questioning clause folded inside a statement — typically introduced by
*whether* or *if* — and the sentence ends with a period, because the sentence
as a whole asserts; it does not ask. The end mark punctuates the SENTENCE's
function, never the topic's uncertainty.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
This is low-frequency territory: a holdover from the paper exam that had not
been confirmed on released digital forms as of our source material
(unverified-claim — treat as wild-card content for high scorers pending
verification against official digital items and our bank). When it appears,
it is staged as a standard conventions item ("conforms to the conventions of
Standard English"), and it probes the same deep idea as
[[sentence-vs-fragment]]: punctuation encodes the STRUCTURE and FUNCTION of
the unit, not its semantic flavor. A student who punctuates by topic —
uncertainty mentioned, so "?" — is exactly who the distractors are built for.

## Mental model
**A 2×2 grid with only two livable cells.** Two independent signals must
agree:

- **Word order.** Inverted order (verb before subject: *is the sample*, *did
  the trial*) signals a true question. Statement order (subject before verb:
  *the sample is*, *the trial did*) signals an embedded clause.
- **End mark.** "?" for asking sentences; "." for asserting sentences.

The four answer choices typically populate the full grid
{inverted, statement order} × {?, .} — and only the coherent diagonal cells
can ever be right:
- inverted order + "?" → a direct question, correctly asked;
- statement order + "." → an embedded question, correctly asserted;
- the two mixed cells (statement order + "?", inverted order + ".") are
  **internally inconsistent — auto-kill without consulting the passage.**

After *whether* or *if*, only one cell survives at all: statement order and a
period. The grid means half the choice set dies on internal evidence alone,
before any reading — the same one-right-answer leverage as
[[choice-set-diagnosis]].

The confirming tool is the **detachability test**: lift the questioning part
out of the sentence. If it stands alone as a sensible standalone question, it
is direct → "?". If it only makes sense welded into the host sentence, it is
embedded → period.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Recognize the item from the choice set: same words, toggled word order
   and/or end mark → this rule, not a boundary count.
2. Kill the mixed grid cells on sight (order and mark disagree → internally
   inconsistent).
3. Scan for *whether/if*: present → statement order + period is forced.
4. Otherwise run detachability on the questioning span: stands alone as a
   question → inverted order + "?"; welded in → statement order + period.
5. Re-read the survivor installed in the passage; the sentence should
   plainly either ask or assert, never both.

## Misconceptions
- **question-content-question-mark** — punctuating by topic: the sentence
  MENTIONS uncertainty, wondering, or investigation, so it "must" take a
  question mark. Formed because school punctuation is taught by feel and
  content, and most question-flavored sentences kids meet really are direct
  questions. Produces "?" picks on *whether/if* sentences. The test writes
  stems about researchers wondering and studies investigating precisely to
  soak the sentence in question flavor while its function stays assertive.
- **order-mark-mismatch-blindness** — evaluating word order and end mark as
  two separate, independently plausible decisions instead of one consistency
  check. Formed because each half of a mixed choice looks fine in isolation
  and nothing in school ever framed the two as needing to agree. Produces
  picks of internally inconsistent cells — and long deliberation on choices
  that were free kills.

## Diagnostic indicators
- **question-content-question-mark:** picks "?" whenever the passage
  discusses an open question; explanation cites the topic ("they don't know
  yet, so it's a question") rather than the sentence's function; misses
  concentrate on *whether/if* items.
- **order-mark-mismatch-blindness:** dwell time spread across all four
  choices; when asked why a mixed cell is wrong, appeals to sound rather than
  to the order-mark contradiction; occasionally picks a mixed cell.

## Remediation pathways
- **question-content-question-mark:** the detachability confrontation — one
  sentence pair with identical topic, one direct ("The team asked: does the
  gene transfer?") and one embedded ("The team asked whether the gene
  transfers."). Lift the questioning part from each; only one stands alone.
  Then install the rule: the mark punctuates what the SENTENCE does. If the
  function judgment itself is shaky, revisit [[sentence-vs-fragment]] — the
  same structural-not-semantic discipline.
- **order-mark-mismatch-blindness:** teach the grid explicitly and have the
  student stamp each choice's cell before any other work; the two mixed
  stamps are automatic eliminations. One session of stamping usually makes it
  permanent.

## Mastery criteria
Shown a four-choice grid item, the student eliminates both mixed cells within
seconds and decides the survivor by *whether/if* or detachability — stated in
function language ("the sentence asserts, so period"), never topic language.
Consistency across the rare appearances matters more than volume here; treat
two clean unprompted solves as strong evidence.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Medium (the entry level — this family doesn't come easier):** clean
  *whether/if* marker present; grid fully populated; the kill chain is
  mechanical.
- **Hard:** no *whether/if* — the embedding is by statement order alone
  (*The report examines how the policy failed.*); question flavor is heavy
  (research framing, multiple wonder-verbs); or the questioning span is long
  enough that detachability takes real parsing.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling:** skip until the core boundary skills are solid — this is
  low-frequency content and their points live elsewhere
  ([[section-economics-and-triage]]).
- **Average:** teach only the *whether/if* → period rule plus the topic-trap
  warning; that covers the common case at trivial cost.
- **Advanced:** full grid + detachability; frame it as a free-kill pattern —
  half the choices die on internal consistency, which is faster than reading.
- **1500+:** the no-marker embedded forms and maximal question-flavored
  dressing. Also name the meta: this is wild-card territory, so knowing it is
  cheap insurance, not a pillar — spend accordingly.
