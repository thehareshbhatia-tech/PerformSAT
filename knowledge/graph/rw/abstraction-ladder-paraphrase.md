# The abstraction ladder: shuttling between concrete wording and general restatement

## What it is
The skill of translating a passage's specific wording into a more general restatement of the same idea — and back down again. Passages specify (a named apparatus, a particular painter, one measured result); credited answers generalize ("a novel technique," "an artist's practice," "an unexpected finding"). Moving fluently up and down this ladder, while keeping the meaning EXACTLY fixed, is the bottleneck skill underneath detail, inference, main-idea, and evidence questions alike.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Recognizing that two differently-worded statements express one idea is close to the definition of comprehension, and it's precisely what school string-matching habits fail to build. The test operationalizes it with a consistent directionality: **answers generalize, passages specify** (unverified-claim as a frequency assertion — direction holds in the strong majority of credited answers; validate the ratio against official items and our bank before the tutor states it as a rule). The trap architecture depends on it too: distractors match at the WORD level while diverging in meaning; credited answers diverge at the word level while matching in meaning.

## Mental model
**A ladder with meaning as the invariant.** Every idea can be phrased at several rungs: "the spectrometer they built detected fainter signals" → "their new instrument improved measurements" → "a methodological advance." Climbing rungs strips specifics but must never add, drop, or bend the claim. Two failure directions: climbing too high (overgeneralizing — one painter becomes "artists," one study becomes "the field") and climbing crooked (a near-synonym that shifts meaning — "distinct" is not "unpredictable"; "detached" is not "anxious"). The correct answer is usually ONE rung up from the passage: more general, same claim, same scope, same intensity.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Pin the passage's claim in concrete terms: who/what, did what, to what, with what qualifier.
2. Climb one rung: restate it generically in your own plain words (this becomes your prediction — see [[predict-then-verify]]).
3. Match choices against the RELATIONSHIP in your restatement, not against shared topic nouns.
4. Scope-check the climb: does the choice's subject stay at the passage's number and breadth (this researcher, not researchers; this practice, not the category)?
5. Test substitutability of any pivotal synonym: would the passage still say the same thing if the choice's word replaced the original?
6. For evidence questions, run the ladder downward too: given a general claim, ask what specific data WOULD count as an instance of it.

## Misconceptions
- **word-match-equals-meaning-match** — Treating shared vocabulary as evidence of shared meaning. Formed by keyword-scanning study habits and search-engine intuitions. Produces verbatim-echo and contradiction-in-costume picks; the student never audits the relationship between the matched words.
- **higher-is-truer** — Believing the most general choice is safest ("it covers more, so it's harder to be wrong"). Formed from multiple-choice folk wisdom. Produces scope-inflation picks: pluralized singulars, one case promoted to a class, superlatives the text never makes. Generality above the supported rung is UNSUPPORTED, not safe.
- **near-synonym-blindness** — Vague vocabulary knowledge causes crooked climbs: the student accepts a neighbor-word ("skeptical" for "cautious," "reject" for "revise") as equivalent. Formed because casual usage tolerates loose synonymy. Produces confident wrong picks that survive a gist check but fail substitution.
- **abstraction-panic** — Freezing when a choice is phrased in academic register ("posits a heretofore unexamined mechanism"). Formed by thin exposure to academic prose; the student can't map the abstract phrasing DOWN to the concrete passage event, so they eliminate it (pairs with confusion-means-wrong in [[supported-not-stated]]).

## Diagnostic indicators
- **word-match-equals-meaning-match**: chosen distractors share ≥2 content words with the passage while the credited answer shares ~0; student's explanation counts word overlaps.
- **higher-is-truer**: consistent scope-inflation picks (plural/class/superlative species); says "this one covers everything the passage said."
- **near-synonym-blindness**: misses cluster on items whose credited answer hinges on one precise word; fails substitution tests when asked directly; also predicts [[evidence-forced-word-meaning]] weakness.
- **abstraction-panic**: eliminates the most abstractly-worded choice disproportionately often; long dwell then avoidance; strong performance on concrete-answer items with same skill.

## Remediation pathways
- word-match-equals-meaning-match: bare phrase-pair drill — no passages, no choices: just "do these two phrasings say the same thing? yes/no/why." Train the equivalence judgment in isolation, then reintroduce context.
- higher-is-truer: rung-labeling exercise — given one passage sentence and four restatements at different rungs, pick the highest rung still fully supported; makes "one rung up" tangible.
- near-synonym-blindness: substitution ritual (would the author accept this word?) plus a running personal log of near-synonym pairs that differ decisively; upstream fix is vocabulary depth ([[second-sense-vocabulary]], [[evidence-forced-word-meaning]]).
- abstraction-panic: translation drills DOWN the ladder — give academic-register statements, have the student say them in plain words; exposure diet of adult nonfiction for the long horizon.

## Mastery criteria
Student can (a) generate a one-rung-up restatement of any passage sentence, (b) sort four restatements by rung and mark where support ends, and (c) reject near-synonyms via substitution with a stated reason. Proof: accuracy on hard items whose credited answers are maximally abstract, plus clean performance on scope-trap distractors.

## Difficulty ladder
- **Easy**: one-word synonym swap at the same rung ("large" → "substantial").
- **Medium**: one-rung generalization of a full clause; one scope-inflated distractor.
- **Hard**: two-rung climbs in dense academic diction; credited answer compresses a multi-sentence relationship (composes with [[reference-compression-tracking]]); distractors include a crooked near-synonym AND a one-rung-too-high inflation, forcing fine discrimination on both axes at once.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: start below the test — bare phrase pairs, concrete and short, yes/no equivalence. The ladder metaphor with three physical rungs drawn out. No timed work.
- **Average**: rung-labeling and substitution drills inside real items; teach the "one rung up" expectation as the default shape of right answers.
- **Advanced**: crooked-climb discrimination — curated near-synonym contrast sets; downward-ladder work for evidence items (general claim → which datum instantiates it).
- **1500+**: hardest composition cases — abstraction plus referent compression plus qualifier tracking in one item; and calibration on when a credited answer is LESS general than expected (the direction is a strong prior, not a law).
