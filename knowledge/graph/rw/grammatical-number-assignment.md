# What counts as singular: assigning grammatical number to tricky subjects

## What it is
Once you've found a subject (or an antecedent), you still have to decide whether
it is grammatically singular or plural — and for a family of subjects, meaning
and grammar disagree. Gerund phrases, collective nouns, clauses used as subjects,
and indefinite pronouns all have a fixed grammatical number that can clash with
how plural they FEEL. This node is the number-assignment half of agreement;
[[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]] is the subject-location half.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
These subjects let the test build items where the ear fails even after the student
finds the right subject: "a committee of experts" feels like many people;
"collecting samples from rivers" contains a plural noun. The College Board is
probing whether number is read off STRUCTURE (what kind of unit is this?) rather
than semantics (how many things are involved?). Indefinite-pronoun heads with
plural of-phrases ("each of the studies") are the workhorse version and recur
across difficulty bands.

## Mental model
**One bounded thing → singular.** Don't memorize lists; ask whether the subject
names a single bounded unit:
- A gerund phrase names one activity — singular, no matter what plural nouns sit
  inside it.
- A collective noun names one body (a committee, a team) — singular in the tested
  standard, regardless of everyday or British usage.
- A that/what/whether clause names one fact or question — singular.
- "Each/every/one/either/neither(+of)" point at individuals one at a time — 
  singular, even with "of the [plural]" attached; the of-phrase is scenery.
- Genuinely plural markers: few, both, several, many, others, "a number of."
  ("THE number of" is one quantity — singular; "A number of" means "several" — 
  plural.)
- Context-dependent set (some, more, most, all, none in its common use): they take
  the number of the noun in their of-phrase — "of the water" singular, "of the
  studies" plural.
- Correlative subjects (either…or / neither…nor): the noun NEARER the verb sets
  the number. A lone "neither" means "neither one" — singular.

## Expert reasoning process
1. Identify the head of the subject phrase: is it a gerund, a quantifier, a
  collective, a clause, or an ordinary noun? The head decides, not the loudest
  noun in the phrase.
2. Apply the bounded-unit question: does this head name one thing/activity/body/
  fact? If yes, singular — override the ear.
3. For quantifier heads, classify: always-singular (each/every/one/either/neither/
  much), always-plural (few/both/several/many), or of-phrase-dependent
  (some/most/all/more). Only in the third class does the of-phrase matter.
4. For or/nor compounds, put your finger on the noun nearest the verb.
5. Check the number decision propagates consistently: verb, any pronoun referring
   back ([[pronoun-antecedent-agreement]]), and any role noun after a linking verb
   ("both founders became advisORS") must all carry it.

## Misconceptions
- **feels-plural-is-plural** — number is assigned semantically: a group of people
  acting is "they," an activity involving many samples is "many." Forms because
  spoken English (and British English exposure via media) licenses notional
  agreement. Produces plural verbs for collectives and gerund subjects. The test
  parks a plural noun inside the subject phrase right before the verb to feed it.
- **of-phrase-sets-the-number** — the student lets "of the [plural]" pluralize
  every quantifier head, including each/every/neither. Forms by overgeneralizing
  from the context-dependent set (some/most/all), where the of-phrase genuinely
  decides. Produces plural verbs/pronouns after "each of the…" — the single most
  recycled trap in this family.
- **head-noun-blindness-in-gerunds** — the student never identifies the -ing word
  as the subject at all, so the phrase's internal plural noun wins by default.
  Forms because school parsing rarely covers verbals as subjects.
- **accompaniment-pluralizes** — "along with / as well as / together with" phrases
  are treated like "and," pluralizing a singular subject. Forms because the
  meaning IS additive; but grammatically these are trailing phrases, not
  coordination — only "and" builds a compound subject.
- **number-the-number** — "the number of X" and "a number of X" are treated as
  interchangeable. Forms because the surface difference is one article; students
  have no reason to suspect it flips number until taught.

## Diagnostic indicators
- Plural verb chosen for a collective or gerund subject, with the student's
  explanation referencing the people/things involved → **feels-plural-is-plural**.
- Systematic misses specifically on "each/neither/every … of the [plural]" items
  while ordinary agreement is fine → **of-phrase-sets-the-number**. This also
  shows up in pronoun items (their/its after "each") — check both banks.
- Asked "what's the subject?", the student names a noun inside the gerund phrase →
  **head-noun-blindness-in-gerunds**; remediate at verbal identification, not at
  agreement.
- Plural verb after "X, along with Y," → **accompaniment-pluralizes**.
- Wrong picks split evenly between "the number"/"a number" frames →
  **number-the-number**.

## Remediation pathways
- **feels-plural-is-plural**: teach the bounded-unit question with a contrast
  pair — "the committee meets" vs "the members meet" — showing that English gives
  you a plural word when you want plural meaning; the collective is a deliberate
  choice of ONE body. If it doesn't land, this is a concept gap, not a drill gap:
  re-derive number as a property of the head word.
- **of-phrase-sets-the-number**: three-bucket sort drill (always-singular /
  always-plural / of-phrase-decides). The insight to force: only ONE bucket reads
  the of-phrase.
- **head-noun-blindness-in-gerunds**: subject-naming drills on verbal subjects
  before any multiple choice; revisit gerund-vs-participle in the foundations
  cluster if the student can't tell the -ing word's job.
- **accompaniment-pluralizes**: strike-the-phrase reflex (same move as prep-phrase
  stripping in [[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]]); contrast "X and Y were" vs
  "X, along with Y, was."
- **number-the-number**: minimal-pair memory hook — "THE number is one number;
  A number are several."

## Mastery criteria
Given any subject phrase, states its grammatical number AND the reason class
("gerund → one activity"; "each → always singular"; "most of + plural → plural")
without plugging into a sentence. Proof: consistent accuracy on hard-band items
mixing two number decisions in one choice set (e.g., verb number + pronoun number)
and on nor-compounds where the nearer noun contradicts the farther one.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: rarely tested pure — an ordinary plural subject with a distractor
  singular verb.
- **medium**: each/every/neither + of-phrase; collectives; "a number of."
- **hard**: gerund subjects stuffed with plural nouns; nor-compounds with mixed
  numbers; number decisions embedded in items that ALSO test apostrophe form or
  pronoun choice, composing [[apostrophe-possession-grid]] and
  [[pronoun-antecedent-agreement]] — number must be settled first because it
  eliminates half the choices cheaply.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: skip the taxonomy at first; teach only "find the head word, ask
  if it names one thing." Use the committee/members contrast repeatedly. Add the
  quantifier buckets only after head-finding is reliable.
- **Average**: teach the three quantifier buckets and the bounded-unit principle
  as the WHY behind them; drill each/of-phrase items until the of-phrase stops
  registering as evidence.
- **Advanced**: nor-compounds, "the/a number," and propagation drills (one number
  decision, three places it must show up in the sentence).
- **1500+**: composition items only — number × apostrophe, number × role-noun
  agreement across a parallel pair ("both X became Ys"), plus speed: the number
  decision should take under five seconds so the rest of the budget goes to the
  second axis.
