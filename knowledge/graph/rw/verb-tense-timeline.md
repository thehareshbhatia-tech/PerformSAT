# Verb tense: reading the timeline out of the surrounding text

## What it is
Tense items ask which time-form of a verb fits the passage's timeline. The
defining property: several of the choices are usually grammatical in isolation —
the target sentence alone cannot decide the item. The evidence lives in the
NEIGHBORING sentences' verbs and in lexical time signals, so tense is the
conventions skill that most requires reading beyond the blank.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
The College Board is probing whether a reader builds a coherent event timeline
from prose — ordering discoveries, publications, predictions — rather than
conjugating in a vacuum. Tense items appear at all bands; difficulty comes from
how far away the deciding evidence sits and whether the passage mixes timeframes
(a habitual-present frame inside past narration, a prediction inside a historical
account).

## Mental model
**Match the neighbors unless the timeline objects.** The default answer is the
tense the surrounding verbs already use (consistency). You deviate ONLY when an
explicit timeline cue demands a marked form:
- *since / for / over the past…* → present perfect (started then, still true).
- explicit dates, named past periods → simple past.
- *by the time…* → past perfect (or future perfect if the frame is future).
- two completed past events → only the EARLIER one takes had + participle; also
  derivable by pure logic about which event must precede.
- a past viewpoint looking at its own future ("in 1990, researchers expected the
  method ___ widespread") → *would*.
- *will* = real future/prediction from now; *would* = hypotheticals, habitual
  past, future-in-the-past.
- if-clauses never contain "would have": the if-side takes had + participle,
  the result side carries would have.

(would have / will have / had been) are rarely the credited answer

## Expert reasoning process
1. Triage from the choices ([[choice-set-diagnosis]]): all choices agree in number
   and differ in time → tense item. (Any number split → agreement; tense variation
   is then noise.)
2. Collect evidence: the tenses of the verbs in the sentences before and after the
   blank, plus any date, duration, or "by the time" signal.
3. Default to the neighbors' tense; scan for a cue that licenses a deviation.
4. If two past events interact, order them: earlier → past perfect, later →
   simple past. Don't award past perfect without a second, later past event.
5. Signal-free items: draw the timeline explicitly (what happened first, what is
   still true now, what is predicted) and place the blank's event on it — this is
   the fallback when no tip-off word exists.
6. In compound predicates, check each verb independently: a shared auxiliary
   cannot serve two different time references; every verb must be fully conjugated
   for its own slot.

## Misconceptions
- **blank-local-tense** — the student judges the blank's sentence alone, where
  several choices sound fine, and picks by feel. Forms because most school grammar
  exercises are single-sentence. Produces near-random picks among grammatical
  choices; the test relies on this by making the deciding verb live one sentence
  away.
- **complex-tense-attraction** — on hard questions the student assumes the
  elaborate form (had been / would have) is more likely right. Forms from the
  schoolish belief that harder question = fancier answer. The exam skews the
  opposite way: marked tenses need explicit licensing evidence.
- **would-have-symmetry** — "if X would have…, Y would have…" feels balanced and
  correct. Forms because the symmetric version is common in speech; the student
  doesn't know the if-clause takes plain past perfect.
- **average-the-passage** — in mixed-timeframe passages the student picks the
  tense most common overall rather than the one governing the blank's own frame
  (e.g., a habitual "every year" clause inside past narration). Forms because
  "match the passage" was learned as a global rule, not a local-frame rule.
- **participle-swap** — irregular verbs: using the simple past where a participle
  belongs after have/had, or a bare participle as a main verb. Forms from
  vowel-shift families being stored as one fuzzy form; produces fragment-flavored
  choices (a bare -ing or participle form is a standard "creates a fragment" decoy
  in mixed choice sets — see [[sentence-vs-fragment]]).

## Diagnostic indicators
- Answer changes when you show the student the previous sentence they skipped →
  **blank-local-tense**; also flagged by fast response times on items they miss.
- Systematically picks perfect/conditional forms on hard items, plain forms on
  easy ones → **complex-tense-attraction** (the difficulty of the item, not the
  evidence, is driving choice).
- Misses concentrated on conditional items, always choosing double-"would have" →
  **would-have-symmetry**.
- Correct on uniform-tense passages, wrong exactly when the blank sits in a
  habitual or prediction frame inside a different narrative tense →
  **average-the-passage**.
- Picks bare -ing / bare participle main verbs → **participle-swap**, and check
  whether the real gap is clause-completeness (route to the boundaries cluster).

## Remediation pathways
- **blank-local-tense**: enforced ritual — before answering any tense item, read
  one sentence back and one forward and say the tenses out loud. Contrast pair:
  the same blank with two different preceding sentences flipping the answer.
- **complex-tense-attraction**: show the licensing rule — every marked tense needs
  a visible cue (duration word, second past event, past-viewpoint future). No cue,
  no marked tense. Then reveal the base rate (with the honesty flag that it's a
  tendency, not a law).
- **would-have-symmetry**: minimal pair drill on if/result clause forms; the hook
  is "the if-clause states the alternate past plainly (had), the result clause
  speculates (would have)."
- **average-the-passage**: timeline diagramming — mark each sentence's frame;
  the blank obeys ITS frame, not the majority.
- **participle-swap**: verify against our bank which irregular families actually

## Mastery criteria
On items with no tip-off words, can verbalize the timeline ("first the discovery,
then the publication — blank is the earlier one") before choosing. Consistently
rejects unlicensed marked tenses AND correctly awards them when the cue exists —
both directions, since over-correction (never picking past perfect) is a known
drill artifact. Proof: hard-band accuracy on mixed-frame passages plus correct
handling of one conditional and one since/for item.

## Difficulty ladder
- **easy**: neighbors all one tense, blank matches; distractor tenses clearly
  unanchored.
- **medium**: one deviation cue (since/for; an explicit date against a
  present-tense frame); conditional forms.
- **hard**: mixed-timeframe passages (habitual frame inside past narration;
  prediction frames requiring a future form in an otherwise-past passage),
  two-event ordering without "by the time," and tense checks embedded inside
  parallel series (all verbs in a series must share tense — composes
  [[parallel-structure]]) or fused with agreement noise
  ([[subject-verb-agreement-disguises]]).

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: skip tense names entirely. Teach two moves: "copy the
  neighbors" and "look for since/for/dates." That alone clears the easy and much
  of the medium band.
- **Average**: add the licensing model — marked tenses are guilty until a cue
  proves them — plus the past-perfect two-event rule and the conditional pattern.
- **Advanced**: timeline diagramming for signal-free items; frame-switching
  passages; would as future-in-the-past, which is the least familiar cue and a
  reliable hard-item ingredient.
- **1500+**: drill the composition cases (tense inside parallelism, tense noise on
  agreement items — the skill of NOT solving tense when it isn't the axis), and
  compound predicates where a shared auxiliary is silently serving two times.
