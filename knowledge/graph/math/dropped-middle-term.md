# The dropped middle term — one misconception, many costumes

## What it is
The canonical Advanced Math misconception: believing that an exponent (or a root) distributes over addition the way multiplication does. It surfaces as (a+b)² = a² + b² (the squared binomial with no middle term), as √(a+b) = √a + √b, and as (a+b)ⁿ = aⁿ + bⁿ generally. One false law, three costumes. This node exists as a single concept because diagnosing ANY costume should trigger remediation of the underlying belief, not just the surface error.

## Why it exists / why the SAT tests it
Both the exponent curriculum and the polynomial curriculum independently identify this as the most trap-relevant fact in their territory — the no-middle-term expansion is reliably present in distractor sets for squared-binomial items, and the term-wise-rooted form for radical items (design pattern confirmed across both sources' exercises; exact bank frequency unverified-claim). It's cheap for item writers: the wrong answer looks complete, feels like a finished procedure, and is generated by a habit the student has practiced correctly for years in the one context where it IS legal.

## Mental model
**Exponents copy factors; addition has no factors to copy.** An outer exponent on a product copies itself onto every factor — legal, because (xy)² really is xy·xy = x·x·y·y regrouped. An outer exponent on a SUM has nothing to copy onto: (a+b)² means (a+b)(a+b), and each term of the first factor must meet each term of the second — which is exactly where the middle term 2ab comes from. The middle term isn't decoration; it's the cross-traffic between the two additions. Same story one level down: roots distribute over products, never over sums.

## Expert reasoning process
Experts don't reason about this — they have the reflex inverted. When they see a power or root sitting on a sum:
1. The notation reads as "this whole bundle, multiplied by itself" (or "the bundle whose square is this"), never as an invitation to distribute.
2. For squares: template output first² + 2(first)(second) + second² fires whole ([[binomial-product-templates]]).
3. For roots over sums: recognized as a dead end — either the radicand factors into a product (then the root splits legally) or the expression stays as is.
4. Verification instinct when anything feels off: plug in small numbers. (3+4)² = 49 ≠ 25. √(9+16) = 5 ≠ 7. Ten seconds, definitive.

## Misconceptions
This node IS the misconception; its variants:
- **squared-binomial-no-middle** — (a+b)² = a² + b². Forms because the distributive property of multiplication over addition is drilled for years and the notation looks analogous; the wrong answer also LOOKS complete (two clean terms). Produces the classic no-middle-term distractor on expansion, equivalence, and "which is a factor" items.
- **root-over-sum** — √(a+b) = √a + √b. Same false analogy in radical clothing; reinforced by the fact that the product version √(ab) = √a·√b IS legal, so the student has seen the move succeed nearby.
- **power-over-sum-general** — (a+b)ⁿ term-wise for any n, including fractional n on binomials (a hard-band costume: a binomial under a fractional exponent, expanded term-wise).
- **middle-term-half** — a residual form after partial remediation: the student includes a middle term but writes ab instead of 2ab (they remember "there's a cross term" but not that there are TWO of them). Distractor sets include this halfway form.

## Diagnostic indicators
- Picks the no-middle-term expansion (unsquared-bundle archetype) on expand/equivalence items — fast, confident timing; the error feels like a completed procedure.
- Picks √a + √b-shaped choices on radical items; the same student typically shows BOTH costume errors — probe the other costume after seeing one.
- **middle-term-half**: picks the ab-coefficient distractor; indicates partial remediation, not absence of it.
- Verbal signature: "I distributed the square/root" — the word "distributed" applied to a power is the tell.
- Downstream contamination: errors in completing the square (the (b/2)² compensation makes no sense to them) and in verifying factorizations.

## Remediation pathways
1. **Numeric collision first**: (3+4)² vs 3² + 4²; √(9+16) vs √9 + √16. The false law must FAIL in arithmetic the student trusts before any symbolic explanation. Two counterexamples, both costumes, same session — the point is that it's ONE law failing, not two facts to memorize.
2. **Rebuild from multiplication**: expand (a+b)(a+b) with the 2×2 grid and watch the middle term be born as two cross products. This is why the fix routes through [[binomial-product-templates]].
3. **Contrast with the legal case**: (2·3)² = 36 = 2²·3² succeeds; (2+3)² fails term-wise. Articulate the boundary: exponents distribute over multiplication/division only. If the boundary won't stick, revisit [[exponent-rules-factor-counting]] — the counting model makes it self-evident (a product is a pile of factors to copy; a sum isn't).
4. **middle-term-half** → recount the grid: two cross cells, both ab, hence 2ab.

## Mastery criteria
Zero costume errors across mixed items (squares, roots, general powers) in a session; can generate the numeric counterexample themselves when asked "why not?"; expansion of (a±b)² is automatic with correct middle sign and coefficient. Proof: the misconception's distractors stop being picked on hard disguised items, not just clean ones — disguise is where partial remediation fails.

## Difficulty ladder
The misconception is harvested at every band. Easy: direct expansion items with the no-middle-term distractor. Medium: equivalence items where the bundle is (x − 3) or carries coefficients; radical costume. Hard: decimals/radicals inside the binomial, fractional exponents on binomials, the trap embedded inside multi-step problems (completing the square, distance formula work, expanding inside function composition) where the slip is silent because no distractor flags it.

## Teaching notes
- **Struggling**: numeric counterexamples ONLY until the false law is dead; then the grid expansion, slowly, twice. Do not present the template as a formula yet — it will be memorized on top of the broken foundation.
- **Average**: both costumes in one lesson, explicitly named as one law; grid rebuild; then template compression with the middle-term-half check.
- **Advanced**: hunt the trap in composed settings — have them find where a worked (wrong) solution silently dropped a middle term mid-problem. Builds the audit habit for grid-ins where no distractor catches them.
- **1500+**: they don't hold the misconception, but they should PREDICT it: on any item containing a squared binomial, identify which answer choice was built for the dropped-middle-term student (and which for the middle-term-half student). Reading the distractor architecture confirms their own answer and speeds elimination.
