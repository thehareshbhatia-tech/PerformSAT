Chapter 1.3.01 · Standard English Conventions

Punctuation & Sentence Boundaries

How to end, join, and separate the parts of a sentence — using rules you can prove, not an ear you have to trust.

16 min read~26% of Reading & WritingNo prior grammar assumed

On the SAT, "Boundaries" questions hand you a sentence with a gap and four choices that differ only by one punctuation mark. The good news most students never hear: you don't need a good ear for these. Every one is decided by a short, fixed set of rules — learn them once and you can prove the answer. .01 The clause: complete vs. incomplete

Almost every punctuation rule comes down to one question: is this piece a complete thought, or not? So we start there.

A clause is a group of words with a subject (who or what it's about) and a verb (what they do or are). There are only two kinds, and telling them apart is the whole game.

Independent = a complete thought

An independent clause can stand alone as a sentence. It has a subject and a verb, and it feels finished.

CompleteThe lab ran overnight.

CompleteThe results arrived by morning.

Dependent = not a complete thought

A dependent clause also has a subject and a verb, but it opens with a word — because, although, when, if, since, while, after, before — that leaves it hanging.

Not completeBecause the lab ran overnight…

Subject and verb are there, but because makes you wait: because the lab ran overnight… what? It has to lean on a complete sentence to finish.

.02 The one test you'll always run

✓The stand-alone test

Cover a piece of the sentence and ask: could this stand on its own as a sentence? If yes, it's complete (independent). If it leaves you waiting, it's incomplete (dependent or a fragment).

Why it matters: every rule in Stage 2 is just this test applied to each side of the gap. Master it and the rest is lookup.

Try it

Which can stand alone? (a) The bridge opened in 1932.  (b) When the bridge opened in 1932.  (c) Carrying traffic for decades.

Show answer
Only (a) — subject (bridge), verb (opened), finished thought. (b) adds When, leaving it hanging; (c) has no subject, so it's a fragment.

R1–R6 The rulebook

Six rules cover every Boundaries question. Each one follows the same shape: the rule, a right example, a wrong example, and the why.

1Two complete sentences take a strong join

When both sides are complete, connect them with a period, a semicolon ( ; ), or a comma + a joining word (and, but, or, so, for, nor, yet — the FANBOYS).

RightThe telescope was old; it still worked.

RightThe telescope was old, but it still worked.

Error — the comma splice: a comma alone can't hold two complete sentences (The telescope was old, it still worked). Two complete sides with nothing between them is a run-on. Fix: upgrade to a period, semicolon, or comma + FANBOYS.

Why: two strong ideas need a strong link. A bare comma is too weak to hold them.

2An incomplete opener attaches with a comma

When a dependent clause comes first, put a comma at the seam where it meets the complete sentence.

RightAlthough the data was noisy, the trend was clear.

Flip sideThe trend was clear although the data was noisy. (complete part first → usually no comma)

Why: an incomplete piece is weak, so it attaches with a weak mark — a comma, or nothing.

3A colon needs a complete sentence in front of it

A colon ( : ) is a spotlight — it introduces a list or explanation. What comes before it must be able to stand alone; what comes after doesn't.

RightShe had one goal: finish the map.

WrongHer one goal was to: finish the map.

Why: Her one goal was to isn't a complete sentence, so nothing can follow with a colon. Run the stand-alone test on the left side.

4Set off a mid-sentence aside with a matching pair

To drop extra information into the middle of a sentence, wrap it in a pair of dashes, a pair of commas, or a pair of parentheses — both ends the same mark.

RightThe bridge — built in 1932 — still carries traffic.

WrongThe bridge — built in 1932, still carries traffic.

Why: opened with a dash, so it must close with a dash. A dash-then-comma mix looks balanced but is a favorite trap.

5Extra info gets commas; essential info gets none

If the middle phrase is removable (lift it out and the sentence still points to the right thing), wrap it in commas. If it's essential to the meaning, use no commas.

ExtraMy sister, who lives in Denver, is visiting.

EssentialThe runner who finished first set a record.

Why (the remove-it test): "who lives in Denver" is bonus detail → commas. "who finished first" tells you which runner → no commas.

6Semicolons separate list items only when items contain commas

Inside a list, upgrade the separating commas to semicolons only when the items themselves already contain commas.

RightThe tour visits Lima, Peru; Quito, Ecuador; and Bogotá, Colombia.

Why: the semicolons keep the city-and-country pairs from blurring. No internal commas? Plain commas are correct.

.04 The decision flow

On any Boundaries question, run this. Cover the gap, judge each side with the stand-alone test, and follow the arrows to the rule.

Is the left side a complete sentence?
No — incomplete opener
Comma at the seam  R2
Yes — it's complete
Now: what's the right side?
Also complete → period · semicolon · comma + FANBOYS  R1
A list or explanation → colon  R3
A trailing dependent clause → usually no comma  R2

The stem is always a version of: "Which choice conforms to the conventions of Standard English?" — about 6 per test, pure rules, no reading required.

.05 Traps to watch for

Trap · the comfortable pause. Both sides are complete and the bare-comma choice reads smoothly because you'd pause there aloud. Sounding right isn't the test — two complete sides can't take a comma alone (Rule 1).
Trap · the half-open aside. A mid-sentence aside opens with a dash but closes with a comma, and looks balanced. Find the opening mark; make the closing mark match it exactly (Rule 4).

.06 Worked example & check yourself

Worked example · medium

Although the recipe called for saffron, a spice far too costly for most medieval households ______ cooks often substituted marigold petals to get a similar golden color.

(A) households, cooks  (B) households cooks  (C) households; cooks  (D) households: cooks

  1. Left side. "Although the recipe called for saffron, a spice far too costly for most medieval households." Starts with Although → incomplete opener.
  2. Right side. "cooks often substituted marigold petals…" Subject cooks, verb substituted → complete.
  3. Follow the flow. Left incomplete → comma at the seam R2. Semicolon (C) needs two complete sides R1; colon (D) needs a complete left side R3; (B) is a run-on.
Answer  (A) households, cooks.

Check yourself · 1 of 3  R1

Both sides are complete. Which are valid: a period, a semicolon, a comma alone, or a comma with "and"?

Show answer
Period, semicolon, and comma-with-"and" — all valid. The comma alone is a splice.

Check yourself · 2 of 3  R3

"The museum has one rule ___ no flash photography." Left side stands alone; a specification follows. Which mark?

Show answer
A colon — complete sentence in front, explanation after.

Check yourself · 3 of 3  R4

"My uncle ___ a retired firefighter ___ volunteers at the museum." How do you set off the aside?

Show answer
A matching pair — two commas or two dashes, not one of each.
